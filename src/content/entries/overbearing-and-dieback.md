## What die-back is

Die-back is the death of twigs and branches that starts at the tip and moves downwards.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2007 review discusses it as the descending death of the side (plagiotropic) branches.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Overbearing means that a plant carries more fruit than its leaves can feed.

The two are linked. Unshaded coffee often bears very heavily. A heavy crop limits how many leaves the plant forms and keeps, which leads to branch die-back.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> As trees enter the reproductive phase with heavy fruit loads, they become more prone to die-back, according to a 2007 review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Fungi and bacteria can also kill branch tips. That pathogen die-back is a different problem and is covered further down.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Fruit comes first

A coffee plant makes its sugars in the leaves. Fruit, new shoots and roots all draw on them. The fruit wins: coffee berries act as priority sinks, as the 2007 review puts it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A sink is any part of the plant that uses more sugar than it makes.

In a Costa Rican field trial, berries received four times as much dry matter as branch growth over the season.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The more fruit a branch carried, the less it grew, and the more often it died back.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The plant does not seem to control the pull of its fruit finely, the review notes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Leaves per fruit

What matters is how much leaf feeds each fruit. In a Brazilian field study, branches were grouped by leaf area per fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> With less than 6 cm² of leaf per fruit, about 78% of the branches died back. With more than 14 cm², about 20% did.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The same study saw no die-back the following year, when the crop was small.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 1993 Brazilian study found that trees in full sun can carry a very heavy crop without die-back if they keep enough leaf area.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## One heavy year, one light year

A coffee branch grows new wood and carries fruit in the same year. The two kinds of growth compete.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> After a high crop the trees grow little, so the next crop is smaller.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> This swing between heavy and light crops is called alternate or biennial bearing. Branching and fruiting wood explains why the crop sits on last year’s wood.

Die-back makes the swing stronger. Plants may need two or three years to recover, according to the 2007 review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Cenicafé, Colombia’s national coffee research centre, notes that the swing is stronger in ageing plantations.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Sun, nutrition and water

According to the 2007 review, shade reduces profuse flowering, overbearing and biennial production, and so branch die-back.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Branch die-back does not usually arise under natural conditions where coffee grows under shade.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a Costa Rican trial, 45% shade cut yield by 18% but reduced alternate bearing.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Shade and agroforestry covers the trade-offs.

Fruit also takes most of the nutrients. In late fruit growth, berries may take over 95% of the plant’s current uptake of potassium, phosphorus and nitrogen.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a Colombian trial, the leaves of fully loaded trees fell short of nitrogen and potassium.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Nutrition and fertilisation covers rates and timing.

Stress adds to the load. The 2007 review links die-back to dry soil and air, high temperatures and strong sun.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A coffee textbook chapter states that full-sun coffee without fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation suffers strong biennial production and die-back.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Source–sink physiology

**Allocation.** In the 2005 trial on five-year-old Costa Rica 95 in their third production cycle, fruit load was set at 0, 25, 50 and 100% of the initial load.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Increasing fruit load significantly reduced branch growth and berry dry mass at harvest; ring-barking the branches reduced growth further.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Dry matter allocation to berries was four times that to branch growth. Branch die-back and berry drop rose significantly with fruit load.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The authors read this as competition for carbohydrates between berries and shoots, and among berries.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Fruit photosynthesis.** Green berries photosynthesise a little. At the bean-filling stage and field light levels of 200–500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, their photosynthesis covered about 30% of their daily respiration and 12% of their total carbon needs.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Leaf photosynthesis.** Whether leaves raise their output when the fruit pulls harder is contested. In the 2005 trial, leaf net photosynthesis rose with fruit load. Leaves on loaded, non-isolated branches reached three times the rate of leaves on ring-barked branches without berries.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2008 study cited by the 2007 review found more than 50% higher photosynthesis and stomatal conductance in trees with a full crop and half the leaf area than in defruited trees.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2012 Brazilian study found no compensatory increase as leaf area per fruit fell.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2024 Colombian study found no significant change in leaf gas exchange across fruit loads.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> See Contested.

**Shoot growth.** At Viçosa, Brazil (20°45′S, 650 m), vegetative and reproductive growth of field-grown arabica run at the same time.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Removing the fruit raised branch growth and leaf area gain markedly. The authors attributed much of the restriction in fruiting trees to starch exhaustion, alongside supra-optimal temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Nitrogen supply in amino form did not differ enough to explain the growth difference.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Reserves, roots and nutrients

**Starch.** Early studies cited by the 2007 review linked die-back to strong falls in starch in the root and trunk.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a 2006 study cited by the review, heavily bearing arabica (3,765 kg of dry beans per hectare, as reported) had 30% less starch than defruited trees, with no fall in total soluble sugars.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 1993 Brazilian study found that branches of bearing trees died back regardless of their starch content.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The review concludes that carbohydrate stores are probably not the only factor that sets off die-back.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Roots.** A 2005 study cited by the review followed irrigated, five-year-old conilon at Linhares, Brazil, through a year of 12,000 kg of dry beans per hectare.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Root dry matter fell from 3.30 kg per plant in October (pinhead fruit) to 1.50 kg in January (bean filling) and 1.60 kg in April (early ripening).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It recovered to 2.18 kg in July and 3.50 kg in October.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Root dry matter did not change significantly in earlier years with smaller, regular crops. The author concluded that heavy crops may badly impair root function and so aggravate die-back and decline.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Nutrients.** In the 2024 Colombian trial, three-year-old Cenicafé 1 trees in full sun at the Naranjal station (Chinchiná, Caldas, 1,383 m) were thinned to nine fruit loads from 20 to 100%.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> At 20–40% load, leaf nitrogen averaged 2.14%; at 100% load it was 1.77%, with potassium at 1.02%.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The authors describe deficiencies of nitrogen, potassium, sulphur and copper at high fruit load. They attribute them to the pull of the fruit during endosperm filling.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Leaf total soluble sugars fell linearly as fruit load rose.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> That study did not record die-back.

**Nitrogen trials.** A 1961 Costa Rican study cited by the 2007 review found 44% less die-back with 100 g of nitrogen per plant per year and 66% less with 200 g, compared with no nitrogen.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 1993 Brazilian study found no relationship between leaf nitrogen or potassium and die-back.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the 2012 study, die-back showed only weak, though significant, relationships with leaf nitrogen and starch.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## The 2012 leaf-to-fruit study

The study ran on seven-year-old Catuaí Vermelho IAC 99 at Coimbra, Brazil (720 m), in full sun and without irrigation, planted at 2.0 × 1.0 m.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Branches were sorted into three classes of leaf area per fruit: below 6 cm² (mean 3.5), 6.1–14 cm² (mean 9.4) and above 14 cm² (mean 23.0).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Die-back was counted where a branch had at least 10 cm of dead tissue at its tip.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

In 2007 about 78%, 50% and 20% of branches died back in the three classes, regardless of canopy position.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Branch growth rose with leaf area per fruit in every canopy position.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the low-yield season of 2007–2008 there was no die-back.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors found die-back probably not closely related to carbohydrate, amino acid and mineral concentrations.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Older figures set a higher bar for leaf area. A 1983 study in Zimbabwe, cited by the 2007 review, put the leaf area needed for one fruit at about 2,000, 3,200 and 7,200 mm² at the top, middle and bottom of the crown.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> That is 20, 32 and 72 cm² (our conversion). A 2002 study found no further gain in bean size above 2,000 mm² per fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Even the best-supplied class of the 2012 study averaged 23.0 cm² per fruit and still lost about 20% of its branches.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Varieties and decline

The 2007 review states that Bourbon and Caturra are more prone to die-back than Mundo Novo.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Brazil, Catimor is highly productive but rapidly enters a decline marked by sudden die-back after a few years of high yields.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Plants that recover over two or three years while still carrying an appreciable crop may finally degenerate, according to the review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Die-back caused by pathogens

The descending death of side branches has often been blamed on fungi such as *Colletotrichum* and *Phoma*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A growing body of evidence suggests that these infections follow the weakening of the tissue rather than cause it, according to the 2007 review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Some pathogens do cause die-back as a disease of their own. In Brazil, diseases of the *Colletotrichum* complex include anthracnose of branches, fruit and leaves, and die-back; they are attributed to *C. gloeosporioides*.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The same study notes that coffee berry disease is absent from Brazil and restricted to Africa.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> *Phoma* leaf spot damages coffee by drying of stems, flower necrosis and mummified pinhead fruit. In a 2025 Brazilian study the disease was higher at 15–20 °C and in windier periods; higher plantations are more exposed to cold winds.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Bacterial halo blight (*Pseudomonas syringae* pv. *garcae*) causes lesions on leaves, flowers and pinhead berries and die-back of twigs and branches. In Brazil most strains collected since 1997 came from regions near or above 800 m.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> These diseases show lesions on leaves, flowers or young fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry gives a tested field key for telling them apart from physiological die-back.

## Open questions

- **The trigger.** Starch exhaustion, nutrient depletion and root loss have all been proposed. No study consulted for this entry separates them in one design (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Water and crop load.** The 2007 review links die-back to water deficit and heat.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured die-back across crop loads under controlled irrigation.
- **Canephora.** Most measurements are on arabica. For conilon, the root data come from one study cited in a review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Telling causes apart.** No source consulted gives a tested field key that separates physiological from pathogen die-back.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Chaves et al., 2012 – Leaf-to-fruit ratio and branch die-back.</strong> Sorted 360 branches of seven-year-old Catuaí in full sun in Brazil by leaf area per fruit. Die-back rose steeply as leaf area per fruit fell, from about 20% to about 78% of branches. Photosynthesis, carbohydrates and minerals changed little, so the authors doubt a simple starvation trigger.</p>
<p class="meta">Environmental and Experimental Botany 77, 207–218 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envexpbot.2011.11.011">doi:10.1016/j.envexpbot.2011.11.011</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Vaast et al., 2005 – Fruit load and carbon allocation.</strong> Set fruit loads from none to full on Costa Rica 95 and ring-barked some branches. Berries took four times the dry matter of branch growth; die-back and berry drop rose with load, while leaf photosynthesis also rose.</p>
<p class="meta">Tree Physiology 25(6), 753–760 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/treephys/25.6.753">doi:10.1093/treephys/25.6.753</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Amaral et al., 2001 – Fruiting, carbohydrates and nitrogen.</strong> Compared fruiting and defruited arabica at Viçosa, Brazil. Defruited trees grew much faster; the authors linked the restriction mainly to starch exhaustion and high temperatures, not to nitrogen supply.</p>
<p class="meta">Revista Brasileira de Fisiologia Vegetal 13(1), 66–74 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1590/S0103-31312001000100008">doi:10.1590/S0103-31312001000100008</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Leon-Burgos et al., 2024 – Fruit load and leaf nutrients.</strong> Thinned Cenicafé 1 trees in Colombia to nine fruit loads. Full loads left the leaves short of nitrogen, potassium, sulphur and copper and lowered leaf sugars, with no significant change in gas exchange.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientia Horticulturae 329, 113005 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scienta.2024.113005">doi:10.1016/j.scienta.2024.113005</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>DaMatta et al., 2007 – Ecophysiology of coffee growth and production.</strong> Reviews die-back as a complex, descending branch death tied to heavy crops, full sun and stress. Gathers older field data on starch, roots, nitrogen and varieties, and argues that pathogens are mostly secondary.</p>
<p class="meta">Brazilian Journal of Plant Physiology 19(4), 485–510 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1590/S1677-04202007000400014">doi:10.1590/S1677-04202007000400014</a></p>
</div>
