## What the two words mean

Endothermic means that heat is taken up; exothermic means that heat is released.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a roast, both kinds of process run side by side, and the balance between them shifts as the roast goes on.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

At the start, a large energy input is needed to evaporate water and to set chemical reactions going. A roasting textbook chapter calls this the endothermic phase.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Towards the end, much less heat input is needed, because the process itself becomes progressively more exothermic.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

A second chapter of the same textbook describes the roast in the same way: an endothermic drying phase, then an exothermic phase in which most flavour compounds form, then cooling.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The glossary terms endothermic reaction and exothermic reaction give the short definitions.

## Heat in, heat out

During a roast, heat flows into the bean from hot air and hot surfaces. The heat transfer entry describes those paths. Inside the bean, the heat has three main uses or sources:

- **Warming.** Heat raises the temperature of the solid bean and the water in it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Evaporation.** Turning water into vapour takes up a large amount of heat. One model uses 2.3 million joules per kilogram of water.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In a 2025 model, this evaporative cooling starts at 100 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Reactions.** Some reactions take up heat and others release it. According to the textbook chapter, the beans eventually start to generate heat on their own.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Early on, heating the water dominates, so the roast is primarily endothermic.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Later, the process becomes progressively more exothermic, and much less heat input is needed.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In a 2025 single-bean model, evaporation still had the larger effect on bean temperature than reaction heat.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Where the balance turns

The sources do not agree on when the roast turns exothermic. A roasting textbook chapter ties the switch to first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A study of aroma compounds in the exhaust of a production drum roaster placed it at 625 s, before first crack at about 730 s.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

Model studies give temperatures instead. One assumes reaction heat from 150 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Another says exothermic reactions can begin once the bean nears 200 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A 2013 study links them to roasting temperatures above 230 °C, and a 2009 study places them at about 250 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Laboratory heat measurements disagree too. In one, ground green coffee mainly took up heat up to 245 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In another, six green coffees each switched at a different temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Part of the spread comes from method: the studies measure a whole bean, a model, a ground sample or the exhaust gas.

## What it means at the roaster

**Heat input goes down.** A 2020 study describes the usual practice: start near maximum burner output, then step it down as the roast goes on.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The stated aim is to slow the browning reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A roasting textbook chapter adds that a roaster may adjust the flame at first crack, linking this to the switch to exothermic.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**The roast gains momentum.** According to the textbook chapter, the final stages speed up and gradually approach the conditions of a combustion process.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A few seconds can then separate the intended roast degree from an over-roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**The stop must be abrupt.** For this reason, the chapter calls for an abrupt stop with efficient precooling or cooling.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A doctoral thesis also states that the roast must be stopped by rapid cooling.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The cooling and quenching entry describes how.

**Fire.** According to the same chapter, beans can catch fire if the roast runs on uncontrolled.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2009 model study notes that exothermic reactions near 250 °C lead the beans to burn, but that industrial roasts normally stop well before that point.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Thermodynamics of the roast

**Three terms.** A single-bean energy balance has a heat-transfer term from the surroundings, an endothermic evaporation term proportional to the loss of moisture, and an exothermic reaction term.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In a 2025 model, the reaction term was switched on above 150 °C. Its rate followed an Arrhenius law and stopped once the total heat of reaction was reached.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> That total was 232 kJ per kg dry matter, a value both this study and a 2013 study take from a 2002 model.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The two papers print different values for the activation parameter of that model (5500 K and 500 K).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Evaporation against reaction heat.** In the 2025 model, leaving out evaporation made the model overestimate bean temperature during the exothermic period from 150 °C. Leaving out reaction heat made it underestimate.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Evaporation had the stronger effect, but both terms were needed for the best fit.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Orders of magnitude.** In a fluid-bed study, 10.2–11.4 g water per 100 g green coffee evaporated during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> At 2.3 × 10⁶ J/kg<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>, that is about 235–262 kJ per kg green coffee (our calculation). This is the same order as the 232 kJ per kg dry matter of reaction heat used in the models.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Part of the evaporated water is itself formed by reactions, so the comparison is rough.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**What the reactions are.** According to the textbook chapter, Maillard reaction, Strecker degradation, pyrolysis and caramelisation are among the main reactions of carbohydrates during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The off-gas study links the exothermic phase to Maillard reactions forming coffee aroma compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The 2025 model describes the heat as coming from oxidation reactions inside the grain.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry measured the heat of single roasting reactions inside a bean.

## Measuring heat release

**Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC).** A DSC measures the heat flow into a small sample against an empty reference while both are heated at a set rate. A 2023 study heated 10 mg cross-sections of six green coffees from 20 to 250 °C at 10 °C/min under nitrogen.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> All showed an endothermic phase followed by an exothermic one, at a different temperature for each sample.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The authors conclude that denser, moister coffees needed more heat.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The paper's table and text label the same values differently, so we do not give its peak temperatures here.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Thermal analysis of ground coffee.** A 2005 study heated 2 g of ground green coffee, dried under vacuum, in a silica tube under argon at 20 °C/min.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> It found mainly endothermic reactions between 100 and 245 °C, then exothermic decomposition with two reactions up to 430 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The heat taken up was estimated at 100 kJ/kg; the two exotherms released about 60 and 20 kJ/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Because the sample was dried first, the thermograms showed no sign of the bean's own water.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Atmosphere matters.** In a 2005 thermoanalytical study in synthetic air, green coffee showed a broad endotherm from dehydration, followed by two exotherms.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In freeze-dried samples, a strong exotherm above 360 °C was attributed to heat generated by the sample in an oxidising atmosphere.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A roaster's air is neither pure air nor inert gas, so these results transfer only in part.

**Calorimetry of self-heating.** The textbook chapter bases its statement on heat release on a 1982 calorimetric study of self-heating in coffee and chicory.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> That study could not be accessed for this entry. A doctoral thesis lists earlier calorimetric and textbook values for the switch to exothermic, which disagree with one another.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Fitting bean temperatures.** Model studies compare a heat balance with measured bean temperatures; reaction heat is either fitted as a term or invoked to explain where the model deviates.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In a 2009 study, a lumped model matched published bean data better at 190 °C air than at 290 °C. The authors attribute the deviation to exothermic reactions at about 250 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Off-gas signals.** In a production drum, aroma compounds in the exhaust changed abruptly at 625 s and again at 730 s, with first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The authors read the first change as the transition to the exothermic phase. It was a change in the gas profile, not a heat measurement.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Energy balance on a roaster.** No study consulted for this entry measured reaction heat from an energy balance over a production roaster.

## How models handle reaction heat

Model studies treat reaction heat in three ways:

- **Included as a source term.** The 2025 single-bean model adds reaction heat above 150 °C and fits the data best with both evaporation and reaction heat.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Left out.** A 2013 CFD study of single beans lists the reaction-heat parameters but states that the major chemical transformations were not yet in its model.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 2017 heat-and-mass-transfer study treated its reaction-energy term as small and did not use it in a simplified model.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Lumped into heat capacity.** A 2009 model folds evaporation and other thermal effects into one apparent specific heat that depends on temperature and initial moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Relation to first and second crack

**First crack.** In a fluid-bed study, reaction gases rose significantly only from a bean core temperature of 180–200 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The first crack entry covers the cracking itself and the temperatures reported for it. A textbook chapter ties the switch to exothermic to first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The off-gas study placed the transition about 105 s before first crack (our calculation from 625 s and 730 s).<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> No source consulted measured heat release in a bean at the moment of first crack.

**Rate of rise.** A 2024 study describes the practitioner term “flick”, a sudden increase in the rate of rise after first crack, as typically associated with the onset of exothermic reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In that study, the flick profile was made by changing the roaster's gas and airflow, so it does not separate burner heat from reaction heat.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The thesis found that published temperature curves do not show a late rise clearly attributable to exothermic reactions, and its own laboratory and industrial roasts showed no substantial additional rise either.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Second crack.** No source consulted for this entry links second crack to a measured change in heat release. The strongest heat release in thermal analysis began at 245 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In one drum study, medium roasts ended at 191.1–204.1 °C (measuring point not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Risks

- **Overshoot.** In the late, exothermic stage, a few seconds can turn the intended roast degree into an over-roasted product.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> See roast defects.
- **Burning and fire.** Beans can catch fire in an uncontrolled roast, according to the textbook chapter<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>; a model study places burning at about 250 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Late stop.** The roast must be stopped abruptly and cooled efficiently, or it runs on after the drop.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Copied set points.** Temperatures for the switch come from different methods and bases; none is a probe value for a given roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Onset of net heat release.** Reported positions range from 625 s in one drum roast to about 250 °C in a model (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Size of reaction heat in a whole bean.** The models use one literature value<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>; the thermal analysis consulted measured a dried, ground sample.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Self-heating in roast curves.** Whether the late roast shows a temperature rise from reactions alone is not settled.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Coffee to coffee.** One DSC study found the switch at different temperatures for six coffees<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>; how origin, processing and density shift it is not established.
