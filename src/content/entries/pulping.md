## What pulping removes

Pulping is the mechanical step that opens the wet process. It strips the skin and pulp from the fresh cherry.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The pulp leaves as a by-product; the seeds leave as the main product, still wrapped in parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A layer of mucilage stays on them: in Cenicafé's figures it makes up about 22% of the weight of pulped coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> What happens to that mucilage – fermentation, a machine, or drying it on the seed – decides the process that follows.

Pulping should happen soon after picking. Delay allows uncontrolled fermentation and affects the final flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Cenicafé advises not leaving harvested cherry more than 6 hours unpulped;<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> a Kenyan field report sets a limit of 10 hours from harvest;<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> an FAO manual for Lao PDR asks for same-day processing, without mixing in older cherries.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## How a pulper works

All common pulpers do the same thing: they squeeze and tear.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The cherry is pressed between a moving, roughened surface and a fixed plate. The skin splits, the seeds pop out, and the pulp is dragged away.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

- **Drum pulper** – a rotating cylinder with flutes turns inside a fixed plate that carries pulping channels and ribs.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In the Colombian design the cherry is compressed in the space between the breast plate (*pechero*) and the teeth of the drum sleeve (*camisa*); the pulp is carried off through the space between the teeth and the rib (*vena*) of the breast plate.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> One small drum pulper, built in Benin and tested on Arabica from Rwanda and Robusta from Benin, used a roughened sleeve with punched bulbs 20 mm long, 6 mm wide and 5 mm high.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Disc pulper** – one or more roughened discs turn against fixed surfaces; the pulp is rubbed off by the disc bulbs and chop rails.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The tested prototype used two roughened discs 300 mm across, one fixed and one rotating.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Pulpers that work without water** – Cenicafé found that several commercial horizontal-cylinder pulpers work without added water, fed from a dry hopper, with little difference in capacity.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Its ecological-processing work reports the same for horizontal- and vertical-shaft pulpers.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The gap between the moving surface and the fixed plate is the main setting. A Philippine national standard requires it to be adjustable and to narrow gradually along the path of the cherry.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Classifying the cherries by size before pulping, and setting the pulper to that size, reduces damage to the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Cenicafé's newer design sets the breast-plate gap with steel feeler gauges.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## The pulping line

A pulper rarely works alone. A wet-processing system pictured in an FAO guide shows the order: receiving tank, siphon, pulper, separating screen, repasser (a second pulper), then fermentation tanks.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

- **Before the pulper** – cherries are washed and sorted to remove twigs, leaves and other foreign matter.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Green immature cherries and dried fruits can be removed in a water separator.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In that layout, the siphon sits between the receiving tank and the pulper.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **After the pulper** – a separating screen follows, then a repasser, which is a second pulper.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The Codex code asks each mill to decide what proportion of unpulped cherries and nipped beans it accepts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Green-cherry separation** – where machines harvest the crop, mills can use a classifier-type pulper that separates hard, immature cherries from ripe ones.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Water and pulp

Conventional Colombian mills use 40–50 litres of water per kilogram of dry parchment coffee; about half of it goes on pulping, carrying the pulp to pits in water and carrying the pulped coffee to the fermentation tanks.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> An earlier Cenicafé report gives about 40 litres per kilogram for the whole conventional process.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Pulping without water removes the use at this stage entirely, and the pulp can be moved to covered pits without water.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In the Becolsub module, which combines dry pulping with mechanical demucilaging, the target for the whole process is 0.7–1.0 litres per kilogram.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Pulp is the largest by-product. Cenicafé puts it at about 43.58% of the fresh fruit's weight, or on average 2.25 tonnes of fresh pulp per hectare per year.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> It carries 73.7% of the mill's potential pollution; the mucilage carries the other 26.3%.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Composted, the pulp is transformed in about four months; vermicomposting yields 35–40% (wet basis).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Other uses include animal feed, biogas and cascara drinks.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Mechanics of skin rupture

Pulping works because ripe fruit gives way easily and the seed does not. In a compression model of Arabica fruit, built on fruit properties taken from earlier studies, ripe cherries deformed most (mean 2.20 mm/mm) and needed the least strain energy (0.07 mJ) and stress (0.25 MPa).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Immature fruit needed about five times the strain energy (0.34 mJ) and stress (1.25 MPa).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The authors conclude that the riper the fruit, the more it deforms and the less energy and stress it takes.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

In the machine, the cherry enters the narrowing gap, the skin splits under compression, and the seeds are squeezed out while the teeth drag the torn pulp through the space between the teeth and the breast-plate rib.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The mucilage helps: it lets the seeds slip through the pulper grooves. Seeds without mucilage may be nicked, scratched or chipped by the rough drum.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

The seed itself tolerates only a small squeeze. Cenicafé reports that seeds deformed by more than 1 mm suffer structural damage that can reduce their commercial value.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The gap therefore has to be wide enough for the seeds and narrow enough to split the skin – which is why cherry size matters.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Unripe cherries and pulper performance

Unripe cherries resist compression,<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> so they tend to pass through unpulped or to be damaged. Classifier pulpers use this: they separate hard, immature cherries from ripe ones.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In Kenya, the peak season makes hand sorting before pulping impossible, so all coffee is pulped as it arrives.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Fruit type matters too. In one trial, the same small drum pulper reached about 98% efficiency on Arabica but 85% on Robusta; the disc pulper did better on Robusta (about 88%) than on Arabica (about 76%).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The test used a smaller gap for Robusta (12 mm) than for Arabica (16 mm).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Nipped and broken beans** – poor adjustment of the pulper causes broken or nipped beans, which count as defects and lower quality.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The FAO manual for Lao PDR lists nipped beans from poor or poorly adjusted equipment as a cause of stinker beans.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Physical damage makes coffee more susceptible to contamination and deterioration.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Unpulped cherries and pulp in the parchment** – these carry fruit into the fermentation tanks; Cenicafé counts its pulper as working correctly with under 2% pulp in the grain and under 1% unpulped fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Recycled water** – mills that recycle pulping water must keep it free of outside contaminants; the gradual build-up of heat and enzymes in it speeds up the fermentation that follows.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Delays at peak season** – frequent breakdowns and congestion at pulping stations delay pulping.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Pollution** – the pulp from one kilogram of cherry, with its mucilage, can use up the dissolved oxygen of 7.4 m³ of clean water.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Measurement and maintenance

**Measuring performance.** The Philippine standard defines three figures by weight, in per cent: pulping efficiency (parchment collected at all outlets against cherry fed in), unpulped loss (unpulped cherry against cherry fed in) and mechanically damaged parchment (against the weight of a sample).<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The Codex code suggests mills monitor the quantity of broken, nipped and naked beans and the water used.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Maintenance.** Before each season, processing equipment should be cleaned, reassembled, lubricated and checked.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The Hawaiʻi guide asks for every operating part to be adjusted to avoid bean damage, and for the pulper to be cleaned and overhauled every year.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Cenicafé built its newer drum teeth in reinforced nylon; they can take a radial force 24.8 times that of copper and 5.5 times that of stainless steel.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Nipped beans and stinkers.** The link appears in a practical manual,<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> but no study consulted for this entry measures how often a nipped bean becomes a stinker, or through which microbial route.
- **Gap versus ripeness.** The mechanical data on fruit ripeness<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> have not been tied, in the sources consulted here, to pulper damage and unpulped rates at different gap settings.
- **Pulp share.** Published shares of pulp differ and do not always state the basis (fresh or dry weight).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bizimungu et al., 2024 – Small drum and disc pulpers.</strong> Built and tested small drum and disc pulpers for Arabica (from Rwanda) and Robusta (from Benin). The drum pulper was more efficient on Arabica and left fewer unpulped Arabica cherries; damage stayed below 1% for both machines.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of the Saudi Society of Agricultural Sciences 23, 404–414 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jssas.2024.04.001">doi:10.1016/j.jssas.2024.04.001</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Pereira et al., 2023 – Compression of coffee fruit.</strong> Modelled ripe, semi-ripe and immature cherries under compression. Ripe fruit deformed more at lower stress and energy – the mechanical reason unripe fruit is harder to pulp.</p>
<p class="meta">AgriEngineering 5(4), 2303–2313 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/agriengineering5040141">doi:10.3390/agriengineering5040141</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Oliveros, Moya & Ramírez, 2001 – A new pulper (Cenicafé).</strong> Describes a small horizontal-drum pulper with nylon teeth and a gauge-set breast plate, rated at 300 kg of cherry per hour; it counts as working correctly with under 1% damage and under 1% unpulped fruit.</p>
<p class="meta">Avances Técnicos Cenicafé 294 · <a href="https://publicaciones.cenicafe.org/index.php/avances_tecnicos/article/download/1798/5222/3934">PDF</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Álvarez, 1991 – Pulping without water (Cenicafé).</strong> Compared pulpers run with and without water and showed that commercial horizontal-cylinder machines can pulp dry. This finding underpins Colombia's ecological wet milling.</p>
