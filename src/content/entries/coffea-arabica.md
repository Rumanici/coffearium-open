## A hybrid species

*Coffea arabica* is a species of the coffee genus, *Coffea*, and the source of arabica coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It arose when two other coffee species, *C. canephora* and *C. eugenioides*, crossed in the wild.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The hybrid kept both parents’ full sets of chromosomes. That makes it a tetraploid, with four sets and 44 chromosomes in all.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Because the sets come from two different species, botanists call it an allotetraploid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> *C. canephora*, one of the two parents, is the species grown as robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

When the cross happened is not settled. A 2024 genome study puts it at 350,000–610,000 years ago; earlier estimates differ widely (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Where it grows wild

Wild arabica is native to the forests of south-western Ethiopia and eastern South Sudan.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In South Sudan the species grows on the Boma Plateau.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A third area, Mount Marsabit in northern Kenya, is included in the native range by Kew’s Plants of the World Online. A 2019 study considers it more likely an area where arabica was introduced and cultivated.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

It is a highland plant. Kew gives an altitude of 950–1,950 m for the wild populations, but two reviews give a higher range (see the figure).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In its native forests, arabica became established as an understorey shrub, growing in the shade of taller trees.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## The plant

The arabica plant is a shrub or small tree with evergreen, usually shiny leaves, 2–8 m tall according to Kew’s plant page<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> and 2–7 m in the Flora of Tropical East Africa.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Its flowers are white and sweetly scented. The fruits are red, sometimes yellow or purple.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Each fruit, the coffee cherry, holds two seeds; their structure is described in [Coffee cherry anatomy](/entries/coffee-cherry-anatomy/).

The species can fertilise itself. Its flowers mainly self-pollinate, although insects sometimes carry pollen between plants.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> *C. canephora* (robusta), by contrast, is strictly self-incompatible, according to a 2017 textbook chapter: it needs pollen from another plant.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Flowering follows a dry spell. A short dry period of 2–4 months helps to trigger it.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Once the flower buds are mature, a rainfall of 5–10 mm can start them growing again.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The fruit takes on average 220–243 days from flowering to ripeness.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> When and how often each origin picks is covered in [Harvest calendars](/entries/harvest-calendars/).

## Climate it needs

*C. arabica* is a plant of cool, moist highlands. Its optimum mean annual temperature is 18–21 °C, and its optimum rainfall 1,200–1,800 mm a year.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Above 23 °C, the fruits develop and ripen faster, which often lowers quality.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Frost can kill the plant.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> *C. canephora*, from lowland forest, has a warmer optimum of 22–26 °C according to this 2006 review<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>; a 2020 yield study in South-East Asia found its best yields below 20.5 °C (see Coffea canephora). How growers match these needs to a site is covered in Site selection and climate.

## How much coffee is arabica

In its July 2026 count, the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) put arabica at 94.4 million of 178.8 million 60-kg bags for 2025/26. That is 52.8% by our calculation.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The share moves from year to year with the size of the harvests, above all in Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Other sources give about 60% or about 65% as a typical share.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Threats and climate change

Wild arabica is listed as Endangered on the Red List of the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN).<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The listing rests on climate change. A 2019 study projected that wild populations could fall by 50% or more by 2088, and by over 80% in a few models. Without climate change in the assessment, the species would be Least Concern.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Farmed arabica faces the same pressure. In Ethiopia, 39–59% of the current coffee-growing area could become unsuitable for coffee by the end of this century, if nothing is done.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The same study found that moving coffee to new areas, together with forest conservation, could increase the suitable area at least fourfold.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> Worldwide, a 2015 study projected that climate change will reduce the area suitable for coffee by about 50% across emission scenarios.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

*C. arabica* is also susceptible to coffee leaf rust. Breeders took resistance genes mainly from the Timor Hybrid, a natural cross of arabica and robusta (see [Arabica varieties and their lines](/entries/arabica-varieties/)).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The index below shows arabica among all accepted coffee species with their Red List categories. ## Genome and origin

*C. arabica* is an allopolyploid with 2n = 4x = 44 chromosomes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Its two subgenomes descend from *C. canephora* and *C. eugenioides*, which a 1999 study showed with molecular markers and genomic in situ hybridisation; genome assemblies of arabica and both parents confirmed it in 2024.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 1999 study also found low divergence between the subgenomes and the parent species, which pointed to a relatively recent origin.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The 2024 study found evidence for a single founding polyploidy event 350,000–610,000 years ago, followed by several bottlenecks before domestication.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It also found that wild accessions and the ancestors of cultivars split about 30,500 years ago, with gene flow between the two afterwards.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Dating the event is difficult because the subgenomes have diverged little from their parent species.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 2020 genome study gave a broad interval of 10,000 or 665,000 years before present.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2017 textbook chapter, citing a 2012 study, describes the speciation as very recent.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## A narrow gene pool

*C. arabica* has little genetic diversity, for three reasons that add up. The species probably arose from a single hybridisation event, so it started from very few individuals.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Bottlenecks before domestication narrowed it further.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> And predominant self-fertilisation leads to high levels of inbreeding.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2020 study found in arabica the lowest level of genetic diversity reported so far in any crop species.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Domestication narrowed it again: the traditional varieties Typica and Bourbon derive from populations grown in Yemen; that history is told in [Arabica varieties and their lines](/entries/arabica-varieties/).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Ethiopia’s wild coffee forests are the main storehouse of *C. arabica*.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The metro map in the varieties entry shows how breeders worked with that narrow base and where robusta genes entered it.

## Reproduction and fruit cycle

The mating system is primarily self-fertilisation; pollinator-mediated outcrossing may occur occasionally.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Field studies in Colombia, reviewed in 2025, attribute 56.4–95.3% of fruit set to self-pollination and 9.4–26.7% to insect-mediated cross-pollination.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Flower buds reach a stage of true dormancy when physiologically mature; rainfall of 5–10 mm can trigger the resumption of growth.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A dry spell of 2–4 months, corresponding to the quiescent growth phase, stimulates flowering.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Fruit development from flowering to maturity takes on average 220–243 days.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Farther from the equator, one or two flowering events per year are typical.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Native range and altitude

Sources agree on the core of the native range, the forests of south-western Ethiopia and the Boma Plateau in South Sudan, but not on its altitude.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Kew gives 950–1,950 m.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2006 review gives 1,600–2,800 m for the Ethiopian forests where arabica is native, and a 2025 review the same range for the highlands where the hybridisation occurred.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Two 2017 textbook chapters give ranges within these limits, one of them citing a 2006 conspectus of the genus.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> For comparison, coffee in Ethiopia is mostly confined to 1,200–2,200 m.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

## Arabica and robusta in brief

The full comparison of the two crop species is in the entry on *C. canephora*. The short version:

| Trait | *C. arabica* | *C. canephora* (robusta) |
|---|---|---|
| Chromosomes | 2n = 4x = 44, allotetraploid<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> | One of the two parent species of arabica<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Breeding system | Mainly self-fertilising<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> | Strictly self-incompatible<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> |

| Caffeine, green coffee | 0.9–1.5% of dry weight (average)<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> | 1.2–2.4% (basis not stated)<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> |
| Lipids, green coffee (basis not stated) | about 15% (average)<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> | about 10%<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> |
| Sucrose and trigonelline | Higher<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> | Lower<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> |
| Chlorogenic acids | Lower<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> | Higher<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> |

A 2001 study of wild accessions found more trigonelline and sucrose in *C. arabica* and more chlorogenic acids and caffeine in *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> According to a 2017 textbook chapter, arabica also holds more carbohydrates, lipids and organic acids, and robusta more protein, ash and transition metals.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Caffeine by species and by brew is covered in [Caffeine](/entries/caffeine/).

## Conservation status in detail

The IUCN Red List (version 2026-1) lists *C. arabica* as Endangered.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Plants of the World Online gives the criterion as A3b, a projected population reduction.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The 2019 study behind it modelled the species under several climate models, emission scenarios and migration scenarios. It projected population reductions of 50% or more by 2088 and declines of about 30% in extent of occurrence and area of occupancy in many cases.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It used a generation length of 16–30 years, with 21 years as a conservative value.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Without climate change the species would be assessed as Least Concern, which makes the listing an example of how strongly climate projections shape Red List outcomes.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Kew’s plant profile gives the same Endangered status.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The wild forests matter for the crop. At least 80% of Ethiopia’s coffee comes from forests, forest-like habitats or shaded farms, and the wild coffee forests are the main storehouse of *C. arabica*.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The threats to all wild coffee species are covered in [The coffee genus](/entries/coffea-genus/).

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Salojärvi et al., 2024 – The arabica genome.</strong> Assembles chromosome-level genomes of arabica and both parent species and resequences wild and cultivated accessions. Dates the founding hybridisation, traces the bottlenecks that left the species narrow and separates wild populations from the ancestors of cultivars.</p>
<p class="meta">Nature Genetics 56(4), 721–731 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-024-01695-w">doi:10.1038/s41588-024-01695-w</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Scalabrin et al., 2020 – One polyploidisation, little variation.</strong> Sequences arabica genomes and compares them with the parent species. Concludes that a single polyploidisation event and predominant self-fertilisation explain why arabica has less genetic diversity than any other crop studied.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 10, 4642 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-61216-7">doi:10.1038/s41598-020-61216-7</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Moat, Gole & Davis, 2019 – Least Concern to Endangered.</strong> Models the future of wild arabica under many climate scenarios and applies the Red List criteria. Including climate change moves the species from Least Concern to Endangered.</p>
<p class="meta">Global Change Biology 25(2), 390–403 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.14341">doi:10.1111/gcb.14341</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Moat et al., 2017 – Ethiopian coffee under climate change.</strong> Maps where coffee can be grown in Ethiopia now and under future climates. Finds that 39–59% of the current growing area could become unsuitable this century, while relocation and forest conservation could expand suitable land at least fourfold.</p>
<p class="meta">Nature Plants 3, 17081 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/nplants.2017.81">doi:10.1038/nplants.2017.81</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>DaMatta & Ramalho, 2006 – Drought and temperature in coffee.</strong> Reviews how arabica and robusta respond to heat, cold and water stress. Gives the optimum temperature and rainfall ranges of both species and explains arabica’s origin as an understorey shrub.</p>
<p class="meta">Brazilian Journal of Plant Physiology 18(1), 55–81 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1590/S1677-04202006000100006">doi:10.1590/S1677-04202006000100006</a></p>
