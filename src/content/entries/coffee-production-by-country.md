## Who grows the coffee

Coffee is grown in many countries, but a few supply most of it. The US Department of Agriculture (USDA) counts world production in bags of 60 kg of green coffee. For marketing year 2025/26 it estimates 178.8 million bags.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Brazil produced 63.0 million and Vietnam 31.7 million of them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The next three are Colombia with 12.5 million bags, Indonesia with 12.4 million and Ethiopia with 11.6 million.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Together, the five largest producers grew 73.3% of the world’s coffee (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A chart of the 12 largest producers and a world map are in The coffee belt.

USDA describes Brazil as the producer that sets the pace. Its July 2026 report states that Brazil accounts for nearly 40% of world production and strongly influences supplies and prices.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Brazil, over 70% of the arabica is grown in Minas Gerais and about 70% of the robusta in Espírito Santo.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

For 2026/27, USDA forecasts a record world crop of 189.7 million bags. Brazil is forecast at a record 71.9 million bags and Vietnam at 32.5 million.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Forecasts change from one report to the next, so this entry uses the 2025/26 estimates.

## Arabica and robusta by country

Two species make up almost all traded coffee: arabica (*C. arabica*) and robusta (*C. canephora*).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> USDA splits each country’s crop between them. In 2025/26 the world produced 94.4 million bags of arabica and 84.4 million bags of robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> That is 52.8% arabica and 47.2% robusta (our calculation).

The split differs strongly between countries. In USDA’s tables, Colombia, Ethiopia, Honduras and Peru produce only arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Vietnam grows almost only robusta: 30.5 of its 31.7 million bags.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Brazil grows both, 38.0 million bags of arabica and 25.0 million of robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Each species is therefore concentrated in fewer countries than coffee as a whole. Brazil grew 40.2% of the world’s arabica in 2025/26; Vietnam grew 36.1% of its robusta (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> USDA states that Brazil typically supplies 40–50% of world arabica, depending on its two-year crop cycle.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How the two species differ in the cup and in the field is set out in Arabica or robusta.

## How concentration has grown since 1990

The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) publishes production by country for every calendar year since 1961.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In its series, Brazil and Vietnam together grew 25.7% of the world’s coffee in 1990.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> By 2024 they grew 47.9% (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Most of that change came from Vietnam. Brazil’s share moved within a band: 24.2% in 1990, 34.4% at its highest in 2010 and 30.0% in 2024 (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Its lowest share in the series was 16.8%, in 1995.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Vietnam’s production rose from 92,000 t in 1990 to 2.02 million t in 2024.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Its share of world output grew from 1.5% to 17.9% (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Almost all of this coffee is robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The Central Highlands are the country’s main coffee-growing area.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A 2008 study of smallholders there describes a coffee boom followed by a bust in producer prices.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The planned entry The rise of robusta will cover this growth in depth.

## Grown for export, drunk at home

Most coffee is grown to be exported, but producing countries also drink a large share. The ICO calls the difference “exportable production”: total production minus domestic consumption.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

In 2025/26, USDA estimates the following (shares: our calculation):<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Brazil** drank 22.3 million bags, 35.4% of its crop. Among single countries in the USDA table, only the United States consumed more.
- **Ethiopia** drank 4.5 million bags, 38.9% of its crop. USDA lowered its export estimate because more coffee than expected was drunk at home.
- **Indonesia** drank 4.8 million bags, 38.9% of its crop.
- **Vietnam** drank 4.9 million bags, 15.5% of its crop.

These shares are not the reverse of export shares. Stocks change from year to year, and exports include roasted and soluble coffee converted to green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Some producers also import. In 2025/26 Colombia imported 2.2 million bags to keep up domestic consumption after a smaller crop.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Consumption by country is the subject of Coffee consumption by country.

## Small and emerging producers

FAOSTAT, the FAO database, lists 78 countries and territories with coffee production in 2024 (our count).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The ten largest produced 84.4% of the total of all 78 (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> At the other end, 45 countries produced less than 10,000 t each. Together they made up 0.9% of the total (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Some small producers are well known to buyers of specialty coffee. In FAOSTAT for 2024, Kenya produced 49,500 t, Rwanda about 17,840 t and Jamaica 6,521 t.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Others produce coffee at the edge of the belt: the United States 3,817 t and Nepal about 501 t.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Where Nepal’s coffee grows is described in The coffee belt.

Ethiopia has grown fastest of the ten largest producers in recent years. Its crop rose from 8.15 million bags in 2021/22 to 11.56 million in 2025/26, by 41.8% (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> USDA expects a record 12.1 million bags in 2026/27. It writes that over half of the cultivated area was replaced with higher-yielding varieties over the last four years.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Why USDA, ICO and FAO disagree

Three official datasets count coffee production by country. They differ in their year, their unit and how they fill gaps, so their totals never match exactly.

- **Year.** USDA uses each country’s marketing year, which begins in April, July or October; Brazil’s runs from July to June and Vietnam’s from October to September.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The ICO converts production from each country’s crop year to the coffee year starting on 1 October.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> FAOSTAT uses the calendar year.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> How the two trade years work is set out in Crop year and coffee year.
- **Unit.** USDA and the ICO count 60-kg bags of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> FAOSTAT counts tonnes.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> One bag is 0.06 t (our calculation).
- **Who reports.** USDA’s figures are based on reports from its overseas posts and on secondary information.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> ICO Members report estimates for the current and following crop year.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Where Members give none, the ICO uses other sources, among them USDA.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> FAO collects official statistics from member countries through questionnaires.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Gaps.** If FAO has no official or unofficial value, it imputes one and flags it.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the FAOSTAT extract of 11 October 2026, 29 of the 78 values for 2024 were imputed; they make up 12.6% of the total tonnage (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Ethiopia’s value is one of them.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The ICO also states that its country figures are built for a consistent supply and demand balance. In its words, they do not represent the production occurring on the ground in each country.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The years do not line up: Brazil’s USDA year 2024/25 runs from July 2024 to June 2025, while FAOSTAT counts January to December 2024.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> For the world total, USDA and the ICO lie within 0.4% of each other (our calculation). FAOSTAT is higher by both of its measures.

Two smaller points affect the line chart. FAO’s world value for 2024, 11.28 million t, is larger than the sum of the 76 country values republished with it, 11.13 million t (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The chart divides by the world value, so Brazil’s 2024 share there is 30.0%. The coffee belt entry divides by the sum of countries and gives 30.4% for the same year.

## Arabica and robusta in the statistics

USDA and the ICO both split world production by species. Their figures for 2024 are close. USDA gives 102.3 million bags of arabica and 74.5 million of robusta for 2024/25, 57.8% arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The ICO gives 102.1 million and 75.4 million for coffee year 2024, 57.5% arabica (our calculations).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The arabica share moves with Brazil’s crop. In USDA’s figures it fell from 57.8% in 2024/25 to 52.8% in 2025/26, when Brazil’s arabica fell and its robusta reached a record (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> USDA explains that Brazil’s arabica alternates between on-years and off-years, which drives most of the yearly change in world arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The causes of this cycle are covered in Coffee yields.

Roasted and soluble coffee are converted to green coffee before they are counted. The ICO multiplies roasted coffee by 1.19 and soluble coffee by 2.6; USDA uses the same factors.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In its export data, for countries that produce or export both species, the ICO classifies roasted and soluble coffee 50/50 as arabica and robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> FAOSTAT reports one item, “Coffee, green”, with no split by species.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Country figures that do not agree.** For Laos, FAOSTAT and USDA differ by a factor of about 6.5 (our calculation); no source consulted for this entry explains why.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Imputed values.** Ethiopia, the fifth-largest producer, has an imputed FAOSTAT value for 2024.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> No source consulted here assesses how close FAO’s imputations come to later official figures.
- **World total in FAOSTAT.** The FAO world value for 2024 is larger than the sum of the country values republished with it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> No source consulted here explains the difference.
- **Long series by species.** No source consulted for this entry gives a country-by-country split of arabica and robusta back to 1990; the share chart therefore covers all coffee.
- **Domestic consumption.** Estimates of how much producing countries drink change between reports, as the two USDA figures for Ethiopia show (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
