## What an F1 hybrid is

“F1” stands for the first filial generation: the plants that grow from seed of a cross between two parents. In arabica coffee, breeders cross a cultivated line with a wild tree collected in Ethiopia or Sudan.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The cultivated parent is an American cultivar or a line that carries genes from the Timor Hybrid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Centroamericano and Mundo Maya both have T5296, a descendant of the Timor Hybrid, as one parent.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Their other parents are the Sudanese landrace Rume Sudan and the wild Ethiopian accession ET01 from the genebank of CATIE in Costa Rica.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> How the F1 group fits among the other lines of arabica is shown in [Arabica varieties and their lines](/entries/arabica-varieties/).

## Why the parents are distant

Breeders cross wild Ethiopian and Sudanese trees with cultivated lines from the Americas because the two groups are genetically distant.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In Central America, the first such hybrids considerably widened the genetic diversity of the coffee grown in the region.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Why cultivated arabica started from so narrow a base is the subject of [The arabica bottleneck](/entries/the-arabica-bottleneck/).

Such hybrids can outyield their parent lines, an effect attributed to hybrid vigour, or heterosis.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In a 2005 trial, hybrid populations yielded 22–47% more than their mother lines.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In 15 trials in Central America, hybrids also yielded earlier and varied less between harvests than pure lines.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a 2017 study with young plants in a climate-controlled room, two F1 hybrids produced fewer leaf rust spores than the inbred line Caturra.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Why the seed is not replanted

An F1 tree carries a mixed set of genes from two distant parents. Its own seed comes from self-pollination, and in this second generation (F2) the characteristics segregate: the plants are no longer uniform.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The breeding system of the species is described in [Coffea arabica](/entries/coffea-arabica/).

World Coffee Research (WCR) states it plainly in its catalogue: seed taken from hybrid plants will not have the same characteristics as the parent plants.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Producers therefore buy new hybrid plants from nurseries rather than saving seed.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Pure-line arabica varieties, by contrast, are propagated by seed.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Two routes to the field

There are two ways to plant many uniform F1 plants. One is to clone the best hybrid tree; the other is to produce hybrid seed with a mother plant that makes no pollen.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Cloning.** Most F1 hybrids are multiplied as clones. Somatic embryogenesis grows embryos in a laboratory from body cells of the selected tree, not from fertilised seed.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The plantlets can then serve as mother plants for rooted cuttings in a nursery, which multiplies them further and lowers the cost.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to a coffee science textbook, these methods need high skills and suitable laboratories.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Seed from a mother without pollen.** In 2001, researchers found an arabica tree that makes no pollen among Ethiopian trees in the CATIE genebank.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Such a tree cannot fertilise itself. Planted beside a pollen parent, the seed it sets comes from cross-pollination, ideally by that parent.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Crossed with the Sarchimor Marsellesa, it gave Starmaya, the first arabica F1 hybrid produced from seed in this way.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Varieties in the F1 group

Centroamericano was released for producers in Central America.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In a trial in seven contrasting environments, it and Mundo Maya outperformed the most widely planted American cultivar.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Starmaya, bred by CIRAD and ECOM, was released by ECOM after field trials in Nicaragua.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Marsellesa, the pollen parent of Starmaya, is not an F1 hybrid. It is a Sarchimor (Timor Hybrid 832/2 × Villa Sarchi CIFC 971/10), a line selected to the eighth generation (F8).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Ruiru 11 from Kenya, released in 1985 (1986 according to the Starmaya paper), is catalogued as an F1 hybrid of a different kind.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Its seed comes from hand-pollinating Catimor mother plants with a multi-cross male selection. Producers receive a mixture of sibling hybrids.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Yield in the Central American trials

A comparison published in 2011 ran 15 trials in Costa Rica, El Salvador and Honduras, at 750–1,580 m. Of these, 10 were under agroforestry and 5 in full sun.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> About 20 hybrids from crosses of American cultivars with Ethiopian and Sudanese accessions (ET6, ET15, ET25, E41, E416, E531, Anfilo and Rume Sudan) were grown beside pure-line cultivars such as Caturra, Catuai, Pacas and CR95.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Yield was recorded over three to seven harvests per trial and expressed in grams of green coffee per tree per year.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

<figure>
<div data-figure="f1-hybrids-trial-yields"></div>
<figcaption>Mean yield of the F1 hybrids and of the pure-line cultivars in each of the 15 trials, in g green coffee per tree per year, averaged over 3–7 harvests. Trials set up in 1999–2001.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup></figcaption>
</figure>

The hybrid mean was above the line mean in every trial, by 8% to 127%; the difference was significant in 14 of the 15 trials.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Over all trials, the hybrid mean was 460 g against 290 g under agroforestry (+58%) and 754 g against 562 g in full sun (+34%).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In nine trials, the best hybrid, named Centroamerica in the paper, produced on average about 37% more than the best line of each trial.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The authors conclude that hybrids could encourage producers who had moved to full sun to return to agroforestry.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Stability and earliness also favoured the hybrids. The coefficient of variation of yield ranged from 29% to 70% for hybrids and from 46% to 94% for lines; the difference was significant at eight sites.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The hybrids’ earlier yields were statistically significant in nine of the 15 trials.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

A later trial in seven contrasting environments compared new F1 clones with conventional varieties. The conventional varieties were the least productive, producing 50% less than the best hybrid.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> For Starmaya, a multi-site trial in Nicaragua found 30% more coffee per plant than Caturra and Marsellesa. In a commercial field at Matagalpa, Starmaya averaged 41.1 bags (46 kg) of green coffee per hectare over three harvests (2015–2018), against 30.4 for Marsellesa.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Cup quality and other traits

The 2011 paper reports earlier sensory evaluations in which the hybrids performed as well as or better than the traditional lines.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the 2020 trial, H1-Centroamericano and Starmaya contained more d-limonene than Caturra, and both scored better than the control in sensory analysis.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> These are results of named trials, not general properties of F1 hybrids.

Heterosis has a cost in fertility. The 2005 study found the hybrids significantly more sterile than their parental lines.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Selection on an index of yield, bean weight and fertility improved yield and bean weight, but the gain in fertility was not significant.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Rust resistance depends on the parents: in Starmaya, field observations found 8% of plants susceptible to leaf rust, against 1% in Marsellesa.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Hybrids and leaf rust in a controlled study

A 2017 study tested whether hybrid vigour affects leaf rust in arabica that carries no specific resistance. It compared the inbred line Caturra with two F1 hybrids, GPFA 109 and GPFA 124. All three are susceptible, and the hybrids were not expected to carry specific resistance to prevalent races.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Six-month-old potted plants were grown in a phytotron, a climate-controlled growth room, under eight treatments.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> These combined two day/night temperature regimes (27–22 °C and 23–18 °C), two light levels and two nitrogen doses.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The light levels, 300 and 1,000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of photosynthetically active radiation, were meant to mimic agroforestry and full sun.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The nitrogen doses totalled 134 and 266 mg per plant over 11 weeks.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Four plants per treatment were inoculated with a mixture of rust spores collected in Colombia.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

Sporulation was measured as the weight of spores per infected leaf area, 43 days after inoculation.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Under the warmer regime, it was significantly lower in the hybrids than in Caturra in all four combinations of light and nitrogen. Under the cooler regime, the difference was significant only with high light and low nitrogen.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The paper reports slower infection in the hybrids in every treatment, although with the warmer regime and high light the course in GPFA 109 was very similar to that in Caturra.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> With the warmer regime, low light and low nitrogen, the latent period lasted about 21 days in Caturra and 37 days in the hybrids.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The authors suggest that hybrid vigour may have reduced disease incidence.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

Temperature, light and nitrogen also changed sporulation. In Caturra, the warmer regime gave about 2,000 times more sporulation than the cooler one according to the abstract; the results section gives 10,000 times more spores per infected leaf area.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In Caturra, high light with low nitrogen gave significantly more spores than the other treatments under both regimes.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Under shade and sufficient nitrogen, rust incidence on the hybrids was very low.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> These results come from young plants, two hybrids and one line in controlled conditions. For the field, the authors cite only their own unpublished preliminary observations that susceptible F1 hybrids are less severely attacked than pure lines.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Somatic embryogenesis and rooted cuttings

In the coffee process described in a 2018 review, the whole mass-propagation cycle takes about 24 months.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Embryos are multiplied in liquid culture, at 15,000–50,000 somatic embryos per gram of embryogenic suspension depending on the genotype, and pre-germinated in temporary immersion culture.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Acclimatisation in the nursery took 20–30 weeks in the 2010 process.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Cloning in culture can introduce mutations (somaclonal variation). The review reports rates of about 1.3%, and 0.9% in a sample of 800,000 plants; adjusted protocols brought it below 0.5%, with over 99% of trees conforming to the mother plant.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Horticultural rooted mini-cuttings taken from the laboratory plantlets multiplied each plant 14 times in six months and halved the production cost.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

According to a coffee science textbook, cuttings of upright shoots grown on mother plants have been used on a large scale in Central America to propagate arabica hybrids.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Since 1997, producers across Central America have had access to some F1 hybrid clones.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> As of 2018, more than 20 million arabica hybrid plants had been planted in the region.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Male sterility and seed purity

The pollen-free tree, CIR-SM01, is a natural mutant with a tall growth habit.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Its sterility comes from a malfunction of the tapetum, the tissue that feeds developing pollen, at an early stage.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The trait is recessive: in four F2 populations with 1,250 plants, 18.3% were sterile, which the authors suggest may reflect a two-gene (epistatic) 9:4:3 ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The sterility was complete and stable across environments and plant ages.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Seed gardens rely on natural pollination by the pollen parent.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Purity was the weak point. In 2016 fields, a mean of 16% of Starmaya plants were off-types, against 3% for Marsellesa.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The authors trace this to stray pollen and to residual heterozygosity in both parents, and aimed for at most 5% off-types with fixed parents, windbreaks and insect-proof netting.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The method limits the number of hybrids that can be made by seed to the number of available male-sterile plants.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Ruiru 11 shows the older seed route: hand pollination. WCR notes that this has made Ruiru 11 seed hard to supply in sufficient volume.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Cost and access

Cloned plants cost more than seedlings. In 2011, the extra cost per tree was USD 0.5–0.6, an investment of USD 2,500–3,000 per hectare.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the 2018 review, adding rooted mini-cuttings cut the production cost from USD 0.50–0.60 to 0.25–0.30 per plant.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The same review gives a sales price of about USD 0.8–1.0 for a cloned F1 plant against USD 0.3–0.5 for a seed-derived plantlet, and expects the extra cost to be repaid after three to four harvests.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Even with mini-cuttings, a cloned hybrid cost about 1.5 times as much as a traditional seedling in 2019.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The Starmaya authors estimated a hybrid seed at about USD 0.07, half the price of a cloned plantlet.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to a coffee science textbook, F1 varieties need vegetative propagation, which raises plantlet costs, and plantlet cost remains a barrier to wider use of in vitro methods.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> According to WCR, only a handful of F1 hybrids had become commercially available to producers, and only in select countries.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Seed purity at scale.** Whether seed gardens can keep off-types at or below the 5% the Starmaya authors set as commercially acceptable is not yet shown in a published field study consulted for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **More male-sterile mothers.** Seed-propagated hybrids are limited to crosses with the male-sterile plants available.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Performance outside Central America.** Starmaya was introduced to Vietnam and Cameroon for comparison with local varieties; no results were published in the sources consulted for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Rust on F1 hybrids in the field.** The lower sporulation of two hybrids was shown in young plants in a phytotron; the authors’ field evidence is unpublished preliminary observation.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bertrand et al., 2011 – Hybrids under shade and in full sun.</strong> Compared about 20 F1 hybrids from wild Ethiopian and Sudanese parents with pure-line cultivars in 15 trials in Costa Rica, El Salvador and Honduras. The hybrids gave higher, earlier and steadier yields, with the largest advantage under agroforestry.</p>
<p class="meta">Euphytica 181(2), 147–158 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s10681-011-0372-7">doi:10.1007/s10681-011-0372-7</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bertrand et al., 2005 – Yield, fertility and bean weight.</strong> Tested hybrids of Latin American cultivars and wild Sudan-Ethiopian trees at two sites. Hybrid populations out-yielded their mother lines but were more sterile, and index selection raised yield and bean weight.</p>
<p class="meta">Euphytica 141(3), 255–262 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s10681-005-7681-7">doi:10.1007/s10681-005-7681-7</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Etienne et al., 2018 – Cloning coffee at commercial scale.</strong> Reviews three decades of somatic embryogenesis in coffee, from bioreactors to rooted mini-cuttings. Reports production volumes, costs and the low rate of off-type plants in Central American F1 hybrid propagation.</p>
<p class="meta">Frontiers in Plant Science 9, 1630 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2018.01630">doi:10.3389/fpls.2018.01630</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Georget et al., 2019 – Starmaya.</strong> Describes the pollen-free arabica mutant, its inheritance and the seed gardens that use it to produce F1 seed. Reports yields, off-type rates and costs against cloned plants.</p>
<p class="meta">Frontiers in Plant Science 10, 1344 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2019.01344">doi:10.3389/fpls.2019.01344</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Marie et al., 2020 – Hybrids across environments.</strong> Compared new F1 clones with conventional varieties in seven contrasting environments, for yield, rust, bean traits, aroma compounds and sensory scores. Two hybrids, Centroamericano and Mundo Maya, outperformed the most planted American cultivar.</p>
<p class="meta">Euphytica 216(5), 78 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s10681-020-02608-8">doi:10.1007/s10681-020-02608-8</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Toniutti et al., 2017 – Hybrids, environment and leaf rust.</strong> Grew the inbred line Caturra and two susceptible F1 hybrids in a phytotron under two temperature regimes, two light levels and two nitrogen doses, then inoculated them with leaf rust. The hybrids were infected more slowly and produced fewer spores, most clearly under the warmer regime.</p>
<p class="meta">Frontiers in Plant Science 8, 2025 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2017.02025">doi:10.3389/fpls.2017.02025</a></p>
</div>
