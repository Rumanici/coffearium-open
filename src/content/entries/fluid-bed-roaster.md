## How a fluid-bed roaster works

A fluid-bed roaster has no moving parts inside its roasting chamber. The beans are kept in motion only by the current of hot air.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The air enters at the bottom of the chamber through a perforated plate and leaves at the top.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It must flow fast enough to lift the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A patent for such a roaster describes the air velocity as sufficient to levitate the beans in a bed of air.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

The same air mixes the beans and heats them; convection is the main heat transfer.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A fan draws the air in, a heater warms it, and a probe just before the chamber measures its temperature to control the heater.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> At the end of the roast, the beans fall by gravity into a separate cooling unit.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Some designs shape the chamber so that the air stream rotates, a "rotating fluidised bed".<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Others add inclined walls on which beans slide down. While sliding, they take up a larger share of heat by conduction before re-entering the fast air.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 1976 patent used an inclined back wall to guide the beans back into the airstream.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Spouted beds and the bean fountain

In a spouted bed, the air drives a jet of beans rapidly upwards at the air inlet.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2021 tracking study followed single beans in such a roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It found two zones: a dense bed in which beans move slowly, below 0.5 m/s, and a spout in which they rise at 0.5–1.5 m/s.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> From the bed, beans fall downward to the spout, parallel to the wall.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The motion changes during the roast. Beans become less dense, so they are more easily lifted and move faster in the spout.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> At the highest air flow with a small batch of green coffee, the study saw a circulating flow with no true bean bed.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Batch size and air flow therefore shape how the beans move.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Air temperature and air flow as controls

A fluid-bed roaster is steered through the hot air: its temperature and its velocity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The laboratory fluid bed of a 2005 study allowed accurate control of both.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The research roasts cited here held the air at one temperature for the whole roast.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In one study, a fast roast used 260 °C air for 170 s and a slow roast 228 °C air for 720 s, both to the same colour.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Air flow matters too. In a 2025 study on a 0.5 kg spouted-bed roaster, batch size and air flow changed the time–temperature profiles more than the inlet air temperature did.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> More air also speeds up drying: a high air-to-bean ratio can raise the air velocity at the bean surface, and the bean then dries faster.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The air-to-bean ratio is the mass of air per mass of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Fluid bed and drum compared

According to a 2017 roasting chapter, both are hot-air roasters, and both transfer heat by convection, conduction and radiation at the same time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The shares differ. In a fluid bed, convection dominates; in a drum, a substantial amount of heat may pass by conduction, especially with direct heating under the drum.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A drum is limited in air flow by the speed at which beans would be carried away with the exit air.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A fluid bed needs high air speed to keep the beans moving at all.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

When researchers made a laboratory fluid bed and a commercial drum follow the same bean-temperature profile, the kinetics of aroma formation were similar in both.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Heat transfer in a stream of air

**Convection first.** In a fluid bed, convection accounts for the main heat transfer.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a spouted bed, the heat-transfer coefficient in the spout is high, because beans there meet the full air-to-bean convection.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> As the beans lighten, more of them are carried by the air, so a greater fraction is heated by convection late in the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 1997 spouted-bed study described the heating as an unsteady-state heat-transfer process and derived one overall heat-transfer coefficient for it.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Conduction as an option.** Inclined walls add a phase of higher conduction while beans slide back.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2000 doctoral thesis on hot-air roasting recommended a fairly high proportion of conductive heat transfer and low air-to-bean ratios.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Radiation is disputed.** A roasting chapter treats radiation as usually negligible in hot-air roasters.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a laboratory fluid bed, however, the bean core never reached the set air temperature; the authors attributed this to radiation and called this typical of small equipment with a large air-to-bean ratio (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Air-to-bean ratio and the bean.** High ratios dry the bean faster and change the combinations of bean temperature and water activity during the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the same chapter, they slightly decrease final bean volume, and changing the ratio alone makes a limited but statistically significant difference in flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A flavour chapter links higher ratios to aroma stripping and a flatter flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Measuring temperature in a fluid bed

**Air temperature is the control signal.** In the laboratory fluid bed of the 2005 study, a probe placed just before the chamber controlled the heaters.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A patented fluid-bed roaster places its thermocouple at the air nozzle.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> This is the traditional way of automating a roaster: set and hold a hot-air temperature. The bean temperature then varies with green-coffee moisture, weather and a cold roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**The bed probe is a mix.** Thermocouples in a spouted bed measure a combination of bean and air temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2025 model of a spouted bed, fitted to logged inlet-air, outlet-air and product thermocouple data, shows the expected difference between thermocouple and bean temperature; it estimates system parameters such as a thermal response coefficient.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to a roasting chapter, most roasting systems use a probe that touches both beans and hot air, so their reading is a mix temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Bean core in the laboratory.** Research fluid beds made direct measurement possible. Fine thermocouples went into holes drilled in single beans, which were tied to a wire and moved partly freely within the fluidised batch.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The thesis behind that roaster averaged at least 10 curves from individual beans to allow for differences between them.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Exhaust measurement.** Air above a fluidised bed is turbulent. In the 2005 study, exhaust air flow could not be measured directly during roasting; it was calibrated in empty runs instead.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Carbon dioxide and water vapour were then measured in the exhaust in real time.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Fast and slow roasts in research

Several laboratory studies of high-temperature short-time (HTST) and low-temperature long-time (LTLT) roasting used fluid beds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> These roasters allowed defined process conditions, with accurate control of hot-air temperature and air velocity.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

- **Gas and water.** A 2005 study roasted 100 g batches fast and slow to the same colour and followed carbon dioxide and water in the exhaust (see Roasting and Degassing).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Aroma.** A 2008 study compared fast and slow roasting in a commercial drum and laboratory fluid beds. When both roasters followed the same bean-temperature profile, aroma kinetics were similar.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Structure.** According to a roasting chapter, at the same colour, fast-roasted beans expand more and have greater volume and porosity than slow-roasted beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Air flow.** In the 2025 spouted-bed study, batch size and air flow shaped the time–temperature profile more than inlet temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

In these studies the air temperature was held constant through the roast, while the bean temperature rose.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Recirculation, chaff and off-gas

According to the same roasting chapter, smaller roasters usually draw in fresh air and release the hot exhaust (an open system), which wastes energy.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Most large-scale operations recirculate a major part of the off-gas to the heater and back into the chamber.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 1976 fluid-bed patent recycled vented air through a recirculation duct to conserve heat.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Part of the off-gas must always leave the system. This share may pass through an off-gas cleaning step to meet air-pollution rules.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Chaff comes off the beans in the air stream. Patented designs collect it in a cyclone in the exhaust<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>; the laboratory roaster of the 2000 thesis removed silverskin by suction.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Continuous and industrial designs

Hot-air roasters can run in batches or continuously.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 1976 patent covers both batch and continuous versions of its fluid-bed roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 1968 CATIE paper, also presented at a 1969 chemistry congress, reported that coffee could be roasted continuously in a fluid bed within limits of temperature, air velocity and residence time.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> According to a 2017 roasting chapter, continuous systems were popular decades earlier but had become nearly extinct; batch roasters dominate because they are more flexible and easier to control.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

A patent granted in the United States describes a fluid-bed system for batches from small profile sizes to large production sizes.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry gives market shares of fluid-bed roasters.

## Sample and small roasters

The fluid-bed principle works at very small scale. Research roasters have roasted 100 g batches<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>, and a pilot spouted bed 200–500 g.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Small patented systems cover "profile" (sample) batch sizes as well as production.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry examined small home air roasters.

## Risks and defects

- **Bean breakage.** Any bean movement causes mechanical stress, and a good design minimises breakage.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Decaffeinated beans can be less stable mechanically and need more attention in fluid-bed roasters.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Batch size out of range.** With a small batch and high air flow, the bean bed can disappear into a circulating flow.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> As beans lighten they fluidise more easily; the tracking study suggests that commercial roasting may be best started at moderate air flow, reduced as density changes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Aroma stripping.** High air flows have been linked to flatter, less complex flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Reading the wrong temperature.** Air-temperature control does not guarantee the same bean temperature from batch to batch.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The bed probe reads bean and air together.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Off-gas.** Recirculating systems must vent part of their off-gas.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Air-to-bean ratio and flavour.** Sources disagree on how much the ratio itself matters (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Radiation.** Its share in small air roasters has not been measured separately in the sources found.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Sensory studies.** No peer-reviewed sensory comparison of specialty coffees roasted in fluid beds and drums at production scale was found.
- **Home air roasters.** No study consulted for this entry measured their air flow, temperatures or roast uniformity.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Geiger et al., 2005 – Gas and water in a laboratory fluid bed.</strong> Roasts 100 g batches in a fluid bed with controlled air temperature and velocity, fast (260 °C, 170 s) and slow (228 °C, 720 s) to the same colour. Measures bean-core temperature with drilled-in thermocouples and carbon dioxide and water in the exhaust; the core stays below the air temperature.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 70(2), E124–E130 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb07084.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb07084.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Al-Shemmeri et al., 2021 – Following single beans in a spouted bed.</strong> Tracks labelled beans by positron emission particle tracking in a pilot spouted-bed roaster at three batch sizes and several air flows. Beans move slowly in the bed and fast in the spout, and the flow pattern changes as they lose density.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Engineering 311, 110709 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfoodeng.2021.110709">doi:10.1016/j.jfoodeng.2021.110709</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Al-Shemmeri et al., 2025 – Modelling a spouted-bed roast.</strong> Builds a batch-scale heat and mass transfer model from logged inlet, outlet and product temperatures of a 0.5 kg spouted bed. Batch size and air flow shaped the profiles more than inlet temperature, and the model estimates system parameters such as a thermal response coefficient.</p>
<p class="meta">Beverages 11(6), 162 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/beverages11060162">doi:10.3390/beverages11060162</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Baggenstoss et al., 2008 – Fluid bed versus drum.</strong> Follows 16 aroma compounds in fast and slow roasts in a commercial drum and laboratory fluidising beds, all stopped at the same lightness. When both roasters followed the same bean-temperature profile, the kinetics of aroma formation were similar.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 56(14), 5836–5846 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf800327j">doi:10.1021/jf800327j</a></p>
</div>
