## Coffee under trees

The arabica coffee plant evolved in the undergrowth of tropical forests and tolerates shade.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Many producers still grow it under taller trees, called shade trees. A farm where crops grow under planted or kept trees is an agroforestry system.

According to a 2017 textbook chapter, most coffee once grew in systems that resembled forest.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Agronomists then promoted full-sun monocultures to raise yields and to fight leaf rust.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> These systems used dense hedgerows and heavy doses of fertilisers and pesticides.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Many farms converted to shaded or unshaded monocultures.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Shade is a trade-off, not a rule. According to the same chapter, trees protect coffee from heavy rain, strong sun and frost, add organic matter and limit weeds.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Badly managed shade can lower yields, worsen some pests and diseases, and compete with coffee for water and nutrients.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Where the climate fits coffee less well, shade matters more; where it fits well, coffee can grow without shade if inputs are high.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> How climate and altitude decide where coffee grows at all is covered in site selection and climate.

## From rustic coffee to sun coffee

Researchers group coffee farms by how much forest structure is left above the coffee. A 1999 study from Mexico described five systems, from forest-like to treeless.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2008 review gave each class its canopy cover, tree species and number of canopy layers (strata).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## How shade is measured

"40% shade" does not mean the same thing in every source. Studies measure different things.

- **Canopy cover** describes how much of the area above the coffee is covered by tree crowns. The 2008 review classes systems this way.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Irradiance reduction** is how much less light reaches the coffee than in full sun. A 2022 meta-analysis expressed shade this way.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Trees per hectare** counts the shade trees. The same meta-analysis used it as a second measure.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The three are related but not equal, and the meta-analysis found no agreed standard for recording such data.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The chart shows shade percentages from three sources side by side, each with what it measures.

## Which trees give the shade

Ideal shade trees, according to the 2017 textbook chapter, form an even canopy, root deeply and grow fast.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Evergreen species that survive hard pruning and drop plenty of leaf litter are preferred.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

- **Nitrogen-fixing legumes.** Most shade trees chosen belong to the genera *Inga*, *Erythrina*, *Leucaena*, *Gliricidia* and *Albizia*.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Their root nodules fix nitrogen, which adds to soil fertility.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Productive trees.** Banana, citrus, macadamia and, in Asia, durian and stink bean are grown with coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> They give producers fruit, timber, firewood and a second income.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

In commercial polycultures and shade monocultures the shade trees are pruned regularly.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Fallen leaves and pruned branches return organic matter and nitrogen to the soil.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## What shade changes

- **Temperature.** Shade trees damp both high and low temperature extremes.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In Mexico, swings in temperature, humidity and sunlight grew as shade cover fell.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Yield.** Results differ by cultivar. Low to moderate shade often costs little yield, while heavy shade lowers it for most cultivars.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Ripening and bean size.** Shade slowed ripening by up to a month in one trial and raised bean size in two.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Wildlife.** Farms with more trees keep more ant, bird and tree species.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Pests and diseases.** Effects go both ways. Shade favours some, such as leaf rust at equal fruit load, and limits others.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Microclimate buffering

**Leaf and air temperature.** In a Costa Rican trial under optimal growing conditions, coffee was grown with and without *Inga densiflora*.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The maximum temperature of shaded coffee leaves was up to 5 °C lower than in the monoculture.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> At night the minimum air temperature was 0.5 °C higher under the trees.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In the later years of the trial, without adequate pruning of the shade trees, radiation transmittance under the canopy fell to less than 40%.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A review of shade management puts the buffering of high and low extremes at up to 5 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Variability more than means.** A 2007 study compared a high-shade (60–80%), a medium-shade (35–65%) and a low-shade (10–30%) site in Chiapas, Mexico.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Seasonal means of temperature, humidity and solar radiation differed little between sites.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Their fluctuations increased significantly as shade cover decreased, and soil moisture swung more at the low-shade site.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The author proposed shade management as a possible adaptation for farms facing climate extremes.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Frost.** According to the 2017 textbook chapter, shade trees protect coffee from frost and from large day–night temperature differences.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The higher night minimum under *Inga* in Costa Rica points the same way.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured frost damage under shade.

## Yield response by cultivar

A 2022 meta-analysis built an open dataset of 255 yield records from 25 field studies covering 19 cultivars.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Shade was recorded as irradiance reduction relative to full sun (148 records) or as shade trees per hectare (80 records).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The interaction of shade and cultivar was highly significant.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Across 18 *C. arabica* cultivars, yield responses to shade were neutral, inverted-U shaped or falling.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

| Response to shade (irradiance reduction) | Cultivars | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Positive or neutral | Catucaí, Catimors (incl. Costa Rica 95), Sarchimors (Tupi, Obatã), Caturra, Oeiras, Pacas, Icatú | Koutouleas et al.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Inverted U, possible optimum 25–45% | Bourbon, Mundo Novo | Koutouleas et al.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Falling at all shade levels | Kona Typica | Koutouleas et al.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Trees per hectare | Overall negative; Catuaí inverted U | Koutouleas et al.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |

Overall, yields tended to be highest in full sun or under low to moderate shade, about 10–39%.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> High (40–70%) and very high (above 70%) shade gave the lowest yields for most cultivars.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The authors conclude that low to moderate shade of about 10–40% in many cases has little negative impact on yields.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> They found no data on wild Ethiopian accessions or landraces.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Single trials show the cost on good sites. Under the optimal conditions of the central valley of Costa Rica, 45% shade lowered the productivity of Costa Rica 95 by 18% but reduced alternate bearing.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Alternate (biennial) bearing is the swing between heavy and light crops in successive years. In the *Inga densiflora* trial, yields were similar in both systems while the shade trees were establishing.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In later years, when the shade trees were not adequately pruned, yield was 30% lower in the agroforestry system than in the monoculture.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> According to the 2017 textbook chapter, full-sun systems need fertilisers, pesticides and often irrigation; without them, plants suffer strong biennial bearing and dieback.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Bean size and cup quality

**Ripening.** In the central valley trial, 45% shade delayed the ripening of the berry flesh by up to one month.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Shade improved bean size, bean composition and beverage quality.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Sun-grown beans had higher sucrose, chlorogenic acid and trigonelline contents, which the authors linked to more bitterness and astringency in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Heavier fruit loads reduced bean size through competition among berries.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**A sub-optimal site.** At a low-elevation site in Costa Rica, Caturra and Catimor 5175 grew in full sun and under unpruned *Erythrina poeppigiana*.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Fruit weight and bean size rose significantly under shade.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Appearance of green and roasted coffee and the acidity and body of the brew improved for both varieties.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The effect on aroma was neutral for Caturra and slightly negative for Catimor.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

The author suggested that shade promotes slower, more balanced filling and more uniform ripening of berries.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The 2017 textbook chapter reads the evidence the same way: shade helps quality most in sub-optimal, heat-stressed regions.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Both quality trials are from Costa Rica; how far the result holds at high altitude or in other origins is not settled by them.

## Biodiversity and certification

A 2008 review pooled studies of ants, birds and trees across Latin American coffee landscapes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Sun coffee sustained the highest species losses.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Rustic coffee had equal or greater ant and bird richness than nearby forests and protected more species than any other coffee system.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Ant species were lost in every coffee system except rustic coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The review's own data show how class criteria and field values differ. Rustic farms in its dataset had a mean canopy cover of 62.05%, below the class criterion of more than 90%; sun farms had 5.05%.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Mean tree richness was 12.18 species in rustic coffee and 2.35 in sun coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Shade in certification.** The Smithsonian Bird Friendly label sets explicit shade criteria (as stated by the Smithsonian in August 2025).<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Farms in its agroforestry pathway must keep an average of 40% shade over coffee and 10 tree species per hectare, most of them native.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Farms in its forest conservation pathway set aside 40% of the land as forest instead.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> All Bird Friendly farms must be organic and free of deforestation in the last 10 years.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The source does not state how shade is measured for certification.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Other labels and their rules are compared in certifications and labels; what a "shade-grown" claim on a pack may say is covered in sustainability claims.

## Pests and diseases

**The rust paradox.** Shade affects coffee leaf rust in two opposite ways. Shade limits fruit load, and lighter-bearing plants are less susceptible.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> To separate the two factors, a Costa Rican trial evened out fruit loads by hand in shaded and sunny subplots.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> At equal fruit load, the epidemic was more intense under shade: incidence rose by 21.5% and severity by 22.4%.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The microclimate under shade, with smaller intra-day temperature swings and more frequent leaf wetness, was more conducive to the fungus.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The authors concluded that shade has antagonistic effects on rust.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The disease itself is covered in coffee leaf rust.

**Borers.** In eastern Uganda, the coffee berry borer (*Hypothenemus hampei*) was more common on sun-exposed plantations.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The white stem borer (*Monochamus leuconotus*) showed the opposite pattern and was more common under shade; for this pest, the differences between shade levels were most pronounced at low altitudes.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**General pattern.** A review of shade management states that more shade may raise the incidence of some commercially important pests and diseases and lower others.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It describes shade management as a possible tool of integrated pest management.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Water, nutrients and carbon

**Water.** Over three years in Costa Rica, soil water content showed better access to soil water at 100–200 cm depth in coffee with *Inga densiflora* than in coffee alone.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Runoff was 5.4% of rainfall in the agroforestry system and 8.4% in the monoculture.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Modelled drainage at 200 cm depth was 62% of annual rainfall in the agroforestry system and 68% in the monoculture.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No water stress was recorded in either system, as relative extractable soil water stayed above 20% in the dry seasons.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Simulations showed possible competition for water if the dry season were 4–6 weeks longer.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The authors read the results as complementarity in water use between coffee and shade trees.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Nutrients.** Shade trees can produce up to 14 Mg per hectare and year of litterfall and pruning residues, containing up to 340 kg nitrogen.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> At 100–300 trees per hectare, nitrogen fixation by leguminous shade trees may not exceed 60 kg N per hectare and year.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Shade trees may also compete with coffee for growth resources.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Carbon.** In the *Inga densiflora* trial, permanent above-ground biomass in the agroforestry system was twice that of the monoculture after seven years.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> How tree carbon enters a coffee's footprint is discussed in carbon footprint of coffee.

## Shade management

Shade is managed mainly by choosing the species, the planting density and the pruning.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Denser shade gives more environmental benefits, but too many trees restrict light and lower productivity.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to the 2017 textbook chapter, a full-sun system suits optimal sites where intensive inputs can be applied; shade is favoured where conditions are sub-optimal or inputs uncertain.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A review adds that the effects of shade depend on site, species and varieties, root and crown traits, and management.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Standard forestry practice needs adapting for open-grown trees in coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **A shared measure of shade.** Canopy cover, irradiance reduction and tree counts are used side by side, and the meta-analysis found no standard for recording them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Values across studies can only be compared with care.
- **Wild and landrace material.** The meta-analysis found no yield data for wild Ethiopian accessions or landraces under shade.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Quality beyond Costa Rica.** The two quality trials consulted here were both in Costa Rica, one at an optimal and one at a low-elevation site.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry tested shade and cup quality at high elevation or in Africa or Asia.
- **Frost.** No study consulted for this entry measured frost damage with and without shade.
- ***C. canephora*.** The yield findings above concern *C. arabica*; robusta was excluded from the meta-analysis dataset.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Koutouleas et al., 2022 – Shade and yield across arabica cultivars.</strong> Pooled 255 yield records from 25 field studies into an open dataset and modelled yield against shade by cultivar. Responses differed by cultivar, and low to moderate shade of about 10–40% often cost little yield.</p>
<p class="meta">Agronomy for Sustainable Development 42, 55 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s13593-022-00788-2">doi:10.1007/s13593-022-00788-2</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Philpott et al., 2008 – Biodiversity loss in coffee landscapes.</strong> Compared ant, bird and tree richness across coffee systems and forests in Latin America. Sun coffee lost the most species; rustic coffee matched or exceeded nearby forests for ants and birds.</p>
<p class="meta">Conservation Biology 22(5), 1093–1105 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1523-1739.2008.01029.x">doi:10.1111/j.1523-1739.2008.01029.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Vaast et al., 2006 – Fruit thinning, shade and bean quality.</strong> Grew Costa Rica 95 in full sun and under 45% shade on an optimal site, with different fruit loads. Shade delayed ripening by up to a month, improved bean size and cup, and cut productivity by 18%.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of the Science of Food and Agriculture 86(2), 197–204 · <a href="https://agritrop.cirad.fr/530251">Cirad Agritrop record</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Lopez-Bravo et al., 2012 – Shade and rust at equal fruit load.</strong> Evened out fruit loads by hand under shade and in full sun to separate their effects on leaf rust. At equal load, rust incidence and severity were higher under shade, so shade acts on rust in two opposite ways.</p>
<p class="meta">Crop Protection 38, 21–29 · <a href="https://agritrop.cirad.fr/564248">Cirad Agritrop record</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Siles et al., 2010 – <em>Inga</em> shade, microclimate and biomass.</strong> Compared coffee with and without <em>Inga densiflora</em> on an optimal Costa Rican site. Shade cut maximum leaf temperature by up to 5 °C and doubled permanent above-ground biomass after seven years; yield was 30% lower in later years, when the shade trees were not adequately pruned.</p>
<p class="meta">Agroforestry Systems 78, 269–286 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s10457-009-9241-y">doi:10.1007/s10457-009-9241-y</a></p>
</div>
