## What body and mouthfeel are

Mouthfeel is how a coffee feels in the mouth. The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) defines it as the tactile perception of the brew while it is in the mouth, leaving out temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It covers the brew’s thickness (viscosity), its texture, and other tactile properties such as astringency, a mouth-drying sensation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Mouthfeel is therefore touch – not taste, which belongs to the taste buds, and not smell.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

“Body” is the older word. According to the SCA, body meant the brew’s weight in the mouth together with its texture.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The SCA’s 2024 Coffee Value Assessment (CVA) widened it to “mouthfeel” so that astringency and a metallic feel are included as well.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A cupping textbook chapter describes the same two parts. Weight is the heft on the tongue, and texture the slipperiness, both compared with pure water.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It places the sensation mainly between the tongue and the roof of the mouth.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Astringency is usually classed as a feeling, not a taste. A review of wine research traces this view to a 1954 proposal that astringency is a feeling rather than a taste.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In coffee, the SCA describes mouth-drying as a drying, puckering or tingling sensation on the tongue and in the mouth.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## What in the cup gives body

A cup of coffee is mostly water, but what is carried in that water is not all dissolved. Four kinds of material contribute to how it feels.

- **Dissolved solids.** According to a brewing chapter, stronger coffee at the same extraction yield contains more solids and feels thicker in the mouth.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In a comparison of nine brew methods, the more total solids a brew held, the higher the panel rated its body.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Colloids.** These are large molecules dispersed in the brew. According to a roasting-chemistry chapter, polysaccharides – long chains of sugars from the bean’s cell walls – give the brew viscosity and mouthfeel.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> One 2025 study found that a melanoidin-rich fraction adds astringency.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Suspended particles.** Very small coffee particles, called fines, pass some filters and float in the cup. According to the brewing chapter, mouthfeel depends partly on them, particularly in French press and espresso.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Oil droplets.** Especially in espresso, coffee oil reaches the cup as tiny droplets spread through the water, an emulsion.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A cupping chapter describes “brew colloids” in which extracted oils gather around microscopic bean fibres.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## How brew method and filter change it

**Strength comes first.** In a study of nine brew methods, the espressos clearly had the most body. The Karlsbad pot and filter coffee had the least.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Total solids followed the same pattern: above 4% for the espressos, 1.43% for the French press and 1.03% for filter coffee, measured by oven drying.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A 2019 study of eight methods measured dissolved solids from 1.35% in French press coffee to 8.44% in a specialty espresso.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> More on brewing principles is in [Pour-over and immersion](/entries/brew-methods/).

**The filter decides the rest.** According to a brewing chapter, paper absorbs oil very efficiently, so paper-filtered brews are low in fat and are rated rather low in body.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In a French press, fines and the relatively inefficient metal mesh leave more sediment than a drip filter does. Pressing also squeezes oil from the bed into the brew.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the nine-method study, the French press brew carried the most fat per cup. It also had more body than the Karlsbad pot and the filter machine.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

The diterpenes cafestol and kahweol show how much a filter can hold back. In a 2025 study, paper-filtered home brews contained about 12 mg/L cafestol (range 4–24 mg/L), and one French press brew 86.8 mg/L.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Whether the type of paper matters is not settled. In a 2018 study, micro-perforated papers let the most diterpenes through.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In the 2025 study, perforated and ordinary papers did not differ significantly.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Why fines form, and how a pour moves them in the bed, is explained in [Particle size and fines](/entries/particle-size-and-fines/) and [Pour-over technique](/entries/pour-over-technique/).

**Oil is not the whole story.** French press coffee is high in oil. Yet a brewing textbook notes that it is not generally perceived as fuller. The likely reason is that it is drunk as a long, weaker cup.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Grit.** Very small particles feel rough. The SCA names cezve (ibrik) coffee as the example of a rough, gritty, chalky or sandy mouthfeel.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## How cuppers describe mouthfeel

In the CVA descriptive assessment, the cupper rates the total intensity of mouthfeel on a 15-point scale.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Intensity here means the amount of body or thickness.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The cupper then ticks up to two terms from a check-all-that-apply (CATA) list to say what kind of mouthfeel it is.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The SCA’s list has five terms:<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

- **Rough** (gritty, chalky, sandy) – the feel of very small particles.
- **Oily** – the feel of the brew’s oils.
- **Smooth** (velvety, silky, syrupy) – a smooth texture.
- **Mouth-drying** – the drying, puckering sensation of astringency.
- **Metallic** – an aroma and mouthfeel the SCA links with tin cans or aluminium foil.

Each term has a sensory example in the standard, such as alum solution for mouth-drying.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the affective assessment, the cupper rates how good the mouthfeel is, not how strong.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A note such as “lacks body” or “the silky body is its best quality” belongs there.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How the two assessments fit together is set out in the [cupping](/entries/cupping/) entry.

The Coffee Taster’s Flavor Wheel is built on the World Coffee Research (WCR) Sensory Lexicon.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The lexicon has its own mouthfeel group: mouth drying, thickness, metallic and oily.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It lists body (fullness) separately, in a group of overall attributes it calls amplitude.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> For each attribute it gives references that serve as the standard for that attribute.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The CVA document notes that the lexicon does not fully cover mouthfeel, which is why the SCA added terms of its own.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Our [flavour wheel](/tools/flavour-wheel/) shows the CVA descriptor families.

## The physics of thickness

**Strength and viscosity.** In the eight-method study, viscosity grouped the brews much as strength did. The espressos were the most viscous, the moka pot and a high-pressure espresso variant came next, and the four filter and immersion brews did not differ significantly from each other.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Density did not differ between methods.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The authors note that dissolved solids are known to affect the sensory property called body, citing the nine-method study.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In that study, total solids explained much of the variation in rated texture and body (r² = 0.69).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> One capsule espresso stood out, scoring higher for body than its total solids would suggest.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Pressure and temperature.** According to a brewing chapter, an earlier study found that raising espresso pressure gave a more viscous brew with more intense body.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The same chapter notes that hotter water extracts more lipids from the bed, which it links to body and mouthfeel.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Without heat, fewer low-polarity compounds such as coffee oil are extracted.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Espresso as a multi-phase liquid.** A crema chapter describes espresso as an emulsion of oil droplets, a suspension of solid particles and a foam of gas bubbles, all in one cup.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The same chapter reports a consumer study that grouped espresso mouthfeel terms into thick, rich, round, lingering, mouth-coating and smooth.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The chapter suggests that preparations of different concentration need a mouthfeel vocabulary of their own.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Time in immersion.** In a 2024 descriptive study of full immersion brews, astringency and a viscous mouthfeel generally increased with brew time at 4 °C and 22 °C, whatever the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Brews of 8, 12 and 24 h at both temperatures were significantly more astringent than 4-h brews.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Across samples, astringency was positively correlated with TDS.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> More on cold brewing is in the [cold brew](/entries/cold-brew/) entry.

## The chemistry: polysaccharides, melanoidins and phenolics

**Polysaccharides.** Cell-wall polysaccharides do not seem to contribute to aroma, but according to a roasting-chemistry chapter they give the brew viscosity and mouthfeel.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Roasting breaks down part of them, mainly the side chains of the arabinogalactans; cellulose and mannans stay largely intact.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured how much of the viscosity of brewed coffee is due to polysaccharides.

**Melanoidins and astringency.** A 2025 study traced astringency in a high-body drip coffee by splitting the brew into fractions and tasting each.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The astringency sat in the fraction below 5 kDa; the larger molecules tasted like water.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> After three rounds of separation, the strongest signals in the final fraction came from condensed polyphenolic compounds, and its light absorbance pointed to melanoidins.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Added to water, or to a low-body coffee to match the high-body one, it was perceived as drying or astringent.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Some of its molecules matched products of roasted chlorogenic acids in a model system.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Chlorogenic acids and over-extraction.** A brewing chapter counts chlorogenic acid lactones among the bitter or astringent compounds that dissolve only after the early, easily soluble acids and sugars.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> As extraction continues, the profile moves from sweet and acidic towards bitter, harsh and astringent.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Green coffee itself is described as tasting sweet and astringent.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The chemistry of these acids is covered in [Acids in coffee](/entries/acids-in-coffee/).

**How astringency works.** Research on wine gives the mechanism most often discussed. Phenolic compounds bind with proteins, including those in saliva.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Some researchers argue that the resulting loss of lubrication drives the drying sensation; others found evidence against this link, and the review calls the mechanism undefined.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The coffee study tested perception, not this mechanism, for its melanoidin fraction.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Roast.** A roasting chapter describes body as rising with roast degree to a point and then falling, so that over-roasted coffee may taste thin.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> For a study that rated body across roast colours, see [Carbohydrates and body](/entries/carbohydrates-and-body/); how the timing of roast phases relates to body is discussed in the [Maillard reaction](/entries/maillard-reaction/) entry.

## Measuring mouthfeel

**Sensory panels.** Trained panels rate mouthfeel attributes against references. The WCR lexicon gives a reference for each attribute, including thickness, oily and mouth drying.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The 2024 immersion study used “astringent” and “viscous” as its mouthfeel attributes.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In the CVA, the descriptive form records intensity and CATA terms, and the affective form a separate judgement of quality.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Instruments.** Total solids can be measured by drying a weighed sample to constant weight<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> or, as dissolved solids, with a refractometer.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Viscosity was measured with an Ostwald-type capillary viscometer at 20 °C in one study.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Lipids were measured as esterified fatty acids after transesterification.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No peer-reviewed measurement of turbidity in brewed coffee, by filter type, was consulted for this entry; figures that circulate for it are left out.

**Practical comparison.** You can compare filters by brewing the same coffee, dose and water through paper and through a metal mesh, then tasting both side by side for thickness and grit. Keep the strength the same, for example by checking both with a refractometer, because stronger coffee feels thicker on its own.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Oil versus strength.** No study consulted for this entry varied oil content while keeping strength constant. Whether oil itself adds body in filter-strength coffee is therefore not established.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Turbidity and fines.** The textbook link between fines and mouthfeel<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> lacks a peer-reviewed measurement of suspended particles by filter type among the sources consulted here.
- **Polysaccharides.** Their contribution to the viscosity of brewed coffee is stated in a textbook<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> but was not quantified in any study consulted for this entry.
- **Astringency compounds.** The melanoidin fraction was shown to add astringency; whether it acts through salivary proteins, as wine phenolics are thought to, was not tested.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Shared vocabulary.** The CVA added mouthfeel terms because the lexicon does not fully cover them.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How consistently cuppers use these new terms has not been tested in a study consulted here.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Gloess et al., 2013 – Nine methods side by side.</strong> One coffee was brewed by nine methods, from espresso to filter machine, and analysed for total solids, fatty acids, aroma and sensory profile. Rated body rose with total solids; espressos had the most body, the Karlsbad pot and filter coffee the least, and the French press the most fat per cup.</p>
<p class="meta">European Food Research and Technology 236, 607–627 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00217-013-1917-x">doi:10.1007/s00217-013-1917-x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Angeloni et al., 2019 – Eight methods, viscosity and strength.</strong> Eight brew methods were compared for dissolved solids, density, pH, viscosity and chemical composition. Espressos were the strongest and most viscous; V60, AeroPress, cold brew and French press did not differ significantly in viscosity.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Research International, 1327–1335 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodres.2018.10.022">doi:10.1016/j.foodres.2018.10.022</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Linne et al., 2025 – Melanoidins and astringency.</strong> A high-body drip coffee was separated step by step, and each fraction was tasted against the coffee. A polyphenol-rich melanoidin fraction made water and a low-body coffee perceptibly more astringent; the authors present this as the first evidence that melanoidins contribute to coffee’s tactile profile.</p>
<p class="meta">Food & Function 16, 2870–2880 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1039/d4fo04934a">doi:10.1039/d4fo04934a</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Liang et al., 2024 – Immersion brews over time.</strong> A trained panel profiled full immersion brews made at 4, 22 and 92 °C and sampled over time. At the two cooler temperatures, astringency and viscous mouthfeel generally increased with brew time, and astringency rose with dissolved solids.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 14, 19298 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-69867-6">doi:10.1038/s41598-024-69867-6</a></p>
</div>
