## What coffee berry disease is

Coffee berry disease (CBD) is a fungal disease of arabica coffee (*Coffea arabica*). It is caused by *Colletotrichum kahawae*.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Older papers call the fungus *Colletotrichum coffeanum*.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> It was first reported in Kenya in 1922.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The fungus infects flowers and fruit at every stage, from flowers to ripe cherries, and occasionally leaves.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The heaviest losses follow infection of green berries.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Infected green berries develop dark brown spots that can cover the whole cherry and affect how the seed develops.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Where nothing is done to control it, the disease can destroy up to 70–80% of the crop.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Coffee leaf rust attacks the leaves. Coffee berry disease attacks the flowers and fruit, the crop itself.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Kenya, a 2012 study ranks leaf rust as the second most important coffee disease, after CBD.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Which stages of the fruit are at risk

A coffee fruit takes months to grow from flower to ripe cherry. Its susceptibility changes along the way.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

- **Flowers** can be infected at all stages.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Pinhead fruit**, the tiny fruit just after flowering, is fairly resistant.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Expanding green berries** are the most susceptible, about 4–14 weeks after flowering.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Colombia’s coffee research institute, Cenicafé, gives the critical period as 4 to 14–20 weeks of fruit age.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Mature green berries**, hard and full-sized, are fairly resistant again.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Ripening berries** become susceptible once more.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The disease shows two kinds of spot. An **active lesion** is a dark, sunken spot that spreads fast and can cover the whole berry. A pale pink crust of spores forms on it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A **scab lesion** is buff-coloured, grows slowly and produces few or no spores.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to a 2017 textbook chapter, a scab can stay latent until ripening and then damages mostly the pulp, not the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## Rain, cool weather and altitude

The fungus needs free water from rain, mist or dew to infect.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Rain is the main force that spreads it. Water running down branches and drops falling from diseased berries carry the spores, mostly downwards; wind-driven rain carries them a short way.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

For many years the disease was confined to higher altitudes.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 1970 review links its spread to a wetter and cooler phase of the East African climate in 1961.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> According to Cenicafé, the disease is most severe in the highlands of Africa.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a 2025 survey in southern Ethiopia, altitude was among the most important factors linked to disease intensity.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

The entry on site selection and climate covers how altitude shapes the coffee farm.

## Resistant varieties

Breeding has been the main answer. High fungicide costs led coffee research centres to give priority to breeding resistant varieties.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to a 2017 textbook chapter, resistant varieties have been available for some time and are a more effective solution than spraying.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

- **Rume Sudan** is a highly resistant arabica variety.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **The Timor Hybrid**, a natural cross of *C. arabica* and *C. canephora*, carries a resistance gene, and so do Catimor lines derived from it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Ruiru 11** was released in Kenya in 1985 and **Batian** in 2010. Both resist CBD and leaf rust.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **In Ethiopia**, about 20 pure lines with good tolerance to CBD were selected and given to producers from 1978 onwards.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The entry on Ethiopian landraces describes the country’s own coffee populations.

The classic Kenyan varieties SL28 and SL34 are susceptible to CBD.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The varieties themselves are covered in the entry on arabica varieties.

According to a conference paper on Kenyan varieties, outbreaks of CBD and leaf rust led Kenya to start breeding for disease resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Susceptible varieties in Kenya need intensive spray programmes against CBD, leaf rust and bacterial blight.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Why other coffee regions watch for it

The European and Mediterranean Plant Protection Organization (EPPO) keeps a database of plant pests. As of October 2026, it lists the disease as present in 14 African countries, from Ethiopia and Kenya to Angola and Cameroon.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Cenicafé gives 15 African countries, citing a 2022 CABI datasheet.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Cenicafé calls the disease one of the main threats to the coffee industry in Asia and Latin America.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Outside Africa the picture changed in 2024. A report from Yunnan, China, identified the fungus on *Coffea liberica*.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> EPPO lists China as “present, restricted distribution”.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2025 survey of coffee anthracnose in China, based on samples from 2019, did not find it (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Several countries list *C. kahawae* as a quarantine pest, a pest that import rules aim to keep out.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> According to Cenicafé, exclusion is the first line of defence.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## The pathogen and its name

*Colletotrichum kahawae* (authority Waller & Bridge) is listed by EPPO in the family Glomerellaceae, order Glomerellales, class Sordariomycetes.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> EPPO also lists the name *C. kahawae* subsp. *kahawae*.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Older papers, such as a 1982 histology study, used the name *Colletotrichum coffeanum* for the berry disease pathogen.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Literature that cites “*C. coffeanum*” therefore has to be read with care. A 1991 fact sheet distinguishes CBD isolates from other *Colletotrichum* strains that live on bark, and notes that related species cause other diseases in Guatemala and Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The disease belongs to a wider group of fungal problems on coffee fruit. A 2017 textbook chapter lists CBD among the fungal diseases that attack coffee cherries, with berry blotch (*Cercospora coffeicola*), American leaf spot (*Mycena citricolor*), pink disease and oily spot (*Colletotrichum* spp.).<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Cenicafé lists the same and other diseases as possible confusions in the field, including anthracnose caused by other *Colletotrichum* species complexes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Infection and the two lesion types

**Germination and entry.** After the conidia (asexual spores) germinate, they form appressoria that penetrate the cuticle directly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A short biotrophic phase, in which the fungus feeds on living cells, is followed by necrotrophic growth that kills the tissue.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Temperature and water.** According to a 1991 University of Hawaii fact sheet, the optimum temperature for germination and lesion development is 22 °C. Germination can occur up to 30 °C, and the minimum is 10 °C. With added nutrients, the optimum rises to 27 °C and the maximum to 35 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Infection needs free water, and spores lose their infectivity after 10–12 days of drying.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Active lesions.** These begin as small, dark, sunken spots that spread rapidly and can cover the berry. The fungus forms a pale pink crust of spores on the surface.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The spore-bearing structures are acervuli; Cenicafé describes them as orange.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Scab lesions.** These develop slowly, are buff-coloured and sporulate sparsely or not at all.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In a 1982 histology study of inoculated berries and seedling hypocotyls, resistant varieties quickly formed a layer of cork cells beneath the infection that stopped the fungus.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A 2006 review states that these cork barriers corresponded to the scab lesion.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Effect on the seed.** According to the 2017 chapter, severe infection in the green stage produces dark brown spots that end up covering the cherry and affect bean development and quality.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> A milder attack forms a scab in which the infection stays latent until ripening and affects mostly the pulp.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## Epidemiology

**Inoculum between crops.** A 1970 review describes infective conidia produced on the maturing wood of the twigs.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Where only one crop hangs on the tree, reducing this twig inoculum can work; where crops overlap, control needs a series of protective sprays.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 1991 fact sheet states that CBD isolates are rarely recovered from tissues other than berries (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Dispersal.** Rain is the main factor in spread. Dispersal is mainly downwards in the tree, by water running along branches and drops from diseased berries, with wind-blown rain carrying spores over short distances.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Cenicafé likewise describes short-distance dispersal of conidia by rain water.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Altitude and site.** The disease was confined for many years to the higher altitudes, and its spread was associated with a wetter and cooler phase in East Africa in 1961.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In southern Ethiopia, districts, altitudes and cultivar types were the most important variables associated with disease intensity in a 2025 study.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> No altitude band with a source meeting our number rule was found for this entry, so none is given.

**Wild coffee.** In surveys of arabica in four Ethiopian montane rainforests, CBD was found most often in Bonga (40.0%) and Yayu (26.3%), and less often in Harenna (18.6%) and Berhane-Kontir (6.0%).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Genetics of resistance

**Three loci.** A 2006 review, citing Kenyan work from 1980, describes resistance in arabica as apparently controlled by major genes at three loci.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Rume Sudan carries the dominant R gene and the recessive k gene. The moderately resistant variety K7 carries only k. The Timor Hybrid carries one gene at the T locus, with intermediate gene action.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Other authors estimated three to five recessive genes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Ck-1.** A 2008 mapping study crossed Catimor (resistant) with SL28 (susceptible).<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> It found eight AFLP and two microsatellite markers tightly linked to a major resistance gene, named Ck-1, within an 11 cM segment introgressed from *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The authors consider Ck-1 likely to be the T gene of earlier studies.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Species differences.** Resistance appeared complete in *C. canephora* and partial in *C. arabica*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Breeding results.** In Kenya, the complex hybrid Ruiru 11 showed a good level of resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Ruiru 11 (1985) and Batian (2010) combine resistance to CBD and leaf rust.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> According to the 2014 conference paper, Batian is tall and true-breeding, while SL28, SL34 and K7 are susceptible to CBD, leaf rust and bacterial blight; K7 resists some races of leaf rust.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In Tanzania, eight clones with high levels of resistance were selected and multiplied vegetatively.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Ethiopia, about 20 pure lines with good tolerance were distributed to producers from 1978.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2011 review of fungal diseases in Ethiopia and breeding in Kenya describes 40 years of breeding against leaf rust, CBD and coffee wilt disease.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Durability of resistance

No breakdown of CBD resistance is reported in the studies consulted for this entry.

- **Mechanism.** The 1982 histology study concluded that cork barrier formation is likely to be stable and race-nonspecific.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Quantitative resistance.** Earlier authors suggested that CBD resistance is horizontal (quantitative), and differential host–pathogen interactions were seldom found in Ethiopia.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **No adapted races in Kenya.** Differential pathogenicity was never observed in Kenya.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In tests of progenies from 66 crosses of Ruiru 11 with 7 isolates of different pathogenicity, crosses and isolates both had significant effects, but their interaction did not. The authors concluded that the pathogen is unlikely to have adapted to the cultivar.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Disagreement.** Some researchers report evidence for physiological races of *C. kahawae*; others found none.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The entry on coffee leaf rust describes, for comparison, how rust resistance from the Timor Hybrid has been lost in several varieties.

## Chemical control and its cost

Fungicides are used to keep CBD in check on susceptible varieties. According to the 2017 chapter, periodical applications are required.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> A 2006 review calls the costs of fungicides against leaf rust and CBD extremely high, and names difficulties of application.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Timing.** A 1991 fact sheet reports that early-season applications work only when flowering is early and the rainy season ends early.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Where crops overlap on the tree, control requires a series of protective sprays.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Product groups.** The same fact sheet recommends that systemic fungicides be mixed or rotated with non-systemic protectants.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Coffearium does not give product or dose advice.
- **Cost.** A share of production costs for CBD sprays alone was not found in a source that meets our number rule, so none is given.

## Quarantine and the risk for Latin America and Asia

**Listings.** The EPPO database records *C. kahawae* on the A1 list of Brazil (2018) and as a quarantine pest in Mexico (2018) and China (2021).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Pathways.** According to Cenicafé, spread by seed has not been demonstrated, but the pathogen can be present as a surface contaminant on beans.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Rules may prohibit or restrict imports of coffee plant material, and travellers carrying infected plant material are named as a risk.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The 1991 fact sheet advises taking seed only from healthy berries, treating it with a fungicide seed dressing and bringing cultivars in through quarantine facilities.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Detection.** Cenicafé published a field guide in 2023 so that symptoms can be told apart from other fruit diseases, for rapid containment and eradication of any incursion into Colombia.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In Colombia, Cenicafé warns that the disease could cause large losses and higher production costs in susceptible varieties.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## What it means for the cup and the buyer

According to the 2017 chapter, there is no evidence that diseases change the synthesis of specific compounds in the bean directly; their effect runs through the plant and the fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Severe CBD affects bean development and quality, while scab affects mostly the pulp.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The entry on harvesting and sorting describes how diseased cherries are removed before processing.

For buyers, the disease matters through varieties. In Kenya, SL28 and SL34 are susceptible, while Ruiru 11 and Batian are resistant.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> According to a 2017 textbook chapter, Ruiru 11 combines resistance to CBD and leaf rust with high yield and compact growth, and a later report pointed to component lines that combine high cup quality with CBD resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Status in China.** Whether *C. kahawae* is established in Yunnan, and which subspecies the isolate belongs to, is not settled in the sources consulted here (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Races.** Whether the fungus forms physiological races that could overcome resistance genes is debated.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Altitude band.** Sources agree that the disease is worse at higher altitude in Africa, but no altitude range with adequate sourcing was found.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **Climate change.** No study consulted for this entry projects how warming will move the disease’s altitude range.
