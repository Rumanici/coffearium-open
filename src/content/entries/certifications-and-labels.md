brief:
  - Fairtrade is the only scheme covered here with a minimum price. From 1 December 2026 it is USD 2.00 per lb for washed arabica, plus a USD 0.20 Premium.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
  - The Rainforest Alliance premium for coffee has no minimum; first buyers negotiate it with certified farms.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
  - In 2022, at least 15.2% and at most 30.8% of the world's coffee area was certified under the main standards.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup>
  - Systematic reviews find higher prices for certified producers but disagree on household income; one found no clear benefit for workers' wages.<sup><a href="#src-s24">24</a>,<a href="#src-s26">26</a></sup>
  - Certification can support due diligence under the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) but does not replace it.<sup><a href="#src-s32">32</a></sup>

contested:
  - 'Effect of certification on household income: a 2018 systematic review of agricultural certification found higher prices and higher income from the sale of produce, but no evidence that total household income improves<sup><a href="#src-s24">24</a></sup>; a 2020 meta-analysis of 97 studies found household incomes 16–22% higher for certified farmers, with substantial heterogeneity across studies.<sup><a href="#src-s26">26</a></sup> Both cover several crops, not coffee alone; the study sets and methods differ.'
  - 'Who gains from a Fairtrade price floor: in Costa Rica, 1999–2014, certification was associated with higher incomes for farm owners, partly through a transfer from intermediaries, and with no effect on unskilled workers<sup><a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup>; a 2018 review across crops found that workers’ wages did not seem to benefit.<sup><a href="#src-s24">24</a></sup>'

## What a label is

EU law defines a sustainability label as a trust mark that sets a product apart for its environmental or social characteristics.<sup><a href="#src-s30">30</a></sup> Behind each coffee label is a standard: published requirements on topics such as prices, forests or farming inputs.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

Three kinds of programme are common in coffee:

- **Certification.** An independent body checks a farm or company against a published standard and grants a certificate. EU law defines a certification scheme as third-party verification against public requirements, with independent monitoring.<sup><a href="#src-s30">30</a></sup> Fairtrade, Rainforest Alliance, 4C, organic and Bird Friendly all use certification bodies or inspectors.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a>,<a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>
- **Verification.** Some programmes began as lighter checks. 4C describes its system from 2004 to 2015 as an entry-level verification system; it now calls itself a third-party certification system.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Company programmes.** A roaster or trader sets its own rules for its suppliers. A 2020 study in Colombia treats Starbucks C.A.F.E. Practices and Nespresso AAA as industry and company-led standards.<sup><a href="#src-s27">27</a></sup> Their requirements are not covered in this entry.

Organic is different again. In the EU, Switzerland and the United States, organic rules are law, not private standards.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a>,<a href="#src-s17">17</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> We describe each label by what its own documents require, not by how it is marketed. We do not rank labels.

## What each label guarantees

Each scheme covers different ground. Only Fairtrade sets a minimum price.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Only organic rules and Bird Friendly require organic farming.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The table compares what each scheme's current documents require.

**Fairtrade.** Fairtrade labelling began with national initiatives that joined to form Fairtrade International in 1997.<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup> Its coffee standard applies to producers and to every company that buys and sells Fairtrade coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Buyers may not pay producers less than the Fairtrade Minimum Price.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The Fairtrade Premium is paid on top of the minimum price or the market price, whichever is higher.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At least USD 0.05 per lb of it must go to productivity, quality or sustainable practices; the producer organisation's general assembly decides how.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Rainforest Alliance.** The UTZ organisation joined forces with the Rainforest Alliance.<sup><a href="#src-s35">35</a></sup> From 1 October 2025, version 1.4 of the Rainforest Alliance Sustainable Agriculture Standard replaced the earlier Sustainability Differential and Sustainability Investments with a single premium.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> For coffee, the standard sets no minimum amount for that premium.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Organic.** EU organic rules prohibit genetically modified organisms (GMOs) and mineral nitrogen fertilisers and limit pesticides.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> US rules require that no prohibited substance was applied to the land for 3 years before harvest.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> Switzerland lets organic coffee in from listed countries or recognised control bodies, with a certificate of inspection for each consignment.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Goods already cleared through customs in an EU member state need no certificate.<sup><a href="#src-s39">39</a></sup>

**4C.** 4C presents itself as an independent third-party certification system with social, environmental and economic criteria.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Since 1 January 2025, its EUDR add-on has been mandatory for all certified units.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Smithsonian Bird Friendly.** This label is run by the Smithsonian's National Zoo and Conservation Biology Institute.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> Every Bird Friendly farm must be organic.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Farms either keep shade trees over the coffee or set aside forest on their land.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>

## Prices and premiums

A **price floor** is a minimum that the buyer must pay, however low the market falls. Only Fairtrade has one among the schemes covered here.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Coffee prices are quoted in US dollars per pound (lb); 1 lb is 0.4536 kg. FOB (free on board) means the price for coffee loaded at the port of export.

Fairtrade raised the minimum for washed arabica from USD 1.40 to USD 1.80 per lb for contracts signed from 1 August 2023.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> From 1 December 2026, it is USD 2.00 per lb.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The organic differential rose from USD 0.30 to USD 0.40 per lb in 2023.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> From 1 December 2026, the Premium is USD 0.20 per lb, the same amount as in 2014.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup>

The floor applies to coffee bought and sold as Fairtrade.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Producing and certifying coffee does not guarantee that a buyer will purchase it as Fairtrade.<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup> The other schemes leave the price to negotiation. Organic coffee sold under Fairtrade earns the organic differential.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The organic texts consulted for this entry set no price.

## How much coffee is certified

The International Trade Centre (ITC) compiles area and production data reported by the standards; its 2024 report uses data from 2022.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> In 2022, Fairtrade, Rainforest Alliance, 4C and organic each covered 6.5–9.0% of the world's coffee area.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup>

Many farms hold more than one certificate, so the shares cannot simply be added. ITC therefore gives a range. At least 15.2% and at most 30.8% of the world's coffee area was certified in 2022.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> ITC itself warns that these figures are estimates that indicate a trend.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup>

Certified does not mean sold as certified. A 2014 review cited Fairtrade statistics in which, on average, 45% of coffee from Fairtrade-certified producers was sold on Fairtrade terms.<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup> The rest was sold outside Fairtrade terms.<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup>

## What the evidence shows

Independent studies ask whether certification changes anything for producers or forests. The answer depends on the outcome measured, the place and the method.

- **Prices.** A 2020 meta-analysis of 97 studies found that certified farmers receive 20–30% higher prices.<sup><a href="#src-s26">26</a></sup> A 2018 systematic review also found positive effects on prices.<sup><a href="#src-s24">24</a></sup>
- **Household income.** Studies disagree. The 2018 review found no evidence that total household income improves; the 2020 meta-analysis found incomes 16–22% higher.<sup><a href="#src-s24">24</a>,<a href="#src-s26">26</a></sup> Both cover several crops, not only coffee.
- **Workers.** In the 2018 review, workers' wages did not seem to benefit.<sup><a href="#src-s24">24</a></sup> A coffee study in Costa Rica found no effect of Fair Trade on unskilled workers.<sup><a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup>
- **Environment.** A 2017 review found negative outcomes significantly less often for environmental than for other outcomes.<sup><a href="#src-s25">25</a></sup> One study found organic certification in Costa Rica reduced chemical input use.<sup><a href="#src-s28">28</a></sup>

The 2017 review concludes that certification is not a panacea, especially for the social hardships facing smallholder producers.<sup><a href="#src-s25">25</a></sup> The Deep dive gives the methods and limits of each study.

## Labels and the law

**EU claims rules.** From 27 September 2026, a sustainability label shown to EU consumers must be based on a certification scheme or set by a public authority.<sup><a href="#src-s30">30</a>,<a href="#src-s31">31</a></sup> This applies to products from outside the EU as well.<sup><a href="#src-s31">31</a></sup> Organic terms under the EU organic regulation can still be used to show compliance with organic rules.<sup><a href="#src-s31">31</a></sup> The sustainability-claims entry covers these rules in full.

**EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR).** The EUDR requires coffee placed on the EU market to come from land not deforested after 31 December 2020.<sup><a href="#src-s32">32</a></sup> Operators may use certification in their risk assessment, but it does not replace their own due diligence.<sup><a href="#src-s32">32</a>,<a href="#src-s34">34</a></sup> As of October 2026, the EUDR applies from 30 December 2026, and from 30 June 2027 for micro and small operators established as such by 31 December 2024.<sup><a href="#src-s33">33</a></sup> The EUDR entry explains the obligations.

An **equivalence arrangement** is an agreement in which two governments accept each other's organic systems. ## Deforestation cut-off dates compared

A cut-off date is the day after which clearing forest for coffee disqualifies the land. The schemes use different dates, and some use more than one.

- **Fairtrade.** The coffee standard sets 1 January 2014 in requirement 3.1.1 (applicable from 2025) and 31 December 2018 in requirement 3.1.2 (from 2027).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Both cover primary and secondary forests, protected areas and areas of high conservation value or carbon storage.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The guidance for 3.1.2 borrows definitions of primary forest and forest degradation from the EUDR.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Rainforest Alliance.** Base requirement 6.1.1 sets 1 January 2014.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Separate EUDR-aligned requirements add 31 December 2020; they do not affect the certification decision for the standard itself.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **4C.** System Update 16 applies the EUDR forest definition and an additional cut-off of 31 December 2020.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Bird Friendly.** Farms must show no conversion of forest to agriculture within the last ten years, a rolling window rather than a fixed date.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>
- **EUDR.** The law sets 31 December 2020.<sup><a href="#src-s32">32</a></sup>

An earlier cut-off date is stricter than the EUDR date for the land it covers. Under the EUDR, certification still does not replace the operator's due diligence.<sup><a href="#src-s32">32</a></sup> Fairtrade's coffee standard added geolocation requirements for 2027, and the Rainforest Alliance runs a separate EUDR alignment check.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Certification and the EUDR in detail

Recital 52 of the EUDR allows certification and other third-party verified schemes to be used in the risk assessment.<sup><a href="#src-s32">32</a></sup> The same recital states that they should not substitute the operator's responsibility for due diligence.<sup><a href="#src-s32">32</a></sup> Regulation (EU) 2025/2650 moved the application date to 30 December 2026.<sup><a href="#src-s33">33</a></sup> Natural persons and micro or small undertakings established as such by 31 December 2024 follow from 30 June 2027.<sup><a href="#src-s33">33</a></sup>

The Commission's simplification review of 4 May 2026 did not consider it appropriate to propose further amendments to the legal text.<sup><a href="#src-s34">34</a></sup> It announced a repository of certification schemes for EUDR commodities, planned before December 2026.<sup><a href="#src-s34">34</a></sup>

## EU label rules in detail

Directive (EU) 2024/825 defines a sustainability label as a voluntary trust mark or quality mark, public or private, that sets apart a product, process or business for its environmental or social characteristics.<sup><a href="#src-s30">30</a></sup> A certification scheme, in its definition, must meet four conditions.<sup><a href="#src-s30">30</a></sup>

1. It is open to all traders on transparent, fair and non-discriminatory terms.<sup><a href="#src-s30">30</a></sup>
2. Its requirements are developed with relevant experts and stakeholders.<sup><a href="#src-s30">30</a></sup>
3. It has procedures for non-compliance, including withdrawal or suspension of the label.<sup><a href="#src-s30">30</a></sup>
4. Compliance is monitored by a third party independent of both the scheme owner and the trader.<sup><a href="#src-s30">30</a></sup>

The Commission's questions-and-answers document states that labels on the market as of 27 September 2026 must comply, with no further transition period.<sup><a href="#src-s31">31</a></sup> The directive does not list which coffee schemes meet the conditions. No source consulted for this entry assesses each coffee scheme against them.

## Impact studies: methods and limits

The central difficulty is selection. Producers who join a scheme may already meet many of its requirements.<sup><a href="#src-s28">28</a></sup> A 2012 study of organic coffee in Costa Rica notes that rigorous evaluations controlling for this bias were virtually non-existent in developing countries.<sup><a href="#src-s28">28</a></sup> It used propensity score matching, which compares certified farms with similar uncertified farms.<sup><a href="#src-s28">28</a></sup>

**Systematic reviews.** The 2018 review included 43 studies for quantitative effects and 136 qualitative studies for barriers and context.<sup><a href="#src-s24">24</a></sup> Most covered Latin America and sub-Saharan Africa, and their quality was mixed.<sup><a href="#src-s24">24</a></sup> The 2017 review screened over 2,600 papers and found 24 cases in 16 papers that compared certified and uncertified smallholders.<sup><a href="#src-s25">25</a></sup> Of 24 cases, 20 concerned coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s25">25</a></sup> Certification was associated with positive outcomes for 34% of response variables, no significant difference for 58% and negative outcomes for 8%.<sup><a href="#src-s25">25</a></sup> The 2020 meta-analysis pooled 97 studies; effects on production costs and yields were mixed and varied across standards.<sup><a href="#src-s26">26</a></sup>

**Coffee studies.** In Costa Rica, 1999–2014, Fair Trade certification of mills was associated with higher prices, sales and revenue when world prices were low and the minimum price was binding.<sup><a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup> It also lowered the chance of a mill closing.<sup><a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup> The authors' estimate, taken at face value, indicates that only 11% of eligible coffee was sold as Fair Trade over that period.<sup><a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup> In Colombia, a study of over 600 farmers in two Fairtrade cooperatives found limited gains from adding company-led and industry standards to Fairtrade.<sup><a href="#src-s27">27</a></sup> Most farmers in that study did not break even, whatever their certification.<sup><a href="#src-s27">27</a></sup>

**Forests.** A 2013 working paper studied a wild-coffee forest in Ethiopia using satellite data from 2005 and 2010.<sup><a href="#src-s29">29</a></sup> Forests under a shade coffee certification programme were less likely to be deforested than forests without forest coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s29">29</a></sup> Uncertified forest coffee showed no significant difference.<sup><a href="#src-s29">29</a></sup>

**Limits.** Most findings come from a few countries and periods. Standards have been revised since many studies were done, including the 2023 and 2026 Fairtrade prices and Rainforest Alliance version 1.4.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry evaluates the current versions.

## Open questions

- How large the certified coffee area is without double counting; ITC gives only a range and calls its figures estimates that indicate a trend.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup>
- What share of certified coffee is sold as certified under each scheme today. The figures consulted for this entry are older or from a single country.<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a>,<a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup>
- Which coffee schemes meet the EU definition of a certification scheme; the directive names none.<sup><a href="#src-s30">30</a></sup>
- Whether the higher Fairtrade minimum from 1 December 2026 changes producer incomes; no study has measured it yet.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- Why income effects differ so much between studies; the 2020 meta-analysis could explain the heterogeneity only partly.<sup><a href="#src-s26">26</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Oya et al., 2018 – Systematic review of agricultural certification.</strong> Included 43 quantitative studies of agricultural certification in developing countries, most in Latin America and sub-Saharan Africa. Certified producers received better prices and more income from produce sales, but workers' wages did not seem to benefit and the review found no evidence that total household income improved.</p>
<p class="meta">World Development 112, 282–312 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2018.08.001">doi:10.1016/j.worlddev.2018.08.001</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>DeFries et al., 2017 – Certification of tropical commodities.</strong> Screened over 2,600 papers and kept 24 cases comparing certified and uncertified smallholders, 20 of them on coffee. About a third of outcomes improved, most showed no difference, and negative outcomes were rarer for the environment than for other categories.</p>
<p class="meta">Environmental Research Letters 12, 033001 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/aa625e">doi:10.1088/1748-9326/aa625e</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Meemken, 2020 – Meta-analysis of sustainability standards.</strong> Combined results from 97 studies on smallholder farmers. Certified farmers received 20–30% higher prices and 16–22% higher household incomes on average, but effects on costs and yields were mixed and the variation between studies was only partly explained.</p>
<p class="meta">Global Food Security 26, 100373 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gfs.2020.100373">doi:10.1016/j.gfs.2020.100373</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Dragusanu et al., 2022 – Fair Trade and Costa Rican coffee.</strong> Followed all Costa Rican coffee mills from 1999 to 2014 and examined household incomes. Certification was associated with higher prices, sales and revenue when the minimum price was binding, and with higher incomes for farm owners, partly at intermediaries' expense; there was no effect on unskilled workers.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of the European Economic Association 20(4), 1743–1790 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvac026">doi:10.1093/jeea/jvac026</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Dietz et al., 2020 – Multiple certification in Colombia.</strong> Surveyed over 600 coffee farmers in two Fairtrade cooperatives, some also certified under 4C, C.A.F.E. Practices, Nespresso AAA, or Rainforest Alliance combined with Nespresso AAA. Extra certificates brought limited economic gains, and most farmers did not break even regardless of certification.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Development Studies 56(6), 1141–1160 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/00220388.2019.1632433">doi:10.1080/00220388.2019.1632433</a></p>
</div>
