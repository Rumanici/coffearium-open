## What a value chain is

A value chain is all the people and organisations that carry a product from its start to the consumer. For coffee, a textbook chapter lists producers, suppliers, processors and those who serve the drink.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same chapter states that the total value is decided when the consumer buys.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The word “chain” suggests one line, but coffee takes many routes, and some actors are skipped. A lot is a batch of coffee from one origin and process, traded as a unit. This entry describes who the actors are, what each one does and where control sits. How much money each one keeps is covered in Who earns what along the chain.

## From the farm to the export port

**Producers** grow the coffee. They range from smallholders to large plantations.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In Colombia, an estimated 60% of production comes from farms of less than 5 ha.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Kenya’s production is described as increasingly dominated by smallholders.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Collectors**, often called middlemen, buy small quantities from farms and sell them on. In Ethiopia before 2008, producers sold their harvest to licensed collectors, who sold it to licensed suppliers.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> **Cooperatives** pool their members’ coffee instead. In Kenya, cooperative-owned mills gather coffee from small farms.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Processors** run the wet and dry mills that turn cherry into green coffee (see Hulling and dry milling). Mills can belong to cooperatives, plantations or exporters.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> **Exporters** sell the green coffee to buyers abroad.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The terms are set in standard contracts (see Green coffee contracts).

## From the export port to the cup

**International trading houses** buy from exporters and sell to roasters. According to a 2018 NGO report, they handle export and import, logistics, storage, risk management and finance, and they work mainly in bulk grades on thin margins.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> How much of this trade runs through Switzerland is covered in Switzerland and the coffee trade.

**Importers and agents** bring coffee into the consuming country and sell it to roasters. According to the textbook, large conventional roasters rely almost entirely on this importing trade.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> **Roasters** turn green coffee into a branded product. Some specialty roasters buy free on board (FOB) from exporters or cooperatives, and some buy directly from producers (see Direct trade).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> **Retailers and cafés** sell the coffee to the consumer.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Where ownership and power concentrate

After export, few firms handle much of the coffee. In 1998 the two largest roaster groups, Nestlé and Philip Morris, held 49% of the world market for roasted and instant coffee. The top five held 69%.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In the same year the two largest green coffee traders, Neumann and Volcafe, held 29% of the market.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Later figures use other bases. A 2021 study, citing a market-data company’s retail shares, gives 38% of global roast and instant coffee for Nestlé and Jacobs Douwe Egberts in 2014.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Two NGO reports also estimate the share of the ten largest roasters, but on other bases and partly from the same report series, so they are not independent of each other.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> These numbers cannot be compared directly (see Contested).

Concentration shapes who sets the terms. A 2019 review finds that buyers now have more bargaining power over other members of the chain than they had for a long time.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Why the structure matters for producers

Until 1989, export quotas under the International Coffee Agreements aimed to prevent oversupply and keep prices within a band (see The International Coffee Agreements).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The quota system lapsed in 1989.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Many producing countries then dismantled the coffee boards and institutes that had regulated export sales.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2019 review links this to weaker bargaining power for producing countries and producers.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Producers usually have to accept the price the market offers, even when it does not cover their costs.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 1997 study cited in the review estimated that producers’ share of total coffee income fell from about 20% in 1971–1988 to 13% in 1989–1995.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Shares from other studies, years and countries are compared in Who earns what along the chain.

A 2005 book calls the result a “coffee paradox”: a coffee boom in consuming countries alongside a coffee crisis in producing countries.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Its explanation is that consumers pay mostly for symbolic qualities and services, not for the material coffee that producers sell.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## How big the chain is

The International Coffee Organization (ICO) reports that the value of coffee exports grew from USD 8.4 billion in 1991 to USD 35.6 billion in 2018.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The summary does not say which forms of coffee this includes. For green coffee, an ICO overview of 2022 gives an average export value of about USD 20 billion a year.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

The often-quoted figure of “over USD 200 billion” for the whole industry is an estimate, not a statistic. The 2018 Coffee Barometer dates it to 2015 and traces it to a 2017 report for the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The 2022 ICO overview uses it, for revenues at retail level, without a source.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## How value chains are governed

Value chain research asks who coordinates a chain and how. A 2005 theory paper distinguishes five types of governance: hierarchy, captive, relational, modular and market.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> They range from tight coordination with strong power asymmetry to loose coordination with little.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Applied to coffee, a 2002 study argues that the chain after the International Coffee Agreement behaves as a buyer-driven chain.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In buyer-driven agricultural chains in general, the study names large retailers in industrialised countries, brand-name merchandisers and international trading companies as the key actors; in coffee it calls the chain roaster-driven.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The study also found that producer organisations could not replace governments as organisers of exports. Local exporters could not raise the funds to compete with international traders.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

A 2019 review traces the chain through several phases. Its table names roasters as the strongest actors, in some cases also retailers, and finds that government bodies withdrew to oversight or disappeared during liberalisation.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The 2005 book on the coffee paradox argues that producers will keep receiving low prices unless they control at least parts of the “immaterial” production of symbolic quality and services.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Market structures in producing countries

Liberalisation did not make every country alike. Three examples show different ways from producer to export.

**Colombia.** The Colombian Coffee Growers Federation (Federación Nacional de Cafeteros, FNC; the USDA report calls it Fedecafe) represents the sector and manages its programmes.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Most programmes are funded by the National Coffee Fund, which levies USD 0.06 per pound of coffee from producers, as stated in a 2025 USDA report.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> On average the FNC accounts for 18% of Colombian coffee exports; private companies export the rest.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The country has over 550,000 producers.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Ethiopia.** The Ethiopia Commodity Exchange (ECX) was launched in 2008.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Coffee was traded on it from December 2008.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> All coffee trade then had to pass through it; only cooperatives and large estates were exempt and could export directly after ECX grading.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Coffee sold on the exchange lost its traceability to the farm. This applied to 80% of Ethiopian coffee exports; the 20% exported by cooperatives and estates kept it.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In 2017 the rules were changed. Exporters may now sell directly to buyers abroad, bypassing the ECX, and may own washing stations.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

**Kenya.** Producers sell through the Nairobi Coffee Exchange (NCE), a spot market in existence since the early 1930s, through marketing agents they authorise.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> They may also sell directly to buyers abroad; such contracts must be registered with the Coffee Directorate.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Millers, marketing agents and exporters hold the stocks, and the government runs no specific price support programme for coffee farmers.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> This describes the system in 2017; later reforms were not checked for this entry.

## Finance and payment terms

Money moves along the chain more slowly than coffee. A 2019 review reports that roasters owned by investment funds extended the time they take to pay traders from an industry standard of 30 days to up to 120 days, citing a 2015 trade report.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It argues that this turns traders into cheap sources of capital for roasters, a role many smaller and regional traders cannot fill, and so adds to the dominance of a few multinational traders.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to the textbook, a model of investor-owned coffee companies restricts supply to merchants who can tie up capital for long periods, and so excludes small exporters and independent traders.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

At the other end, cooperatives often need pre-financing to buy their members’ coffee. They usually sell before the harvest to get it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> If prices then rise, members may sell to middlemen who pay cash, and the cooperative can fail to deliver.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How such forward sales are priced is explained in How a coffee price is made.

## Open questions

- **Current trader concentration.** No peer-reviewed or official source consulted for this entry measures the market share of the largest green coffee traders after 1998.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Number of coffee farms.** The 2018 Coffee Barometer notes that the widely used estimate of smallholder numbers had stood unchallenged for 15 years; no count consulted for this entry meets the sourcing rule.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Effects of the 2017 Ethiopian reform.** The rules now allow direct sales and traceable lots; no source consulted here measured the effect on producer prices.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **Kenya after 2017.** The description of the Kenyan system rests on a 2017 report; later changes were not checked.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Ponte, 2002 – The “latte revolution”.</strong> Uses global commodity chain analysis to show how the end of export quotas, new consumption patterns and corporate strategy changed the coffee chain. It finds power shifted to transnational roasters and traders and gives their market shares for 1998.</p>
<p class="meta">World Development 30(7), 1099–1122 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/S0305-750X(02)00032-3">doi:10.1016/S0305-750X(02)00032-3</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Daviron and Ponte, 2005 – The coffee paradox.</strong> A book-length analysis of why a boom in consuming countries coexists with a crisis in producing countries. It argues that value lies in symbolic quality and services that producers rarely control.</p>
<p class="meta">Zed Books, London · <a href="https://agritrop.cirad.fr/529401/">agritrop.cirad.fr</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Grabs and Ponte, 2019 – The evolution of power in the coffee chain.</strong> Traces the chain from the quota era through liberalisation to the rise of sustainability standards. The kinds of power changed, but the gap between buyers and producers did not.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Economic Geography 19(4), 803–828 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/jeg/lbz008">doi:10.1093/jeg/lbz008</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Mbakop et al., 2023 – Traceability in Ethiopia.</strong> Uses the 2008 and 2017 rule changes of the Ethiopia Commodity Exchange as a natural experiment. It describes how the exchange removed traceability for most exports and how the 2017 reform restored direct sales.</p>
<p class="meta">Agriculture 13(2), 368 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/agriculture13020368">doi:10.3390/agriculture13020368</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Ghoshray and Mohan, 2021 – The retail–international price margin.</strong> Analyses how retail coffee prices respond to international prices and suggests that concentration among roasters could explain the asymmetric response. It summarises roaster concentration in 1998 and 2014.</p>
<p class="meta">European Review of Agricultural Economics 48(4), 983–1006 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/erae/jbab027">doi:10.1093/erae/jbab027</a></p>
</div>
