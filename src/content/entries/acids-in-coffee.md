## What makes coffee acidic

Acids are responsible for sourness. Together with aroma and bitterness, sourness is a key part of how a coffee tastes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The main acids in green coffee are citric, malic, chlorogenic and quinic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Roasting removes some of them and forms new ones.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

According to the lead author of a drip-coffee study, writing for the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA), coffee's acids are weak acids. They release only part of their hydrogen ions in solution, yet still trigger the sour receptors on the tongue.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Two measures are in use:

- **pH** reflects the concentration of free hydrogen ions in the brew at that moment.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Titratable acidity** is the amount of base, usually sodium hydroxide, needed to bring a set volume of brew to a set pH.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

A 2000 paper reports that arabica brews are more acidic than robusta brews: pH 4.85–5.15 against 5.25–5.40.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Two brews can share a pH and still differ in titratable acidity.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In a 2018 study of light roasts, hot and cold brews had comparable pH, 4.85–5.13, but the hot brews held more titratable acid.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Where the acids come from

Roasting changes the acid profile in three ways. The acids of the green bean break down, chlorogenic acids split, and sugars form new acids.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Acids of the green bean.** Citric, malic and chlorogenic acids decrease during roasting, while quinic acid increases.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Total chlorogenic acids fall with the intensity of roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Chlorogenic acids split.** Their breakdown releases quinic acid and phenolic acids; both chlorogenic and quinic acids also form lactones during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For that reason, a 2000 paper concludes that chlorogenic acid apparently does not add to the acidity gained in roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The lactones are bitter, not sour (see Deep dive).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Sugars form new acids.** Four aliphatic acids – formic, acetic, glycolic and lactic – account for a major part of the acidity formed in roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Their main precursor in the green bean is sucrose, which makes up 3–8% of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

According to a 2023 conference study, phosphoric acid also increased with roast degree.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## How acidity changes during the roast

Because some acids form while others break down, acidity does not simply fall from the start.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In a 2024 study on a 5 kg drum roaster, beans were sampled every minute of 16-minute roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The titratable acidity of their brews rose, peaked during first crack, and fell back to its starting value by second crack.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The roast profile changed how fast acidity rose and fell. It barely changed the peak: seven profiles and three coffees peaked at about the same level.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> All roasts in that study ran past second crack.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The same pattern appears in pH. A 2000 study roasted robusta for 3 minutes at different temperatures. Extract pH fell from 6.1 at 150 °C to 5.7 at 240 °C, then rose to 6.3 at 280 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The paper considers the formation of the four aliphatic acids, particularly formic and acetic acids, a key factor in the pH minimum at medium roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In the cup, darker roasts taste less acidic. In a 2020 analysis of eight sensory studies, darker roasts went with more bitterness and less acidity, fruitiness and sweetness.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> According to two roasting textbook chapters, light roasts give more acidity in the cup than dark roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## pH, titratable acidity and sourness

A 2024 roasting study states that numerous studies found titratable acidity a better predictor of sourness than pH.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In a 2021 drip-coffee study, titratable acidity correlated strongly with perceived sourness.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In one of its experiments pH showed no relation to sourness; in another, only a weak one.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

The evidence is not uniform. A 2013 comparison of nine brewing methods found no correlation between perceived acidity and either pH or titratable acidity.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Its brews ranged from filter coffee at about 1% total solids to espressos above 4%.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Strength matters. In the drip-coffee study, titratable acidity rose in a straight line with total dissolved solids (TDS), the brew's strength.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> It showed little dependence on extraction yield.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Water also plays a part: its alkalinity neutralises part of the coffee's acids.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Acidity in cupping

In cupping, “acidity” is a sensory attribute, not a chemical measurement. The SCA defines it as “the perception of sour taste provoked by the brew, which may vary in intensity and character”.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> In the Coffee Value Assessment (CVA), acidity is rated twice. The descriptive form rates its intensity on a 15-point scale; the affective form rates the impression of its quality on a 9-point scale.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

The SCA's taste reference for sour is a citric acid solution.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> According to the SCA, there are no sensory references for terms such as “juicy” or “bright” acidity. Organic acids used as references tend to mislead, because coffees contain a mixture of many acids.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

Naming a coffee's acidity after one acid is therefore unreliable.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> According to the SCA, an acidity that reminds a taster of green apple has a good chance of not being malic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In a 2023 conference study, coffee experts did not correctly identify any of five acids added to coffee at their usual concentrations.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

The sensory notes from the 2023 conference study come from an abstract. It reports detection thresholds for citric, malic, acetic, lactic and phosphoric acid in brewed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Apart from citric acid, the thresholds lay around the acids' actual concentrations. The authors read this as a contribution to overall acidity, but not one an individual acid can be tasted by.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Its numbers are not given here, because no second source confirms them.

## How sugars become acids

A 2000 study traced the four aliphatic acids to their precursors. Green robusta beans were soaked in sucrose, glucose or fructose solutions, dried back to their initial moisture of about 10% (basis not stated) and roasted for 3 minutes at 150–280 °C in a sample roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Sucrose-treated beans gave more than twice the formic and acetic acid of the control, and glycolic acid rose even more; lactic acid did not change significantly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Model roasts of the pure sugars mixed with sand (240 °C, 15 min) gave the same four acids, in amounts largely independent of the sugar used.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Sucrose reacts more slowly at first, because its glycosidic bond must be split before the more reactive reducing sugars appear.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Labelling with carbon-13 showed how the sugar chain breaks: through endiols and deoxyglucosones into fragments that end as formic, acetic, glycolic and lactic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Several further findings shape what reaches the cup:

- **Intermediates.** Arabinose, erythrose and 1,6-anhydroglucose formed from the sugars and gave the same four acids when roasted themselves.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Polysaccharides.** Arabinogalactan, a cell-wall polysaccharide, also contributes, with relatively more acetic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Oxygen.** Roasting sucrose under argon gave similar acid profiles, so oxygen plays no measurable role.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Amino acids and evaporation.** Glycine lowered the yield of formic acid in model roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a real roaster, formic and acetic acids are partly lost by evaporation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Chlorogenic acids, lactones and bitterness

Chlorogenic acids are esters of hydroxycinnamic acids, such as caffeic and ferulic acid, with quinic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Caffeoylquinic acids are the main group, and 5-caffeoylquinic acid (5-CQA) is the most abundant isomer.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> During roasting they break down into quinic acid and hydroxycinnamic acids, or form lactones.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The losses are large. In a 2009 study, total chlorogenic acids in seven commercial coffees were 34.43–41.64 mg/g green and 2.05–7.07 mg/g roasted.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Total caffeoylquinic acids showed a general correlation with pH.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Chlorogenic acid lactones are among the main contributors to coffee's bitterness, according to two textbook chapters.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Phenylindanes, formed from these lactones, are linked to a lingering, harsh bitterness in dark roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> As chlorogenic acids give way to bitter products, the balance between acidity and bitterness shifts with roast degree.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Development time at constant colour

Roast degree is not the only factor.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2020 study roasted one Colombian coffee four times in a 1 kg drum roaster to the same colour (Agtron 76).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Only the development time after first crack differed: 90, 143, 266 and 390 s.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Acids in the French-press brews were measured by proton NMR.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Formic, citric and malic acids fell as development time grew; 5-CQA fell too, but was about the same in the two longest roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Acetate and lactate stayed within a narrow band, with their lowest values in the longest roast.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Sensory acidity fell from 8.5 to 5.4 across the four roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The authors note that the acids were degraded to about the same extent, so the ratio between them did not change. In their reading, development time cannot be used to highlight one acid.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Measuring acidity

- **pH.** Read with a pH meter on the brew or extract. According to the drip-coffee study's lead author, pH varied more between roast degrees than with brewing.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Titratable acidity.** A fixed volume of brew is titrated with 0.1 mol/L sodium hydroxide to a set end point.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> One study titrated to pH 6.6, about the pH in the mouth, and to pH 8.0, which also covers the weaker organic acids.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A roasting study used pH 8.2 for consistency with earlier drip-coffee work.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Titratable acidity also rises with brew strength, so the brew recipe belongs with the result.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Individual acids.** Chromatography, capillary electrophoresis and NMR give single acids.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The concentration of one acid does not tell how acidic the cup will taste (see Acidity in cupping).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Acids in the brew

According to a brewing textbook chapter, sugars and acids are highly soluble, so under-extracted coffee is proportionally richer in them and leans towards sweet and acidic notes.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

Brewing method shifts the balance. Hot brews of six light roasts held more titratable acid than cold brews of the same coffees, at comparable pH.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The authors suggest that hot brewing extracts more acids that are not deprotonated.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Water changes what reaches the tongue. Alkalinity neutralises part of the extracted acids.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The water entry covers how much.

## Two species: arabica and robusta

The sources do not agree on which species carries more acid. A 2000 paper reports lower pH in arabica brews than in robusta brews.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the authors of a meta-analysis, arabica tends to have lower amounts of organic acids, with a minimum at a medium roast.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In the same analysis, formic acid increased in robusta during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

For chlorogenic acids the picture is similar. A roasting chemistry chapter states that robusta green coffee contains significantly more.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The meta-analysis found the difference not statistically significant over its much larger sample, and similar losses with roasting in both species.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Which acids drive perceived acidity.** No single acid predicts it. In the 2023 conference study only citric acid was clearly detected on its own, and tasters could not identify acids added to coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **pH and titratable acidity versus sourness.** Drip-coffee work favours titratable acidity; a nine-method comparison found neither measure correlated (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Acetic acid in dark roasts.** Earlier analyses and a 2023 study disagree (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Glycolic and phosphoric acids.** No sensory data for glycolic acid was found; phosphoric acid in coffee rests on one conference abstract in the sources consulted.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Peak timing and colour.** The titratable-acidity study did not measure roast colour, so its peak cannot yet be placed on a colour scale.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Titratable acidity always peaked during first crack and fell back by second crack; the profile changed the timing more than the peak.</p>

<p><strong>Alstrup et al., 2020 – Development time and acids.</strong> Roasts one Colombian coffee to the same colour with four development times and measures the brews by NMR and GC–MS. Acids generally declined with longer development, and sensory acidity fell with them.</p>
