## What mucilage is

Mucilage is the innermost part of the fruit flesh – the inner mesocarp. It is a thin, hydrated layer of elongated cells, attached to the parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> After the pulper has removed the skin and the rest of the pulp, this layer stays on the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

It is not evenly thick. Cenicafé measured about 0.4 mm on the flat face of the seed and up to 2.0 mm on the curved side.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It makes up about 22% of the weight of pulped coffee and about 13% of the weight of the whole cherry.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A figure cited in a 2018 study likewise gives about 22% of the "grain wet weight", without saying exactly what the grain includes.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## What it is made of

Mucilage is mostly water. Figures compiled in a 2018 study give 85–91% water and 6.2–7.4% sugars (w/w), with small amounts of protein, non-reducing sugar and pectin.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Cenicafé gives 15% solids, in the form of a water-insoluble colloidal gel.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It also lists cellulose and ash among the components.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The solids are mainly pectic substances and sugars.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> How much of each is disputed: published figures range from a solid fraction that is mostly pectin to one that is mostly sugar.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The comparison is under Contested and in the Reference values table.

## Removing it or keeping it

Mucilage is what separates the processing families. The skin and pulp come off in a pulper; the mucilage does not, because it is bound to the parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

- **Fermentation** – pulped coffee is left in a tank, usually under water, and microbes and enzymes break the mucilage down so it can be washed off.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Enzymes** – added pectinases speed up the breakdown.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In one study, adding pectinase and lactic acid accelerated the breakdown to less than 8 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Machines** – a mechanical demucilager rubs the seeds against each other and against a rotor.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Cenicafé reports that coffee can be demucilaged and cleaned this way with 0.6 L of water per kg of dry parchment coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Alkali** – strong alkali such as caustic soda dissolves mucilage quickly. At the Hawaii Agricultural Experiment Station, a 6% sodium hydroxide solution broke it down in about one minute.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Keeping it** – in the semi-dry method, also called pulped natural, the pulp is removed but there is no fermentation; the mucilage stays on the seed and dries with it.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Honey processing uses the same idea with a chosen amount of mucilage, from about 20% to 80% kept.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The colour names are producer conventions, and the sources that define them do not refer to any standard. A 2016 review gives about 25% mucilage for yellow and about 50% for red, with black usually fully covered, and links the colour to drying as well: yellow dries fast in the sun in about 8 days, red under shade or cloud in about 12, black in about 30.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> A 2023 review instead gives white to black as 20% to 80% of the pulp material left on the seed, with drying over 12 to 30 days.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In a 2025 Arabica study, the treatment that kept 75–80% of the mucilage gave the highest relative content of volatile compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Food for fermentation

Mucilage is the substrate of wet fermentation. Bacteria, yeasts and enzymes turn its pectic compounds and sugars into alcohols and acids – acetic, lactic, propionic and butyric among them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Some of these products do not stay outside: in a trial with Arabica in Ecuador, lactic acid, acetic acid and mannitol formed in the mucilage were also found inside the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

The more mucilage stays on, the more there is to ferment. In a Yunnan trial with Arabica, the water of fermentations with the mucilage left on held far more fructose and glucose than that of mechanically demucilaged coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## A by-product

Mucilage leaves the mill as part of the wastewater, or as a separate stream when it is removed mechanically. Together with the pulp from one kilogram of cherry, it can use up the dissolved oxygen of 7.4 m³ of clean water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Discarded coffee residues can cause environmental problems because of their high biochemical oxygen demand.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

It is also a resource. Cenicafé lists pulp and mucilage as raw materials for compost and for animal protein.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its sugars can be fermented to ethanol: a 2018 study reached 0.46–0.47 g of ethanol per gram of sugar, 90–94% of the theoretical yield.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Pectin chemistry

**Structure.** Pectin is built on galacturonic acid. About a hundred galacturonic acid units linked by α-1,4 bonds form the homogalacturonan chain.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Part of the carboxyl groups carry a methyl ester; the share that does is the degree of methylesterification. Highly methylated pectins are more open to base-catalysed breakdown, while weakly methylated ones hydrolyse faster in strong acid.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Coffee mucilage pectin.** Avallone and colleagues isolated the cell-wall material of mucilage from hand-dissected and commercially pulped beans. Both had a similar make-up: about 30% pectic substances, about 8% cellulose and about 18% other neutral polysaccharides.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The extracted pectins held about 60% uronic acids, were highly methyl-esterified (about 62%) and moderately acetylated (about 5%).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> They were small – about 12–29 kDa – and did not form a gel with sugar under acid conditions.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2023 review confirms the high degree of methylesterification and the roughly 60% galacturonic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**The enzymes.** Pectin is taken apart by three main kinds of enzyme:

- **Pectin methylesterase** removes the methyl ester groups and releases methanol.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Polygalacturonases** split the glycosidic bonds of the chain by hydrolysis, using water.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Most work best at pH 3.5–5.5 and 30–50 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Lyases** split the same bond without water, by β-elimination, leaving an unsaturated product.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Pectin lyases (endo-polymethylgalacturonate lyases) act on esterified pectin and release unsaturated methyl-oligogalacturonates.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Pectin and pectate lyases generally work at alkaline pH, 7.5–10.0.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

The same chemistry explains why alkali works so fast. Highly methylated pectins are especially open to base-catalysed breakdown next to an esterified galacturonic acid unit.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Coffee mucilage pectin is highly methylated.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Which enzymes act in coffee.** *Erwinia dissolvens*, a major pectolytic bacterium of fermenting coffee, was reported in an earlier study, cited by Avallone and colleagues, to produce only an exo-pectate lyase with very limited action on the high-methoxyl coffee pectin.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2023 study found the yeasts *Hanseniaspora uvarum* and *Pichia kudriavzevii* more pectinolytic than the bacteria *Klebsiella pneumoniae* and *Erwinia soli*.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Sugars

Most of the sugar in mucilage is reducing sugar – 63% of the total in a literature figure cited by a 2018 study.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A 2023 review table gives about 30% reducing and 20% non-reducing sugars on a dry-weight basis.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In the fermentation water, sucrose, glucose and fructose first rise and then fall as microbes use them.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Glucose falls throughout most fermentations while ethanol or lactic acid rises near the end.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## How mucilage comes off

Several competing explanations have been proposed for what actually loosens the mucilage; a 2023 review calls the mechanism still unclear and controversial.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

- **Osmotic fracture.** Microscopy of Arabica from Mexico in the 1990s showed the mucilage cell walls apparently intact after fermentation; the pectins were either not split or split to an undetectable degree.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The authors proposed that microbes eating the sugars create an osmotic gradient that breaks the cells where they attach to the parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A review notes that the same group later put the breakdown down mainly to acidification by organic acids.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Enzymes in two phases.** A 2023 study ran seven treatments over 36 hours, suppressing bacteria or yeasts, adding pectinase or lactic acid, or holding pH at 7.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In the first 12 hours, endogenous pectinolytic enzymes partly broke the beans' mucilage down even without microbes. Complete breakdown was reached when yeasts were present throughout; bacteria played no critical role. Adding pectinase and lactic acid accelerated the breakdown to less than 8 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

A field study in Nicaragua tracked seven batches on four farms. The pH started at 5.5–5.7 and fell sharply to about 4.6 as fermentation neared completion.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A review, citing that study, reports that once the fermenting mass reached pH 5, fermentation was complete within about two hours, whatever the starting pH or duration.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In a Yunnan trial, pH fell from about 6.0–6.5 to about 4.0.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Measuring the end point

Traditionally, producers rub the seeds between their fingers: the texture changes from slimy to gritty when the mucilage has gone.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> pH is a simple chemical alternative. The Nicaraguan field study suggests the pH curve could predict when fermentation is complete and help prevent over-fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Risks

- **Over-fermentation** – leaving coffee in the tank after the mucilage has broken down is a known risk; tracking pH is one proposed control.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Water pollution** – mucilage and pulp carry a high oxygen demand into rivers if wastewater is not treated.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Alkali handling** – strong alkali is corrosive and needs careful handling; its user also has to buy it and dose it precisely.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In the Hawaii trials the parchment turned dark yellow, though the green bean looked normal and seed viability was not affected.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Mechanism.** Whether mucilage mainly comes off through enzymatic breakdown of pectin, through acidification, or through osmotic fracture of its cells is still debated.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Composition.** There is no agreed primary analysis of mucilage composition; the published figures disagree strongly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Honey grades.** No standard defines the honey colours. Published definitions disagree on how much mucilage each colour means, and one also ties colour to drying speed and shade.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Avallone et al., 2000 – Polysaccharides of coffee mucilage.</strong> Isolated the cell-wall material from hand-dissected and commercially pulped mucilage and analysed its polysaccharides. The pectins were highly methyl-esterified, of low molecular weight and did not gel with sugar.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 65(8), 1308–1311 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2000.tb10602.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2000.tb10602.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Elhalis et al., 2023 – Yeasts and mucilage degradation.</strong> Ran seven wet-fermentation treatments that selectively suppressed microbes, added pectinase or lactic acid, or held pH neutral. Endogenous enzymes started the breakdown; complete breakdown was reached when yeasts were present, and bacteria played no critical role.</p>
<p class="meta">Yeast 40(9), 425–436 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/yea.3888">doi:10.1002/yea.3888</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Jackels & Jackels, 2005 – Chemical indicators of fermentation.</strong> Measured temperature, pH, glucose, ethanol and lactic acid in seven farm-scale batches in Nicaragua. A sharp pH drop marked the end of mucilage fermentation and could help farmers avoid over-fermentation.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 70(5) · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb09960.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb09960.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Hu et al., 2025 – Mucilage retention in honey processing.</strong> Compared Arabica honey-process treatments that kept different amounts of mucilage. The treatment keeping 75–80% gave the highest relative content of volatile compounds; bacteria were more active at low and fungi at high mucilage levels.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry: X 25, 102251 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fochx.2025.102251">doi:10.1016/j.fochx.2025.102251</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Orrego et al., 2018 – Mucilage to ethanol.</strong> Optimised and scaled up the fermentation of coffee mucilage sugars to ethanol, reaching about 90–94% of the theoretical yield in flasks and 5 L bioreactors.</p>
<p class="meta">Energies 11(4), 786 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/en11040786">doi:10.3390/en11040786</a></p>
</div>
