## What a defect is

A defect is an unwanted particle in green coffee: a defective bean or piece of bean, fruit tissue or foreign matter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Graders find defects by eye, on a sample of the lot.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> A lot is a batch of coffee from one origin and process, traded as a unit. Brazil’s grading regulation defines most defects by how they look. A black bean is opaque black; an insect-damaged bean has one or more holes.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Defects are common. A 2008 Brazilian study states that about 20% of Brazilian coffee production contains defective beans.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In India, the beans rejected after sorting by size and colour, called triage, are about 15–20% of production.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Defective beans add off-flavours and lower overall cup quality.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

Grading systems weight defects differently. The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) splits them into category 1 (primary) and category 2 (secondary) defects.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> One full black bean counts as much as three partial blacks.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> How defects are counted, and which grade a count gives, is explained in green coffee grading standards. This entry is about where each defect comes from.

## Five places defects come from

A defect is the visible end of a problem earlier in the chain. The sources place the causes at five stages:

- **Field.** Insects, drought, poor nutrition and seed formation.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Harvest and wet mill.** Unripe or fallen fruit, delays, over-fermentation and badly set pulpers.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Drying.** Rewetting, too much heat and dirty drying yards.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Dry mill.** Hullers that are badly set, and coffee that is too dry or too wet.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Storage and transport.** Moisture taken up from the air, and pests.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Processing weighs heavily. A 2016 Cenicafé paper estimates that more than 80% of bean defects come from poor processing and drying. They include vinegar, discoloured, soft (*flojo*) and mouldy beans.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Soft beans are put down to a lack of drying.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## Defects that start in the field

**Insect damage.** Insects bore into the fruit while it grows on the tree; the coffee berry borer is the main pest named.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The seed is left with small, round holes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Bored beans ferment faster because of the physical damage.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A Brazilian guide separates clean bored beans from bored beans with fungi in the holes.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Immature beans.** They come from fruit picked before it is ripe.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In the green bean, the silverskin sticks to the seed and the groove on its flat side is closed.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> They make the cup more astringent.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Some immature beans stay pale when roasted; these are called quakers.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Poorly filled and shell beans.** A poorly filled bean has little mass; a Brazilian guide links it to water or nutrient shortage and genetics.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A shell forms when two interlocked seeds separate.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Black and sour beans** can also start on the tree. Water shortage during fruit development (sour) and carbohydrate deficiency from poor farming practice (black) are among the causes named.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Defects made in processing, drying and milling

**Black beans.** Fruit that is overripe or lies on the ground ferments, and dries badly with spells of wetting.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The result makes the cup bitter and disagreeable.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Sour beans.** Brazil defines them as brown, in various shades, from the action of fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> They form when fresh cherry is left in sacks or heaps for many hours, or dried in too thick a layer.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Stinkers.** They look normal or nearly so, but release a putrid odour when cut.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Their taste carries into the beverage and makes it undrinkable.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Over-long fermentation, unclean water and dirty tanks are the usual causes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Nipped and broken beans.** A pulper set too tight nips the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Beans break mostly in the huller, when the coffee is too dry.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Broken beans roast faster than whole ones and tend to char.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Drying faults.** Rewetting during drying lets mould grow.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Too much heat gives reddish “foxy” beans and glassy beans.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Milling leftovers.** Dried cherries (pods), parchment and husk stay in the coffee when the huller is badly set. Stones and sticks come from sweeping the drying yard.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Defects that appear in storage

Dried coffee can take up water again. Above 80% relative humidity, stored coffee starts to absorb water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Mouldy beans give off a musty odour that does not disappear in roasting and passes into the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Warm, damp storage also fades the beans and gives aged, woody or earthy flavours.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Weevils attack coffee in warehouses.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Warehouse practice is covered in green coffee storage.

## Reading a defect for its cause

Some defects carry a clue to the stage that caused them.

- **Bored beans with or without fungi.** The Brazilian guide counts “clean” and “dirty” bored beans apart; dirty ones have fungi in the holes.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **White, naked beans.** Parchment nipped off in the pulper points to a gap set too tight.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **Black with shining silverskin.** A black-green bean is an immature seed damaged by heat.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Whitish spots.** They reveal poor drying, or the start of germination with enzymatic reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Red beans.** Foxy colouring points to over-done artificial drying.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Faded, whitish beans.** Uneven or too-hot drying, or poor storage.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Several strong cup defects leave no mark. The SCA notes that phenolic, potato and mould defects are usually not visible to the naked eye.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Stinkers too look normal or nearly so.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Chemistry of defective beans

**Volatile markers.** In a 2008 Brazilian study of raw defective beans, 2-methylpyrazine and 2-furylmethanol acetate were potential markers of immature beans, and butyrolactone of sour beans.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Black beans from both lots were rich in aldehydes, but no single marker was confirmed.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Metabolite profiles.** A 2017 study split arabica into defective and non-defective fractions. Seventeen compounds, including organic acids, amino acids and reducing sugars, separated green defective from sound beans; 35, dominated by volatile compounds, organic acids, sugars and sugar alcohols, separated them after roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Three roasted-bean markers – 1-methylpyrrole, 5-methyl-2-furfurylfuran and 2-methylfuran – occurred only in the defective fractions.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Physical properties.** Indian triage beans were lower in weight, density and brightness than graded beans, and lower in caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Quakers.** In natural specialty coffees, quakers at Agtron 82.8 or lighter lowered sensory quality from 7 quakers in a cup of 65 beans.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Their colour and sensory character follow from the composition of the immature bean.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The roasting side is covered in roast defects.

## Defects and food safety

Defects matter for safety as well as flavour. The Codex code of practice states that defective beans and husk sometimes contain more ochratoxin A than sound beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In lower-grade coffee, fruits with black and sour defects contained the highest levels.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The code asks producers to remove defects before they enter the food chain, and says sorted-out beans should not be blended back into clean coffee or sold directly to roasters unless sampling and ochratoxin A analysis show them to be acceptable.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The same code ties the main risks to the stages above. Fallen berries should not be collected, above all in humid conditions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Five days or less in the drying yard was enough to prevent ochratoxin A accumulation in the experiments it cites.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Physical damage makes coffee more susceptible to contamination.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> An SCA research programme leaves fungus-damaged beans out of its sensory tests because of the health risk, and analyses them chemically, including for ochratoxin.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The toxin itself is covered in ochratoxin A.

## Defects by process and grade

A 2009 Korean study of green coffees graded NY2 to NY4/5 found that the share of defective beans rose as sensory scores fell.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> No black or sour beans were found in the NY2 grade.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> In a Colombian survey of 162 farm samples, coffee grown below 1,300 m had more bored, vinegar and discoloured beans; bored beans averaged 3.78% of the sample there.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Nipped beans were more frequent in farm-processed samples and in samples dried in mechanical dryers.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Sensory weight of each defect.** An SCA-led programme is setting perception thresholds for category 1 and 2 defects by spiking clean coffee; no results were published in the sources consulted.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **Partial blacks and sours.** No source consulted gives a separate cause for partial and full forms.
- **Withered, shell and floater beans in the cup.** No source consulted measured their cup effect.
- **Amber beans.** Listed as a defect in Colombia<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>. A Colombian extension leaflet appears to link them to soil nutrient problems, but its layout leaves the link unclear<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>; no other source consulted gives a cause.
- **Wet-mill versus dry-mill cuts.** Whether a cut made in the pulper can be told from one made in the huller by the colour of the cut is not described in the sources consulted.
- **How often each stage causes each defect.** The cause map is qualitative; only one survey gave an estimate, for processing and drying faults.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
