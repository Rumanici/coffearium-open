## What soil acidity is

Soil pH measures how acid a soil is. A lower number means a more acid soil. For coffee, the Colombian coffee research centre (Cenicafé) gives a suitable range of pH 5.0–5.5.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Below pH 5.0 the plant grows less, and the same happens above 5.5, according to Cenicafé.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

In acid soils, aluminium ions (Al³⁺) take the place of calcium, magnesium and potassium on the soil particles.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> These three nutrients are called bases. At high concentrations, the exchangeable aluminium is toxic to plants.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Manganese can also reach toxic levels.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Above pH 5.5 the aluminium is neutralised and stops limiting coffee growth.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Acid soils are common in coffee land. In the soil-test records of Antioquia, Colombia, 79% of 69,718 samples had a pH of 5 or less.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How texture, depth and organic matter shape a coffee soil is the subject of Soils for coffee.

## Why coffee soils turn acid

Rain is the first cause. Water moving through the soil washes the bases below the reach of the roots.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Acid cations, mainly aluminium, take their place.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Cenicafé also names the decomposition of organic matter and the oxidation of sulphur.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Nitrogen fertiliser is the second cause. When soil microbes turn ammonium into nitrate (nitrification), acid is released.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Cenicafé calls nitrification one of the most important processes in soil acidification.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Per unit of nitrogen, ammonium sulphate and monoammonium phosphate acidify twice as much as urea or ammonium nitrate.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

In a four-year Colombian trial, 300 kg of nitrogen per hectare and year lowered soil pH by 0.27 units.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How much nitrogen coffee needs is covered in Nutrition and fertilisation.

## What acidity does to coffee roots

Aluminium harms the roots first. In a two-year greenhouse study, coffee plants grew in nutrient solutions with six aluminium levels.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Shoot, root and fruit weight fell progressively as aluminium rose.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Growth was lower even at the lowest level tested.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The roots of these plants became thicker and shorter, with fewer side roots.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Young leaves turned small, pale and curled at the edges.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The plants took up less calcium, magnesium and phosphorus.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Too much manganese gives a different picture. In a sand-culture study, its main symptoms were pale leaf edges and a few dead spots on young leaves.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Lime and gypsum: two different jobs

Lime is ground rock made mainly of calcium carbonate, magnesium carbonate or both.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Dolomitic lime contains both: the one used in a Brazilian trial had 20.1% magnesium oxide.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Liming raises pH and exchangeable calcium and magnesium.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Cenicafé lists among its benefits that it supplies calcium and magnesium and removes aluminium and manganese toxicity.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2008 Cenicafé study calls liming the most recommended practice for acid soils.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

Lime moves slowly. Spread on the surface of two Brazilian coffee plantations (1975–82), it acted mainly in the top 30 cm.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A short trial in a Minas Gerais coffee plantation also found no correction of the deeper layer it sampled.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Gypsum is calcium sulphate. It can neutralise aluminium without changing the pH, according to Cenicafé.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It carries calcium deeper than lime. In a six-year Brazilian field trial with grain crops, gypsum raised calcium at every depth sampled, down to 1 m.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## How much lime

There is no universal dose. Cenicafé leaves it to the soil analysis, and a Brazilian handbook describes three calculation methods.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> One raises the share of bases on the soil particles, called base saturation, to a target.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Coffee trials in Minas Gerais and Paraná took that target from their own state's manual.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

In Cenicafé's trials, 340 kg of agricultural lime per hectare and year raised production by about 15%.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It gives a liming interval of one to two years.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

More is not better. In Brazilian trials, the lowest of three lime rates gave the best yields.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The Deep dive gives the formulas and trial results.

## Aluminium and manganese toxicity

**Aluminium.** The 1982 greenhouse study grew arabica coffee for two years in solutions with 0, 0.074, 0.148, 0.296, 0.444 and 0.888 mmol/dm³ of aluminium.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> All growth measures fell significantly even at 0.074 mmol/dm³.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The first symptom was slower root growth, with thicker roots and fewer laterals per unit of main root.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Root tips stopped developing and turned brown.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Leaf symptoms came only after long exposure. Older leaves showed marginal chlorosis that spread to the centre of the blade.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Visible injury went with leaf aluminium above 300 µg/g.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Aluminium reduced the uptake of calcium, magnesium and phosphorus and raised the uptake of potassium and aluminium.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

A companion study with seedlings held the solution at about pH 4.0 and varied aluminium and ionic strength.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Growth followed the activity of the free Al³⁺ ion most closely. An Al³⁺ activity of 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ went with a 10% reduction of seedling growth.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Only the abstract of this study was consulted.

**Manganese.** A 1981 sand-culture study grew Catuaí Vermelho seedlings at rising manganese supply.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Leaf manganese followed solution manganese closely.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A yield reduction of about 25% went with 20 mg Mn/l in solution, or 2,500–2,800 µg Mn/g in the leaves.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Excess manganese lowered leaf iron and calcium.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Effects of lime on nutrient supply.** Liming does not simply make every nutrient more available. In the Paraná trials, lime raised exchangeable calcium and magnesium and the cation exchange capacity, but lowered exchangeable potassium.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In the leaves it raised calcium and magnesium and lowered manganese, zinc and potassium.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured a full curve of nutrient availability against pH in coffee soils, so no such chart is drawn here.

## Nitrogen fertiliser and acidification

Cenicafé ranks nitrification among the most important acidifying processes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Its note lists the calcium carbonate needed to neutralise the acidity from one unit of nitrogen.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

| Fertiliser | CaCO₃ equivalent per unit of N | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Urea | 3.6 | Cenicafé<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> |
| Ammonium nitrate | 3.6 | Cenicafé<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> |
| Diammonium phosphate (DAP) | 5.4 | Cenicafé<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> |
| Ammonium sulphate | 7.2 | Cenicafé<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> |

At La Trinidad Experimental Station, Tolima, Colombia, four years of 300 kg N per hectare and year were applied as urea and ammonium nitrate.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Soil pH fell by 0.27 units and exchangeable aluminium rose by 0.46 cmolc/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Aluminium saturation rose from 14% to 31%, and the effective cation exchange capacity fell by 25%.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In a separate two-year study in 41 coffee lots, soil pH rose by 0.29 units on average when nitrogen applications (240 kg/ha a year) were stopped.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## How lime requirement is calculated

**Base saturation method (Brazil).** The lime requirement in t/ha is NC = (V2 − V1) × T / PRNT.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> V1 is the base saturation measured in the soil test and V2 the target, both in %.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> T is the soil's cation exchange capacity.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> PRNT is the lime's relative neutralising power, in %.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

The target V2 is set by each state's manual. A coffee trial in Muzambinho, Minas Gerais, took it from the 1999 Minas Gerais recommendations, and a trial with young arabica plants in Paraná from the Paraná manual.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The target values are not given here: the only sources consulted that state them are single Tier B papers.

**Aluminium-based methods.** An Embrapa handbook describes three Brazilian methods: neutralising exchangeable aluminium while raising calcium and magnesium, raising base saturation, and a buffer solution.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The aluminium method multiplies exchangeable aluminium by a factor and adds lime to lift calcium plus magnesium to a set level.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The handbook's factors are written for beans and are not repeated here.

**Colombia.** Cenicafé's 2016 note gives no formula; it leaves dose and source to the soil analysis.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Its survey of soil-test records counts samples with pH ≤ 5 and with exchangeable Al³⁺ ≥ 1 (unit not stated in the table).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In Antioquia, 81% of samples had Al³⁺ ≥ 1; in Valle del Cauca, 10% of 24,834 samples did.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2008 Cenicafé study notes that KCl-extractable aluminium may overstate toxic aluminium in volcanic-ash soils (Andisols).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> It also states that in tropical soils, neutralising aluminium to a non-toxic level, around pH 5.5, is considered sufficient.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

No coffee institute outside Brazil and Colombia was consulted for this entry. Rates for Africa and Asia are therefore not given.

## Lime materials and the calcium–magnesium balance

Liming materials are mainly carbonates of calcium and/or magnesium; oxides, hydroxides and silicates are also used.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Their composition varies. The dolomitic lime of the Paraná trial had 30.6% CaO and 20.1% MgO, with a PRNT of 95%.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The agricultural lime in a Colombian nursery study had 46.7% CaO and 8.1% MgO.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

In that nursery study, five Colombian coffee soils received agricultural lime or Thomas slag in bags.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Both raised pH and calcium in every soil and brought exchangeable aluminium below 1 cmol(+)/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> High lime doses lowered magnesium in several soils, which the authors relate to competition between calcium and magnesium.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

In the Paraná trials, 10 t/ha of dolomitic lime raised the Ca:K ratio of the topsoil to 23.6:1 and 30.0:1 at the two sites in the first year sampled.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The 2.5 t/ha rate gave 14.0:1 at both. The authors give about 13:1 as the target ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Field responses to lime

**Paraná, Brazil, 1975–82.** Dolomitic lime was spread once, without incorporation, on adult plantations at Londrina (Bourbon Amarelo) and Florestópolis (Mundo Novo).<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Rates were 0, 2.5, 5 and 10 t/ha.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Lime raised pH, cation exchange capacity, calcium and magnesium and lowered aluminium, mainly in the top 30 cm.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Frost meant there was no harvest at Londrina in 1976 or at Florestópolis in 1982.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> At 2.5 t/ha the pH of that layer reached 5.0–5.6; at 10 t/ha it rose above 6.0.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

<figcaption>Total yield by lime rate at two sites in Paraná, Brazil: four harvests at Londrina (1975, 1977–79), two at Florestópolis (1980–81). The lowest lime rate, 2.5 t/ha, yielded most. Data: Chaves et al.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup></figcaption>
</figure>

At Londrina, the 2.5 t/ha plots gave 3,993 kg/ha of processed coffee over four harvests, against 2,719 kg/ha without lime.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The 5 and 10 t/ha plots gave 2,879 and 2,936 kg/ha.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> That is 47% more with the lowest rate than without lime (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The authors link the best yields to lower aluminium and better cation ratios, and the lower yields at 10 t/ha to leaf potassium and zinc falling below critical levels.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Colombia.** At the El Rosario (Antioquia) and San Antonio (Santander) experimental stations, 340 kg/ha a year of agricultural lime raised production by about 15% at both sites.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Cenicafé reports that hydrated lime at up to 2 t/ha during establishment corrected acidity and improved growth.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> On a stony soil in Andes, Antioquia, 60 g of dolomitic lime per planting hole was enough to give thicker stems and longer branches.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> For acid soils, Cenicafé prefers large planting holes of 30 × 30 × 30 cm.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Kodagu, India.** A 2016 study analysed a 2008 survey of 112 robusta estates at 800–1,000 m.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Soil pH ranged from 4.5 to 6.3, with a mean of 5.5.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Of the farmers, 63% added lime to reduce acidity and aluminium toxicity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Estates that limed harvested 20% more berries per infructescence (fruit cluster).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Liming was also linked with 1.8 more flowers per inflorescence (5.4%) and less berry loss between June and September.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The study recorded liming only as yes or no, without rates or soil changes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Young plants.** In a 2020 Paraná trial, IPR 107 arabica plants grew in soil columns with lime and gypsum from none to above the calculated requirement.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Neither lime nor gypsum changed any measured growth trait.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Gypsum and the subsoil

**How it moves.** A trial at Jaguariaíva, Paraná, ran from 2002 to 2008 under no-till grain crops on a sandy loam Latosol.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It combined 0–12 t/ha of gypsum with 0 or 3.42 t/ha of lime.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Lime raised calcium only in the top 10 cm after 36 months and to 20 cm after 72 months.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It raised the pH (CaCl₂) of the top 10 cm from 4.7 to 5.4 at 36 months.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Gypsum raised calcium at every depth in proportion to the dose.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It did not change subsoil aluminium concentration. Aluminium saturation at 40–60 cm still fell from 87% to just above 40% after 36 months and below 20% after 72 months.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Gypsum raised pH below 60 cm by only 0.17 units at 12 t/ha.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Without water stress, high gypsum doses lowered soybean yield, which the authors attribute to induced magnesium deficiency.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Gypsum in coffee.** In the Brazilian Cerrado, a coffee study reports, farmers tend to apply gypsum above the recommended rate to improve the root environment in the deep subsoil and ease water stress.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> According to the Minas Gerais recommendations, the gypsum rate depends on soil texture, residual phosphorus or aluminium, calcium and magnesium.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

A trial at São Roque de Minas, Minas Gerais, tested 0, 3.5, 7, 14 and 56 t/ha of gypsum on a very clayey red Latosol.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Catucaí arabica had been planted in 2008 after 8 t/ha of dolomitic lime and 2 t/ha of gypsum.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

<figure>
<div data-figure="soil-acidity-and-liming-gypsum-trial-yields"></div>
<figcaption>Cumulative yield of four harvests (2011–2014) by gypsum rate, São Roque de Minas, Minas Gerais, Brazil. Sack size is not stated in the paper. Data: Cogo et al.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup></figcaption>
</figure>

The 14 t/ha rate gave the highest cumulative yield, 300 sacks/ha against 238 without gypsum.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In the first year, gypsum practically doubled yield, but this effect was not maintained in later years.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The 56 t/ha rate gave 255 sacks/ha, less than 14 t/ha but more than no gypsum; the authors call it excessive.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> It also reduced the diversity of mycorrhizal fungi, while 7 t/ha increased it.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured coffee root depth after gypsum.

## Open questions

- **Lime targets outside Brazil and Colombia.** No liming recommendation from African or Asian coffee research institutes was consulted for this entry.
- **Gypsum and coffee roots.** The aim of deeper roots is stated for Cerrado coffee, but no study consulted here measured root depth or drought response after gypsum.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Yield response at the pH optimum.** The Colombian range of pH 5.0–5.5 comes from a technical note; no study consulted here gives a yield curve against soil pH for coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Which aluminium measure predicts harm.** Solution studies point to Al³⁺ activity, while field practice uses KCl-extractable aluminium, which may overstate toxic aluminium in Andisols.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

<p><strong>Chaves, Pavan and Igue, 1984 – Response of coffee to lime.</strong> Spread 0–10 t/ha of dolomitic lime once on adult plantations at two sites in Paraná, Brazil, between 1975 and 1982. <p><strong>Pavan and Bingham, 1982 – Aluminium toxicity in coffee.</strong> Grew arabica coffee for two years in nutrient solutions with six aluminium levels. Every growth measure fell even at the lowest level; roots thickened and lost laterals, and calcium, magnesium and phosphorus uptake fell.</p>
