## What a landrace is

A landrace is a local variety tied to the place where it grows.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> According to a 2020 book chapter, Ethiopian coffee landraces generally show adaptation to a specific location.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A released variety, by contrast, comes out of a breeding programme and is released to growers.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Practitioners note that Ethiopian coffee is often labelled “heirloom”; in 2018 a specialty roaster called the word vague and poorly defined.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Genetic work shows why one label says little. In a 2022 study, Ethiopia’s cultivated trees came from local landraces, released varieties or both, and their genetic diversity was large and continuous.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Ethiopia, the home of arabica’s diversity

*Coffea arabica* is native to the forests of south-western Ethiopia, and Ethiopia is the centre of its diversity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Commercial varieties grown elsewhere show little diversity, and genomes separate the ancestors of cultivars from wild arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> How a few plants left Ethiopia is told in [Arabica varieties and their lines](/entries/arabica-varieties/).

The species itself is narrow. It mainly fertilises itself, and its genetic variation is extremely low in both wild and cultivated plants.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Within that narrow species, Ethiopian accessions, the samples kept in collections, are more diverse than commercial varieties.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Four ways coffee is grown in Ethiopia

A 2008 review describes four growing systems: forest coffee, semi-forest coffee, garden coffee and plantation coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Most Ethiopian coffee grows under trees. A 2017 study reports that at least 80% of Ethiopia’s coffee comes from forests, forest-like habitats or farms with shade cover.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> DNA markers find the highest diversity in the forest coffees of the south-western highlands.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same review finds landrace naming particularly rich in garden coffee systems, such as Harerge in eastern Ethiopia.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Selections and released varieties

Ethiopia breeds its own varieties from this material. As of a 2021 review, 41 coffee varieties had been released.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The main breeding aims are yield, resistance or tolerance to diseases, and cup quality.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A 2011 review covering Ethiopia and Kenya describes decades of breeding against three fungal diseases: coffee leaf rust, coffee berry disease and coffee wilt disease.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

According to a 2020 book chapter, breeders in southern Ethiopia select within local landraces for each growing region, because landraces are adapted to their place.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In Ethiopia, selections against coffee wilt disease come from local landraces.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Ethiopian coffee outside Ethiopia

Collections outside Ethiopia hold landraces and wild accessions gathered in the 1960s.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Some of this material reached farms. According to a 2017 textbook, Geisha and Rume Sudan are wild or semi-wild selections from the Ethiopian and Sudan regions. They suit niche markets because they yield little.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Geisha’s journey is told in [Arabica varieties and their lines](/entries/arabica-varieties/).

## Wild and cultivated: what the genomes show

Arabica genomes separate wild accessions from the ancestors of cultivars. A 2024 genome study dates that split to about 30,500 years ago, followed by a period of gene flow between the two groups.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A 2020 study found extremely low genetic variation in both wild and cultivated germplasm and traced it to a single polyploidisation event; it links predominant self-fertilisation to high levels of inbreeding.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Marker studies still find clear structure within Ethiopia. A 2019 microsatellite (SSR) study of Ethiopian accessions and Brazilian cultivars found two genetic groups: one of Ethiopian accessions, one mainly of commercial cultivars.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Ethiopian accessions were more diverse, and the 19 accessions from west of the Great Rift Valley carried 34 private alleles, variants found in no other group.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

A 2022 study genotyped 555 samples, 356 of them from cultivated trees in Ethiopia and 186 from Yemen.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In Yemen it found an “Ethiopian Legacy” cluster beside the Typica/Bourbon and “New-Yemen” clusters, and it links that legacy cluster to southern Ethiopia.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The diversity among Ethiopian cultivated trees was large and continuous.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Collections and genebanks

Most studies of arabica diversity in Ethiopia rest on accessions from two surveys of the 1960s, one by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and one by ORSTOM.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The FAO Coffee Mission collected in 1964–1965.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> All of the ORSTOM survey and 90% of the FAO survey took place in the south-west.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> As of 2022 the surveys were almost 60 years old, and the 2022 study sees a need for a more up-to-date one.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Ethiopian accessions held in Brazil come from open-pollinated seed of the original collection at the Tropical Agricultural Research and Higher Education Center (CATIE) in Costa Rica.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

In Ethiopia, the field genebank of the Jimma Agricultural Research Center (JARC) held 4,780 accessions spread over 10 research stations, as reported in 2008.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The genebank of the Institute of Biodiversity Conservation at Choche (Limu) held 5,196 accessions, according to the same review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Breeding in Ethiopia

A 2011 review of fungal diseases in Ethiopia’s coffee forests, which also reports breeding progress in Kenya, describes about 40 years of breeding against leaf rust, coffee berry disease and coffee wilt disease; its abstract does not tie that span to one country.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Breeding for berry disease resistance has used major genes; in Ethiopia, breeders select wilt-tolerant accessions from local landraces.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The disease and its history are covered in [Coffee berry disease](/entries/coffee-berry-disease/).

According to a 2021 review, Ethiopian programmes use selection, crosses within *C. arabica*, germplasm enhancement and hybrids from selected parents.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> According to a 2020 book chapter from southern Ethiopia, the selections 744 and 75227 are released berry-disease-resistant cultivars and serve as standard checks in trials.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The same chapter describes a strategy of breeding from local landraces for each coffee-growing region, because landraces show specific location adaptation.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Access and benefit sharing

The Nagoya Protocol, in force since October 2014, governs access to genetic resources and the fair sharing of benefits from their use.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Working under it requires Prior Informed Consent (PIC).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Material collected before the 1992 cut-off of the Convention on Biological Diversity is considered freely accessible and, in theory, not covered by the Protocol.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

The 2024 paper counts the Ethiopian landraces and wild accessions collected in the 1960s, now in field collections abroad, among such freely accessible material.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The same paper names Ethiopia as one of the countries most active in shaping the Protocol. It uses Geisha to ask whether the Ethiopian communities that kept such resources would have been acknowledged or rewarded under today’s rules.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry states Ethiopia’s current rules on exporting coffee seed or plants.

## Conservation and climate

Coffee genetic resources in Ethiopia are being lost quickly, according to a 2008 review that also covers conservation in genebanks and in the forests.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to a 2017 textbook, the forests holding the ancestral plants are shrinking, and local cultivated varieties are interbreeding with the wild plants.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

Climate adds pressure. A 2017 study projected that 39–59% of Ethiopia’s current growing area could become unsuitable for coffee this century without major interventions.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Relocating coffee, together with forest conservation, could increase the suitable area at least fourfold.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The study calls Ethiopia’s wild coffee forests the main storehouse of *C. arabica*.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The Red List status of wild arabica is covered in [Coffea arabica](/entries/coffea-arabica/).

## Open questions

- **Sampling of the 1960s collections.** Most diversity studies rest on surveys made almost entirely in the south-west; a 2022 study of cultivated trees from the whole country calls for a new survey.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **How much of Ethiopia’s diversity is in genebanks.** The 2008 review counts accessions but also reports fast genetic erosion in the field.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Benefit sharing for older material.** Accessions collected before 1992 are considered freely accessible under the Nagoya Protocol, and how the communities that kept them would be acknowledged remains open.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
