## What planting density means

Planting density is the number of coffee plants on one hectare (10,000 m²). It follows from the spacing: divide 10,000 by the area each plant gets, in square metres.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Rows 3 m apart with plants 1 m apart in the row give about 3,333 plants per hectare; a 3 × 3 m grid gives about 1,111 (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

The yield of a field is the number of plants multiplied by the yield of each plant.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Closer planting adds plants but gives each one less light and space. Choosing a density means finding the point where the gain in plants still outweighs the loss per plant.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Densities have risen over time. A 2007 review states that earlier arabica plantations had fewer than 2,000 trees per hectare.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Compact, disease-resistant cultivars then allowed closer spacing, and the review describes closer spacing as adopted worldwide (as of 2007) where tractor-based field work is not needed or not possible.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## What sets the density

**Plant size.** Tall varieties need more room than compact ones. The variety catalogue of World Coffee Research (WCR) places compact Caturra in a higher planting-density class than tall Bourbon or SL28.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> According to WCR, Caturra helped intensify coffee growing through higher-density planting, often in full sun.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The 2007 review cites an optimum of about 5,000 trees per hectare for short cultivars such as Caturra and Catimor.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Number of stems.** A plant can be trained to one upright stem or to several. WCR notes that in Central America trees are typically pruned to one main stem, in Africa to several.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> According to WCR, planting densities are therefore much lower in Africa, but each tree fruits relatively more.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Species.** In Brazil, conilon (a type of *C. canephora*) is grown with several stems per plant.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2007 review cites evidence that conilon density should not exceed 4,000 plants per hectare.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For short-stature arabica cultivars it cites an optimum of about 5,000.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Shade and site.** Shade trees take space and light. In a Colombian trial with shade trees, the densest coffee planting gave the highest yield at every shade-tree spacing tested.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Shade systems are covered in Shade and agroforestry.

- **Brazil (arabica).** In a trial with the cultivar IAPAR 59 by researchers in Paraná (the paper does not name the site), yield per hectare peaked at about 15,000 plants per hectare.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The authors call such very dense plantings *superadensado* in Portuguese.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Layouts on the ground

Plants are set out in a grid of rows. The two numbers that define it are the distance between rows and the distance between plants in a row.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> A square grid uses the same distance both ways, such as 2 × 2 m or 1 × 1 m in Colombian trials.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

A hedgerow is a rectangular layout with plants close together in the row and wider alleys between rows. Conilon rows can be up to 3.5 m apart with plants 0.5 m apart, according to earlier work cited in a 2021 Brazilian paper.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> For conilon grown without machines, spacings as close as 2 × 1 m can be used.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

On slopes, Cenicafé advises planting along the contour, across the slope, with minimal soil disturbance.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> It also recommends live barriers of grasses planted across the hillside against erosion.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Soil conservation is covered in Soils for coffee.

## Stems per plant and plants per site

The stem count changes what “density” means. For multi-stemmed conilon, the 2007 review counts upright stems per hectare rather than plants.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Rondônia, Brazil, robusta yield per plant rose with up to four stems per plant.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> More than four is not advised, because heavily loaded stems tip over.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

In arabica, a second stem can cost early yield. In Kenya, two-stem Ruiru 11 yielded less in its first two crops than single-stem plants at all three sites.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The two-stem plants caught up later, faster in the wetter zone than in the drier one.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

A planting site can also hold two seedlings. In Colombia, two plants per site at 1.42 × 1.42 m gave yields similar to one plant per site at 1 × 1 m.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Cenicafé notes the lower cost of setting up the plantation.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Young plants go into the field from the nursery. Cenicafé advises planting them out outside periods of severe drought, with the root collar at soil level.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Raising them is covered in Germination and seedlings.

## Yield per plant and yield per hectare

The 2007 review splits yield into trees per hectare times yield per tree.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Production per hectare rises with density in the first years, while yield per tree usually falls.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At two Kenyan locations it cites, the fall in yield per tree came mostly from fewer fruits per node; at another site, from fewer fruiting nodes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Field trials show both sides of this trade-off:

- **Robusta, Rondônia, Brazil (2013–2018).** At 3 m between rows, 1,666 and 3,333 plants per hectare (with four stems each) yielded 2.58 and 1.89 kg per plant.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Per hectare, they gave 71.84 and 105.76 bags of 60 kg, averaged over three harvests.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> That is about 4,310 and 6,346 kg of processed coffee per hectare (our calculation, 60 kg per bag).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Arabica IAPAR 59, Brazil.** In the second crop year, yield per hectare of depulped beans peaked at 4,528 kg at about 15,139 plants per hectare.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Yield per plant peaked at 413 g at about 10,383 plants per hectare.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Densities of 3,333–20,000 plants per hectare were tested.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Castillo, Santander, Colombia.** Over six harvests, 10,000 plants per hectare gave 32,963 kg of dry parchment coffee per hectare under the best shade spacing; 2,500 plants gave 12,170.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> That is about 5,494 and 2,028 kg per harvest (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Robusta, Mpigi, Uganda.** Yields were estimated from berry counts, not harvested. Per tree, wide spacing gave more clean coffee: 2.31 kg at 3 × 3 m against 1.75 kg at 3 × 1 m.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The paper estimates 5.82 t per hectare at 3 × 1 m and 4.80 t at 3 × 3 m.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Its own method, yield per tree times plant population, gives about 2.6 t at 3 × 3 m (our calculation), so the per-hectare comparison is uncertain.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

The gain per hectare flattens at high density. In Kenya, Ruiru 11 yields rose more slowly above 4,000 trees per hectare, especially in the drier zone.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In the IAPAR 59 trial, yield per hectare followed a curve with its maximum near 15,000 plants per hectare.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Self-shading and the canopy

Close planting shades the plants mutually. The 2007 review states that the lower fruit-bearing of closely spaced trees may be attributed to shading.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Lower light inside the canopy moves the fruiting zone upwards, and each fruit then needs more leaf area.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How the fruiting zone forms is described in Branching and fruiting wood.

The review lists benefits as well. Mutual shading keeps soil temperatures and evaporative demand low, and roots grow deeper in dense plantings.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Shading reduces excessive flower formation, overbearing and biennial production.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Except at extreme densities, internal plant water tensions are unlikely to rise.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Leaf area sets a limit. The review reports an apparent optimal leaf area index (leaf area per ground area) of about 7–8 for several arabica cultivars.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The best density depends on cultivar, water, nutrients and evaporative demand, according to the review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Density over the life of a plantation

Dense plantings yield early and age fast. In a 19-year trial at Campinas, Brazil, high densities gave high yields in the first harvests.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Their yields then fell markedly after seven harvests, most of all in Caturra, while Mundo Novo kept its potential.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Cenicafé ties the renewal age to density. It gives an optimum renewal age of 5 years at 10,000 plants per hectare, 6 years at 5,000 and 7 years at 2,500.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Dense plantings should not be topped, so they need varieties with high yield per plant.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> After stumping, it advises up to three new shoots per stump at 2,500 plants per hectare, two at 5,000 and one at 10,000.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Pruning and stumping cycles are covered in Pruning and renovation.

For conilon the 2007 review counts stems. The optimum seems to be about 14,000 orthotropic stems per hectare in mildly managed plantations.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In intensively managed systems it falls to 10,000–12,000.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Pests, diseases and field work

Evidence on pests and diseases at high density is thin and mixed. The 2007 review states that high-density systems may increase pest and pathogen attack and make cultural practices more difficult.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

One field comparison measured incidence. On Bunjako Island, Uganda, robusta at 3 × 1 m and 3 × 3 m showed no significant difference in leaf rust.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Red blister disease (*Cercospora coffeicola*) was less frequent under close spacing.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Black coffee twig borer and leaf-eating beetles were more frequent under close spacing.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Coffee berry borer, leaf miners and canopy mealybugs were more frequent under wide spacing.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Close spacing was one field of trees about 3 years old; wide spacing was two fields, one stumped to about 3 years old and one about 8 years old, partly 3 km further inland. Field and age differences therefore cannot be ruled out.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured leaf rust at different densities of arabica. The diseases themselves are covered in Coffee leaf rust and Coffee berry borer.

## Open questions

- **Disease at high density in arabica.** No study consulted for this entry measured leaf rust or berry disease across planting densities of arabica.
- **Quality.** In the IAPAR 59 trial, the authors judged about 10,000 plants per hectare best for physiological and health quality of the seed, below the density with the highest yield.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Whether density changes cup quality was not measured in the sources consulted here.
- **Long-term yield.** The density trials consulted here cover two to six harvests, except one.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The 19-year Campinas trial suggests early gains can fade.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Njoroge & Kimemia, 1994 – Tree training and plant density in Ruiru 11.</strong> Tested one- and two-stem training at 1,600–4,800 trees per hectare at three Kenyan sites. Two-stem trees yielded less in the first two crops; yields rose with density up to 4,000 trees per hectare and more slowly above it.</p>
<p class="meta">Experimental Agriculture 30(1), 89–94 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1017/S0014479700023887">doi:10.1017/S0014479700023887</a></p>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Espindula et al., 2021 – Robusta in different spatial arrangements.</strong> Two trials in Rondônia, Brazil, tested one to six stems per plant and 1,666–3,333 plants per hectare. Four stems per plant gave the most; the densest planting lowered yield per plant but raised yield per hectare.</p>
