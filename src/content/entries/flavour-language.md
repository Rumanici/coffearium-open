## Why coffee needs shared words

Coffee is bought, sold and blended largely on how it tastes. The trade therefore needs words that mean the same thing to a producer, a buyer and a roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> “Flavour notes” printed on many coffee packages carry the same descriptions on to consumers.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Everyday words are not precise enough for this. One taster’s “fruity” may be another’s “fermented”. Sensory science solves the problem with a lexicon: a list of attributes, each with a written definition and a physical reference.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A reference is a sample that shows what the word means, such as a solution or a food smelled from a covered cup.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> WCR presents its lexicon as a universal language of coffee’s sensory qualities.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Since 2024, coffee’s flavour words have come in three linked layers. The World Coffee Research Sensory Lexicon defines the words. The Coffee Taster’s Flavor Wheel arranges them. The cupping form of the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) turns the wheel’s main groups into tick boxes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## The World Coffee Research Sensory Lexicon

The Sensory Lexicon of World Coffee Research (WCR) was developed at the Sensory Analysis Center of Kansas State University.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A second laboratory, at Texas A&M University, validated it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It was built from 105 coffees evaluated by a trained panel and was published in 2016.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> It names 110 attributes of aroma, flavour and texture.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Each entry gives a definition and one or more references. Most references also carry an intensity score, so a taster can judge how strong an attribute is, not only whether it is there.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A second version added new, globally available references for a number of attributes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> WCR offers the lexicon free for personal-use copies.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> We describe it here and link to it, but do not reproduce its definitions.

The lexicon was written for trained sensory panels. According to the SCA, wheel users without that training can still use its definitions and references.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Its authors call it a “living” lexicon, to be updated as new attributes appear; defects were not studied in the original work.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## The Coffee Taster’s Flavor Wheel

The first Coffee Taster’s Flavor Wheel was published by the Specialty Coffee Association of America more than 20 years before its 2016 redesign.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The 2016 wheel, made with WCR, is built on the Sensory Lexicon.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Its layout came from a study, not from a committee. Seventy-two experts sorted the lexicon’s attributes into groups online, without tasting anything.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A statistical method then joined attributes that were often sorted together into clusters and levels. A second method placed the clusters around the wheel.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The result reads from the centre outwards. General words such as “fruity” sit in the inner ring; more specific ones such as a single fruit sit at the rim.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the SCA, colours help the eye find a word. A gap between neighbouring segments means the experts saw them as less closely related.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The wheel is a copyrighted SCA and WCR product; we describe it and do not reproduce it.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## From the wheel to the cupping form

In 2024 the SCA adopted a descriptive assessment as part of its cupping system, the Coffee Value Assessment (CVA). Its form records what a coffee tastes like, separately from how much the taster likes it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The taster rates the total intensity of each section on a 15-point scale and ticks descriptors on check-all-that-apply (CATA) lists.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The tick boxes are taken from the wheel’s inner and middle rings. Each box also stands for the more specific words in the outer ring around it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The fragrance-and-aroma list has the nine inner categories plus several middle-ring ones, such as berry, dried fruit and citrus fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> If a coffee tastes clearly of blueberry, the taster ticks “berry” and “fruity” and writes “blueberry” in the notes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Experts, consumers and words

Experts and consumers use flavour words differently. Studies of wine tasters, reviewed in a 2017 textbook chapter, found experts more accurate and consistent than novices in identifying odours. Their advantage lay mainly in using specific words for particular notes.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The same chapter notes that labels can shift how a smell is perceived.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

Coffee studies point the same way. In a Brazilian survey of 1,005 respondents, chocolate was the flavour consumers most expected in specialty coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> When 101 consumers tasted one coffee without information, they ticked more caramel; told about the coffee, they ticked more citrus.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In a 2026 study with 180 specialty coffee consumers, the word “fermentation” on a label made the same coffee taste more acidic and less liked.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Lexicons in sensory science

In sensory science, a descriptive profile has a qualitative step, choosing the descriptors, and a quantitative step, rating their intensity.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> ISO 11035 describes a method for identifying and selecting such descriptors; ISO 5492 defines the general vocabulary of the field.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> A panel can take its attributes from an existing vocabulary, such as the WCR lexicon or the flavour wheel, or build one in brainstorming sessions.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

Lexicons are made for a purpose and a population. A trained-panel lexicon guides experts to describe every product in a category. Other lists mark which terms consumers can use.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> One attribute pool of 74 brewed-coffee terms was built for both trained panels and consumers; 33 terms were verified with consumers as aroma descriptors, and 28 were flavour descriptors.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A Japanese study listed 127 descriptors. Thirty were judged usable in consumer studies and 60 suited to untrained coffee professionals.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Cupping and descriptive analysis use words differently. Cuppers are industry experts who judge quality and add notes from coffee wheels. Trained panels use a lexicon to refine and quantify attributes.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The SCA treats intensity and description as objective measurements, trainable with references such as the WCR lexicon, and value judgements as a separate test.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## References, calibration and alignment

A lexicon reference fixes the meaning of a word; an intensity score on the reference fixes a point on the scale.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Many lexicon references, however, are products sold mainly in US grocery chains, and WCR itself writes that the lexicon is “not truly global”.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The CVA takes a looser approach for its tick boxes. It lists “category examples” for each box, to be smelled for training and alignment.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Tasters need not all use the same reference, only a representative example of each category; for finer work the SCA recommends the lexicon references or similar local ones.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The SCA calls its example list a living document.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In sensory science, calibration means training panelists to use a defined set of references in one specific test.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The SCA therefore prefers “alignment” for what cupping rooms call calibration.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> For better group performance in descriptive work, the SCA recommends that tasters align their use of intensity scales and descriptors.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Across radically different cultures and markets, the SCA advises agreeing on the description and “agreeing to disagree” on preference.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A high-performing descriptive cupper, in its view, is consistent with their own intensity ratings and accurate in their use of descriptors.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## How well shared words work

**Within a group.** In a study of 13 Colombian coffees, a trained panel developed 59 terms to profile them.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Cuppers given the same coffees were reasonably consistent in grading quality but highly variable in describing flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The authors concluded that cupping gives different information and cannot replace a trained descriptive panel for profiling.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The SCA, by contrast, reports pilot tests in which professional cuppers produced reliable descriptive results after a few hours of training.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Across countries.** In a 2025 study, 46 experts in France, India, Italy, the Republic of Korea and the USA described the same espressos with a questionnaire built from the WCR lexicon.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> They received the lexicon and guidelines but no separate training.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Individual experts were largely consistent across three replications, and two samples were rated similarly in all five countries. The terminology, however, differed between countries, and Korean experts used notably distinct terms.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The authors call for training in the coffee lexicon.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Between experts and consumers.** A 2017 textbook chapter describes expert wording as a bridge that helps consumers anticipate a coffee, but also notes that words can mislead perception.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> It names terms that needed clearer definition, even among experts: quality, sweet perception, intensity or strength, fine acidity and complexity.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The SCA presents the CVA as a common language along the chain and towards consumers, using standardised descriptors.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Culture and translation

Flavour words depend on what people know and eat. In a study of hibiscus tea, lexicons were built in French, Portuguese and English. Training, food culture and familiarity with the product all shaped the words that panelists in four countries used.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry compared the coffee lexicon itself across languages.

For coffee, the SCA warns twice about words that do not travel. Some common acidity descriptors, it notes, do not translate to other languages.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> For affective notes, it singles out “funky” as a vague word untranslatable outside English, valued by some tasters and avoided by others.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Fruit similes cause a related problem: “green apple” may describe an acidity, not an aroma, and suggests malic acid that may not be there.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Coffee vocabularies have also been built outside English. Besides the Japanese list and the attribute pool above, researchers have developed coffee emotion lexicons for China (53 terms) and Korea (29 terms).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> WCR provides introductions to its lexicon in Japanese and Chinese.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A University of Aberdeen project with the State University of Campinas, funded for 2024–2025, compares the sensory language and references of professional Brazilian and British coffee tasters.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

## How our flavour wheel tool is built

The tool is our own design and does not copy the SCA/WCR wheel graphic. Its attributes and their grouping into nine families follow the 2016 wheel study.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The nine families are the inner-ring categories that also head the CVA tick list.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

For each attribute, the tool shows the CVA box under which it is ticked, following the form in SCA 103-2024.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The five main tastes carry the reference solutions of that standard, and the mouthfeel terms are those of its mouthfeel lexicon.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Definitions and references are not repeated: each word links to the WCR Sensory Lexicon.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Sweetness.** According to the SCA (June 2024), research is under way to establish whether sweetness in coffee is a taste or a cross-modal effect of sweet-smelling compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Acidity words.** There are no sensory references for terms such as “juicy” or “bright” acidity, and little research supports specific acidity descriptors.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Defects.** Defect attributes were not studied when the lexicon was developed.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Global references.** Many lexicon references are hard to obtain outside the USA.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Cross-cultural meaning.** Experts in different countries use different terms even with the same lexicon<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>; how far training closes the gap is not established.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Spencer et al., 2016 – Designing the flavour wheel.</strong> Seventy-two coffee experts sorted the attributes of the WCR Sensory Lexicon online without tasting. Hierarchical clustering of their sorts set the groups and levels, and multidimensional scaling placed them around the new Coffee Taster’s Flavor Wheel.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 81(12), S2997–S3005 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/1750-3841.13555">doi:10.1111/1750-3841.13555</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Di Donfrancesco et al., 2014 – Cupping versus descriptive analysis.</strong> Thirteen Colombian coffees were profiled by a trained descriptive panel and assessed by cuppers. The cuppers’ flavour notes varied widely, and the authors concluded that cupping cannot replace descriptive analysis for profiling coffee.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Sensory Studies 29, 301–311 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/joss.12104">doi:10.1111/joss.12104</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Choi et al., 2025 – One lexicon, five countries.</strong> Forty-six experts in France, India, Italy, Korea and the USA described espressos three times with a questionnaire based on the WCR lexicon. Individuals were mostly consistent, but terminology differed between countries, which the authors read as a need for lexicon training.</p>
<p class="meta">Foods 14(4), 593 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/foods14040593">doi:10.3390/foods14040593</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bressani et al., 2021 – What consumers expect and perceive.</strong> A survey of 1,005 Brazilian respondents found chocolate the most expected flavour in specialty coffee. In a CATA test with 101 consumers, information about the coffee shifted their choice of descriptors from caramel towards citrus.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Science and Technology 41(Suppl. 2), 667–675 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1590/fst.30720">doi:10.1590/fst.30720</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Carvalho et al., 2026 – Words on the label.</strong> One hundred and eighty specialty coffee consumers tasted the same coffee under three labels. “Fermentation” made it expected and perceived as more acidic and less liked; “carbonic maceration” raised curiosity without lowering acceptance.</p>
<p class="meta">Foods 15(8), 1287 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/foods15081287">doi:10.3390/foods15081287</a></p>
</div>
