## A mix of sizes, not one size

A grinder does not cut coffee into identical pieces. It breaks the beans into many particles of different sizes and shapes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> How much of the coffee falls into each size class is called the particle size distribution, or PSD.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

A grind setting therefore sets a whole distribution, not one size. Two grinds with the same median size can still differ. According to a 2024 study, different grinding technologies can give different shares of fines at the same median.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The median is the size below which half of the coffee lies.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Size matters for two reasons that pull in opposite directions. Small particles expose more surface and let soluble compounds out faster.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> But very small particles can block the paths between larger ones, slow the water or clog the filter.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Fines and boulders: two peaks

Ground coffee usually has a bimodal distribution: plotted as a curve, it shows two peaks instead of one.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> One peak lies near the average particle size. The other lies among the very small particles, the fines.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Fines.** Particles below 100 µm (a tenth of a millimetre) are often called fines.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The cut-off is a convention and changes from case to case.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a 2023 study, the fines peak always sat at about 25–50 µm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its authors explain this by the bean itself: the fines are fragments of cells, and coffee cells are about that size.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Where fines come from.** According to a textbook chapter on grinding, the peak of fines comes from the cellular structure of the roasted bean, not only from the grinder.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Whenever a bean or a particle cracks, very fine pieces flake off, and this repeats with every further break.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to the chapter, fines are unavoidable in most applications.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Boulders.** Very coarse particles are sometimes called boulders.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In the textbook’s account, fines tend to over-extract and boulders to under-extract, so a wide distribution brings both into the same cup.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## How grind setting changes the distribution

Turning the grinder coarser moves the main peak to larger sizes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The fines peak stays at about the size of a cell.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> What changes is how much of the coffee ends up in each peak.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

One study compared four grinds of the same coffee. About 29% of the volume was below 100 µm in the espresso grind, and about 10% in the drip-filter grind.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a second study, an unsieved drip-filter grind held about 26% and a coarser burr grind about 15%.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> So a finer setting adds fines, not only smaller main particles.

Other factors change the distribution at the same setting:

- **Bean temperature.** In a 2016 study, grinding cold beans gave a narrower distribution and a smaller most common particle size (mode).<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Roast degree.** According to the textbook chapter, darker and faster roasts make beans more porous and brittle. Ground soon after roasting, they give a wide distribution and many fines.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Grinder.** Different grinders need different settings and give different distributions for the same flow time in espresso, according to the same chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The 2016 study found the distribution independent of bean origin and processing method.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## How particle size is measured

Three methods are common: sieving, laser diffraction and image analysis.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

- **Sieving.** A stack of sieves with shrinking openings is shaken, and the coffee left on each sieve is weighed.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2023 study used six sieves on a vibratory sifter, with a brush to free fines stuck to the coarser particles.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Laser diffraction.** Dispersed particles pass a laser beam, and software calculates which spheres would scatter the light the same way.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The international standard ISO 13320 covers sizes from about 0.1 µm to 3 mm.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The coffee can be dispersed in air (dry) or in a liquid (wet).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Image analysis.** A camera photographs the particles as they pass, and software measures their outlines.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It also shows their shape, not only their size.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2024 espresso study used a dual-camera instrument of this kind.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Results from different methods are not directly comparable. Laser diffraction assumes round particles, and for irregular particles its result differs from sieving.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Larger coffee particles in particular are irregular in shape.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**By volume or by number.** The two measuring studies cited here report the share of volume in each size class.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Counted by number, fines dominate instead. By our own arithmetic, one particle of 500 µm has the volume of 1,000 particles of 50 µm.

## What fines do in the brew

**Extraction.** Fines have more surface per volume than coarse particles, so they extract faster.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to a coffee science textbook, small particles allow more efficient extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The size of the effect is debated (see Contested).

**Flow.** Fines fill the spaces between larger particles. In a 2024 espresso study, more fines made the bed less permeable, so water flowed more slowly and shots took longer.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2023 study found the same direction: more fines and more surface, lower permeability.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Channelling.** Channelling is uneven flow, where water finds easier paths through the bed. In a 2020 espresso study, extraction yield dropped again at the finest grind settings.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> According to the authors, this strongly suggests uneven flow at fine settings.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Body and sediment.** According to a coffee science textbook, mouthfeel depends partly on suspended fines, particularly in French press and espresso.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The same chapter links French press sediment to fines and the metal mesh filter.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> According to the grinding chapter, a certain share of fines is deemed necessary in espresso to build pressure, body and crema in its short brew time.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Changing the share of fines

The distribution is changed mainly through the grind setting. A finer setting raises the share of fines as well as moving the main peak.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In espresso, the share of fines then shows up as a change in flow time.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Sifting.** Sieving out part of the grind before brewing narrows the distribution. According to the grinding chapter, this could avoid off-notes from fines and boulders, but it loses coffee and is impractical in a busy café.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a 2023 French press study, sieved grinds of one size class gave less dissolved coffee than ordinary bimodal grinds.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry tested sifting for pour-over or espresso flavour.

## Size statistics

A PSD is summarised by a few characteristic sizes. The textbook chapter on grinding uses x10, x50 and x90: the sizes below which 10%, 50% and 90% of the coffee lies; x50 is the median.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Equivalent diameters compare each particle with a sphere of the same surface or the same volume.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Averaged over the sample, they give the surface mean d[3,2] (Sauter mean diameter) and the volume mean d[4,3] (De Brouckere mean).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The surface mean is strongly pulled down by the fines, while the volume mean lies close to the median.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a bimodal grind the two can lie far apart: 27.34 µm and 457.84 µm for one drip-filter grind.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A ratio of surface to volume gives the specific surface.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Number versus volume (our own arithmetic).** The volume of a sphere scales with the cube of its diameter. One 500 µm particle has the volume of 1,000 particles of 50 µm, but those 1,000 particles have 10 times its surface. A number-based distribution is therefore dominated by fines; a volume-based one by the coarse peak. A sample with about 25% of its volume below 100 µm consists, by count, mostly of fines.

**Choice of basis.** In the 2024 espresso study the median was volume-weighted and based on the projected particle area in images.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The 2023 microtomography study reported its laser-diffraction results as volume shares and specific surface.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Sieving gives mass fractions directly.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Comparisons between studies need the same basis and method.

## Two-domain extraction models

Models of extraction from a bed treat ground coffee as two domains. Easily accessible coffee sits in fines and on the broken surfaces of larger grains; less accessible coffee sits in the intact cells inside larger grains.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Both are described by first-order rate equations.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Because fines have a larger surface-to-volume ratio, they extract faster than coarse grains, so different particle sizes extract at different rates.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Measurements support a particle-size effect, but its cause is less clear. In a 2017 espresso study, smaller particles gave more caffeine and trigonelline per gram of beverage, while tamping pressure had no detectable effect.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> That study introduced a reduced model with only physically meaningful parameters.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2023 study from the same Munich group measured its grinds by laser diffraction and found that their Sauter mean diameters did not differ significantly between grind levels.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Surface area alone therefore could not explain the effect of grind level in that study.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

The 2024 espresso study varied the share of fines directly. Coffee was ground at burr spacings of 160–250 µm; at the three coarsest settings, 1, 2 or 4 g of sieved fines replaced the same mass of coffee in a 20 g dose; shots ran at 9 bar to 40 g of beverage.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Added fines did not produce outlying combinations of extraction yield and time.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors conclude that fines change bed permeability, not the extraction mechanism, and that the surface-area effect on extraction efficiency seems marginal.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Fast shots were already efficient: shots of under 10 s and of 15 s reached 17–18% extraction yield.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Permeability and channelling

According to the brewing chapter, the coffee bed’s permeability decides how water flows; too low a permeability gives high pressure, low flow and long brew times.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> According to the grinding chapter, a wide or bimodal distribution packs to a lower porosity and a higher pressure loss than uniform particles.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Fines lower permeability in both of the measuring studies consulted here.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the 2024 study, a regression on the full distributions linked particles up to 150 µm to longer extraction time, those above 250 µm to shorter time.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Feedback in the bed.** A 2023 model treats channelling as a positive feedback. Extraction dissolves coffee, which raises porosity and permeability, so paths that carry more water open further.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Small differences in porosity are therefore amplified over the shot.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In this model, the drop in yield at fine settings comes from one part of the bed being fully extracted, and the authors caution that average yield may be a poor guide to flavour at fine grinds.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The 2020 espresso study had read the same drop as strong evidence of inhomogeneous flow at fine settings, with poor reproducibility and wasted coffee as results.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

In the conical geometry of a pour-over, flow is uneven for geometric reasons as well; for a fine drip grind in a cylindrical bed, a model predicted a variation of about 5 percentage points in extraction yield.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The brew methods entry covers bed shape.

## Fines migration and bed consolidation

Fines migration is the idea that water carries fines downwards through the bed. If it happens, fines would collect above the filter and lower the porosity near the bottom, because the filter keeps them from leaving.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2023 microtomography study compared capsule beds before and after extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> After extraction, porosity always decreased from the outlet at the bottom to the inlet at the top: the bed was more open at the bottom, not less.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its authors note that fines migration would push the profile the other way.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

According to the brewing chapter, the bed also changes as it wets: particles swell and rearrange under flow and pressure, porosity falls and the pressure drop rises; the finer the distribution, the more the bed consolidates.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured fines migration directly in a pour-over or espresso bed.

## Sifting: what the evidence shows

The 2023 French press study separated one grind into six sieve fractions: below 124 µm, 125–249, 250–399, 400–599, 600–799 µm and above 800 µm.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Brews used 25 g of coffee in 250 g of water at 95 °C for 5 minutes.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The fine grind straight from the grinder gave the highest extraction, 21.4 ± 0.9%; the sieved 125–249 µm fraction gave the lowest.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The authors call this hard to explain and suggest that compounds may be distributed differently between particle sizes.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Homogeneous size classes appeared less able to extract the available solutes than bimodal grinds.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

The textbook chapter on grinding describes the opposite expectation for espresso: a narrower distribution avoids the mix of over-extracted fines and under-extracted boulders.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The French press study did not test espresso or pour-over, and the chapter reports no sensory measurement.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Fines migration.** Whether fines move far enough during a brew to change flow has not been measured directly in any study consulted for this entry; the one imaging study found a porosity profile opposite to what migration would produce.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Sifting and flavour.** No peer-reviewed sensory test of sifted grinds for pour-over or espresso was found; the one French press study found sieved fractions extracted less.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Surface area or permeability.** Two espresso studies found that grind affects the cup mainly through flow, not surface area<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>; how far this holds for low-pressure brews is not established.
- **Grinder comparisons.** Published distributions for named grinder types at matched settings are scarce in peer-reviewed work; the textbook chapter on grinding shows examples but no tabulated values.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Mo et al., 2023 – Microstructure of coffee beds.</strong> Measures four grinds of one arabica blend, from espresso to drip filter, by laser diffraction and X-ray microtomography, and simulates flow through the imaged beds. The fines peak stayed at about 25–50 µm, the size of a cell, while the coarse peak moved with the grind; more fines meant lower permeability.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 13, 16374 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-42380-y">doi:10.1038/s41598-023-42380-y</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Smrke et al., 2024 – Fines in espresso.</strong> Grinds one coffee at six settings and adds sieved fines in steps, then follows flow, yield, aroma and sensory scores. The share of fines mainly controlled bed permeability and shot time, with little effect on extraction efficiency.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 14, 5612 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-55831-x">doi:10.1038/s41598-024-55831-x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Moroney et al., 2019 – Uniformity in the coffee bed.</strong> Models extraction with two domains of accessible and less accessible coffee, in a cylindrical and a conical bed, and compares two measured grinds. Fines, with their larger surface-to-volume ratio, extract faster than coarse grains; for the fine grind, the model predicted about 5 points of yield variation within a cylindrical bed.</p>
<p class="meta">PLOS ONE 14(7), e0219906 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0219906">doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0219906</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Angeloni et al., 2023 – Sieved versus bimodal grinds.</strong> Brews French press coffee from sieve fractions and from ordinary grinds. The homogeneous fractions gave less dissolved solids and fewer bioactive compounds than bimodal grinds; a very fine homogeneous grind gave the fewest of these compounds.</p>
<p class="meta">European Food Research and Technology 249(5), 1247–1256 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00217-023-04210-3">doi:10.1007/s00217-023-04210-3</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Lee et al., 2023 – Uneven extraction.</strong> Models two flow paths through an espresso bed with different porosity. Extraction opens the faster path further, so differences grow during the shot; this can explain why yield falls at very fine grind settings.</p>
<p class="meta">Physics of Fluids · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0138998">doi:10.1063/5.0138998</a></p>
</div>
