## What a glass transition is

Many dry foods are amorphous: their molecules are packed without the order of a crystal. Such a material can be in one of two states.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Below a certain temperature it is glassy, rigid and brittle. Above it, the material turns rubbery and soft.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The change happens over a temperature range, the glass transition temperature (T<sub>g</sub>).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It is reversible: cooling takes the material back to the glassy state.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Water moves T<sub>g</sub> down. Food scientists call water a plasticiser: it softens the material, much as heat does.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In dry foods, a little extra water lowers T<sub>g</sub> sharply.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> So the state of a dry food depends on two things at once: temperature and water content.

## Glass and rubber in the coffee bean

In the coffee bean, the material that matters is the cell wall. The walls are thick and made of polysaccharides.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to a roasting textbook chapter, these polysaccharides are glassy or rubbery depending on moisture and temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The chapter adds that the change is gradual and blurred, because the wall holds several polysaccharides with different transitions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Stored green and roasted coffee beans are both mainly glassy.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In a 2019 calorimetry study, T<sub>g</sub> of green coffee fell from 48.76 °C to 34.89 °C as water activity rose from 0.155 to 0.512.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Roasted coffee was less sensitive: its T<sub>g</sub> fell from 45.73 °C to 40.15 °C over the same range.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Not every study agrees. A 2021 study of ground Brazilian arabica found T<sub>g</sub> midpoints near −11 °C in green, light and dark roasted samples.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Its authors also describe water as a plasticiser of coffee, but their three samples differed by less than 1 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The two studies disagree on the level by about 50 °C at similar water activity (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## What happens in the roaster

According to the textbook chapter, the bean passes from glassy to rubbery and finally back to glassy during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Heat pushes the bean towards the rubbery state. Drying pulls the other way, because a drier material has a higher T<sub>g</sub>.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to the chapter, the speed of drying therefore plays a key role.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The bean swells while it is rubbery. Gas pressure inside the bean drives the expansion, and the thick cell walls resist it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the rubbery state the walls resist less, so they stretch.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> An SCA handbook describes the same picture: the material softens and carbon dioxide inflates the cells.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Where these gases come from is covered under roast gases and CO₂.

The walls stretch but do not burst. According to the textbook chapter, the framework of cell walls stays intact while the cells empty and fill with gas.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A doctoral thesis links the absence of cell-wall rupture, probably, to the rubbery state of the walls at high temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The result is the open, porous bean described under porosity and cell structure.

## Why roast speed changes the bean

Because the state depends on both temperature and water, the speed of drying matters. According to the textbook chapter, the roast profile is therefore crucial for how far the bean swells.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

A 2000 study compared a fast, hot roast with a slow, cooler one to the same roast degree. The fast roast gave beans with greater volume, more pore space and larger micropores in the cell walls.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A doctoral thesis from the same laboratory reviews a patent that explains this with water. Fast roasts retain more water at high temperature, and water softens the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> This explanation was not tested directly in any source consulted for this entry.

How the swelling is timed against first crack is still open. The textbook chapter and the SCA handbook place the transition differently (see Contested), and first crack has its own entry.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## After the roast: brittle beans

Roasted beans are brittle. In a 2001 texture study, roasting made beans lose strength and toughness and become progressively more brittle.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The authors relate this to water loss and to the changed tissue structure.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to a doctoral thesis, their brittleness means roasted beans are first crushed into fragments and then ground finer.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

Water reverses part of this. Beans with more water after quenching were firmer in a shear test.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In a 2007 texture study, plasticisation by water took away the typical brittleness of roasted beans.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Wetter roasted coffee also ground coarser and needed a rest before grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

This links the transition to quench water. A small amount of quench water mainly cools the bean; too much is absorbed and raises its water content.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> How quenching is done is explained under cooling and quenching.

## Physics: plasticisation and the state diagram

**Plasticisation.** Water has a low molecular weight and a low T<sub>g</sub> of −135 °C, so it depresses the T<sub>g</sub> of food solids.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It adds free volume between the large molecules and lets them relax.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Heat has a similar effect, by raising molecular energy.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Gordon–Taylor relation.** For a binary system of food solids and water, a 2010 review gives the Gordon–Taylor equation.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It estimates T<sub>g</sub> of the mixture from the T<sub>g</sub> of the dry solids and of water, their proportions and a fitted constant.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> No Gordon–Taylor fit for coffee bean material was found for this entry.

**State diagram.** A state diagram plots the physical state of a food against temperature and water content.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Below the T<sub>g</sub> curve the material is glassy; above it, rubbery.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to the roasting textbook chapter, coffee has several such lines, one for each cell-wall polysaccharide.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the chapter's account, a roast is a path across this diagram: temperature rises while water content falls.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry drew that path on measured T<sub>g</sub> values of coffee.

**A model of the roasting bean.** A 2019 model of stresses in a roasting bean uses a T<sub>g</sub> that falls with water content, from about 190 °C for dry material.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Below T<sub>g</sub> the model treats the wall material as elastic; above it, as viscous and permanently deformable.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In the simulations, the wetter interior passes through the transition first and becomes mechanically weak.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A large spike in stress follows, which the authors suggest may signal the onset of first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The dry-material value is a model parameter, for which the authors point to a conference paper; it was not measured in that study.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Water: plasticiser and antiplasticiser

At low water contents, water does not always soften coffee. A 2011 study combined compression tests, calorimetry and NMR on green and roasted beans.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Up to a water activity of 0.61 (green) and 0.75 (roasted), water hydrated the solid matrix and hardened the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Only once this hydration was complete did water act as a plasticiser and the beans soften.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Above 0.78 (green) and 0.86 (roasted), hardness fell and the elastic modulus fell further.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

A 2007 study on the same kind of beans found the matching pattern. Strength and toughness rose with hydration up to a critical water activity, 0.75 for raw and about 0.86 for roasted beans; above it, the bean matrix progressively softened.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Quenched coffee fits the hardening side. In industrial and laboratory trials, the maximum shear force of roasted beans rose almost linearly with water content.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The rise was steeper in light roasts than in dark ones, and light roasts were less brittle overall.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The thesis calls this a plasticising effect of water.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In the 2011 study's terms, firmer beans at low water activity are antiplasticisation (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Grinding and storage

**Grinding.** Wetter roasted coffee ground coarser, and it needed time to equilibrate before grinding gave good results.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Drier coffee did not show this time dependence.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In the thesis, the coarser grind is explained by the lower brittleness of wetter beans, which need more energy to break.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> According to the grinding chapter, beans with high moisture remain rather elastic and hard to grind even after a long rest.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The same chapter notes that liquid nitrogen makes roasted beans more brittle.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

**Storage.** Green and roasted coffee are mainly glassy, and this glassy structure slowly changes during storage.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In industrial trials, higher water content lowered the aroma stability of roasted coffee in closed storage.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The thesis explains this most probably by plasticisation: water lowers T<sub>g</sub> and lets reactants move faster.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The thesis names this as the likely cause; the reaction mechanisms themselves were not shown.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The authors of the 2021 study conclude from their T<sub>g</sub> range that roasted beans can be kept at room temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Packaging, oxygen and staling are covered under freshness and storage.

## Measuring the transition

**Calorimetry.** In differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), the glass transition shows as a step in the heat-flow curve.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Studies report its onset, midpoint or endpoint, and the value depends on the scan rate.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The 2021 study used the modulated form (MDSC), which it describes as suited to glass transitions.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The 2021 study reports onset, midpoint and endpoint of the step.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The 2019 study added dielectric spectroscopy at 1.6–2.7 GHz and found it correlated with water activity and T<sub>g</sub>.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Mechanical tests.** Mechanical methods follow the softening directly. A doctoral thesis on hot-air roasting used dynamic mechanical thermal analysis (DMTA) on green-coffee slices heated steadily from ambient temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Its results were not in the part of the thesis consulted for this entry. Compression and shear tests give the hardness, modulus and firmness values above.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Limits.** All the measured T<sub>g</sub> values come from stored beans at room-temperature water activities.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> During roasting the bean dries and changes chemically at the same time. No source consulted for this entry measured T<sub>g</sub> under those conditions.

## Risks and defects

- **Over-quenching.** Excess water is absorbed and raises the water content.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Wetter beans are firmer, grind coarser and need time to equilibrate before grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Shorter aroma stability.** Higher water content lowered aroma stability in closed storage.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Roast profile and swelling.** According to the textbook chapter, swelling happens in the rubbery state, so the drying rate shapes bean volume.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In one study, a fast, hot roast gave larger and more porous beans than a slow one.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Transition during a roast.** No measurement of T<sub>g</sub> or of the state of the cell walls inside a roasting bean was found.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Level of T<sub>g</sub> in coffee.** Two calorimetry studies differ by about 50 °C at similar water activity.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Water retention and expansion.** That fast roasts swell more because they stay wetter is an explanation, not a tested result, in the sources consulted.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Stress and first crack.** A model links a stress spike at the transition to first crack; it has not been checked against measurements.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Glass transition and staling.** Plasticisation is the proposed reason for faster aroma loss in wetter coffee; the mechanisms are not known in detail.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Iaccheri et al., 2019 – Glass transition of green and roasted coffee.</strong> Measures T<sub>g</sub> of green and roasted beans held at set water activities by calorimetry, and relates dielectric spectra to it. Both are mainly glassy; water lowers T<sub>g</sub>, more strongly in green than in roasted coffee.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry 301, 125187 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2019.125187">doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2019.125187</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Rocculi et al., 2011 – Water that hardens before it softens.</strong> Combines texture analysis, sorption isotherms, calorimetry and NMR on green and roasted beans. Water first hardens the matrix as it hydrates it, and acts as a plasticiser only above a critical water activity.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 59, 8265–8271 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf201333a">doi:10.1021/jf201333a</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Schenker et al., 2000 – Pore structure and roasting conditions.</strong> Roasts beans in a fast, hot and a slow, cooler fluid-bed process to the same roast degree and studies volume, pores and microstructure. The fast roast gives larger beans, more pore volume and larger micropores in the cell walls.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 65(3), 452–457 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2000.tb16026.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2000.tb16026.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Baggenstoss et al., 2007 – Quench water and the roasted bean.</strong> Compares air-cooled and water-quenched coffee at laboratory and industrial scale. Wetter beans degassed faster and were firmer; most aroma compounds were similarly stable.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 55(16), 6685–6691 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf070338d">doi:10.1021/jf070338d</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Fadai et al., 2019 – Stresses in a roasting bean.</strong> Models the bean as a material that turns from elastic to viscous at a moisture-dependent T<sub>g</sub>. The wetter interior softens first, and a stress spike follows that the authors link to first crack.</p>
<p class="meta">International Journal of Non-Linear Mechanics 110, 123–130 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijnonlinmec.2018.12.006">doi:10.1016/j.ijnonlinmec.2018.12.006</a></p>
</div>
