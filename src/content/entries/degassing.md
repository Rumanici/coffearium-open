brief:
  - Roasting forms gas inside the bean, mostly carbon dioxide with some carbon monoxide and nitrogen. Freshly roasted beans hold 1–2% of their weight as trapped gas; green coffee holds none.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
  - Gas sits in large voids left by roasting and in porous cell walls. It leaves by pressure-driven flow from large pores and, much more slowly, by diffusion through fine ones.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
  - Darker and faster roasts release more gas, faster. Whole beans degas over weeks, ground coffee over hours to days; grinding releases a large share at once, more when finer.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
  - The gas shapes packaging (valves, nitrogen, resting before sealing), the bloom in filter coffee and the crema on espresso.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
  - Roasted coffee also releases carbon monoxide. Occupational health studies found peaks above 200 ppm near grinders and open hoppers, and advise monitoring and, where needed, ventilating storage spaces.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## What degassing is

According to the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) freshness handbook and a coffee science textbook, green coffee holds no trapped gas.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A roasting chapter in the same textbook states that roasting forms large amounts of gas inside the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The chapter describes this gas, together with water vapour, as what makes the bean swell.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In its account, part of the gas escapes in the roaster; a large part stays trapped, leaving slowly in storage and quickly on grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The SCA handbook calls that slow release degassing, or off-gassing.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

A weighing study and the textbook both put the trapped gas in freshly roasted whole beans at 1–2% of their weight.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The freshness entry covers what this means for staling. This entry looks at the gas itself: what it is, where it sits and how it gets out. It also covers what the gas means at the roastery, at the bar and for the air people breathe.

## Which gases form

The SCA handbook and the roasting chapter name **carbon dioxide** as the main gas from roasting, and carbon monoxide and nitrogen as other important ones.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A classic coffee technology textbook, as cited in a peer-reviewed roasting study, puts the share of carbon dioxide at 87% of the gases formed.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The SCA handbook adds that the composition may vary with variety, roast degree and roast profile.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The share of **carbon monoxide** is not settled (see Contested); its presence, and its importance for safety, are (see Carbon monoxide and safety).<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

According to the SCA handbook, carbon dioxide forms in two groups of roasting reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> One breaks down amino acids; the other, late in the roast, breaks down carbohydrates by heat.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The Deep dive gives the chemistry and the amounts measured.

## Where the gas is held

Roasting turns the dense green tissue into a porous one. According to the roasting chapter, cell contents are pushed against the intact walls, leaving a large gas-filled void in the centre of each cell.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> These inflated cells are called **macropores**. The SCA handbook describes the cell walls as porous too, with **micropores** far too small to image.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> One study and a related thesis describe the pore structure as controlling how substances move in and out of the bean in roasting and storage.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Not all of the gas is free: some is held in the solid material or dissolved in oil (see Pore structure and bound gas).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The roasting chapter describes darker roasts as creating more volume and porosity, although swelling levels off at some point.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## How it gets out: flow and diffusion

The SCA handbook describes two mechanisms that release the gas.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

- **Pressure-driven flow.** In the handbook's account, gas held at elevated pressure in the macropores flows out through small openings, from high to low pressure.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> This flow depends only on the total pressure, not on which gas surrounds the coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Diffusion.** Diffusion is the slow spread of molecules from where they are many to where they are few. In the handbook's account, carbon dioxide held in the cell walls or dissolved in oil diffuses through the fine pores to the surface.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Diffusion is much slower than flow.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It depends on the carbon dioxide outside the coffee, not on the total pressure.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> So a nitrogen atmosphere, even at high pressure, does not stop it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Release measured in individual studies is discussed under Release kinetics. The SCA handbook noted that it was not fully understood which mechanisms were responsible.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## What speeds it up or slows it down

- **Roast degree.** Darker roasts hold and release more gas, and release it faster.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In one study, whole arabica beans held about 6 mg/g of residual carbon dioxide in light roasts and about 15 mg/g in dark roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Very dark roasts held no more than dark ones.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the weighing study, ground arabica and robusta coffee released more gas from light to medium roast, but not from medium to dark.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Roast speed.** In the weighing study, faster roasts released gas faster; in medium and dark roasts they also released more.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A study using a fluid-bed roaster, which roasts beans in a stream of hot air, found significantly faster degassing after high-temperature, short-time roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Whether fast roasts also form more gas in total is disputed (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Grind size.** In one study, the finer the grind, the faster the degassing.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to the SCA handbook, grinding destroys or damages the large pores and shortens the paths for diffusion.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Temperature and moisture.** According to a grinding chapter in the same textbook, both make molecules diffuse faster and so speed up degassing, along with aroma loss.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The SCA handbook states that freezing strongly slows degassing but does not stop it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In one study, water-quenched coffees with higher moisture content degassed faster than air-cooled ones.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Their pore size distribution did not explain the difference.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Arabica and robusta.** In the weighing study, ground robusta released up to 3.9 mg/g after grinding at espresso fineness, and ground arabica up to 3.0 mg/g.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry compared whole beans of the two species.
- **Origin and processing.** The SCA handbook reports whole-bean coffees of different origins and processes degassing at clearly different rates at the same roast degree.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It suggests lower green coffee density as one possible reason for the fastest one.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Whole beans versus ground

According to the SCA handbook, whole beans release their gas over weeks.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the weighing study at 35 °C, whole beans were still releasing gas after 400 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A light roast measured for 800 hours had lost 3.4 mg/g and was still releasing.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In another study at 25 °C, a dark, fast-roasted coffee took more than 800 hours to release about 14 mg/g.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> That was about 90% of its residual carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Grinding changes the timescale. Part of the gas escapes during grinding itself, and more of it the finer the grind (see figure).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the weighing study, darker roasts lost a larger share on grinding than medium roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The authors suggest that more of their gas sits in large pores that grinding destroys.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The rest then leaves within hours to days.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In that second study at 25 °C, release from a fine grind levelled off within 50 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Coarse and medium grinds were still degassing slowly at the end of the test.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The SCA handbook reports a storage result that does not fit a simple picture. Beans stored whole for several weeks and then ground released more gas than the whole beans could still release.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The handbook offers two explanations: gas pockets that only grinding opens, or a very slow release mechanism not seen in whole beans.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Packing and degassing in practice

**Why bags have valves.** According to the SCA handbook, coffee sealed airtight without a valve visibly inflates and can burst.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The handbook and the roasting chapter describe valve bags as letting the gas out instead.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In the handbook's account, total pressure acts only on flow from the large pores; diffusion depends on the carbon dioxide outside the coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The handbook adds that the released gas pushes oxygen out of the bag, which slows oxidation.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Packing before or after degassing.** One study lists three ways manufacturers handle the gas: vacuum packing so the pack does not billow, a special film that lets carbon dioxide escape, or tempering.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Tempering means holding the coffee before packing to release as much gas as possible.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> According to the roasting chapter, industrial lines use tempering silos for beans and degassing silos and machinery for ground coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> This avoids overpressure in later steps or in sealed packs such as capsules.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

The grinding chapter states that ground coffee for sealed containers, from which no gas can escape, must be fully degassed.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In its account, a barista grinding small amounts does not need to rest the grounds.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The SCA handbook uses capsules to show the trade-off: too little degassing builds pressure that can deform or burst them, too much costs aroma.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The freshness entry summarises cupping trials on resting before bagging and on nitrogen flushing.

**Nitrogen flushing** replaces the oxygen in the pack.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the SCA handbook, it does not stop degassing, because carbon dioxide still diffuses out into a nitrogen atmosphere.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Swollen bags** are a sign of gas still leaving the coffee. In the SCA handbook's reading, a swollen sealed bag or a deformed capsule means the coffee was packed before enough gas had left.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## In the cup

According to the SCA handbook, the gas left in the grounds is released the moment water reaches them. This raises resistance to water flow and changes contact between water and coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Espresso settings are adjusted to the coffee's age for that reason.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In filter brewing, the same release shows as the **bloom**.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> On espresso, the gas forms the **crema**, the foam on top of the shot.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A review of espresso foam describes its gas phase as mainly carbon dioxide generated during roasting and trapped in the cell structure.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

In a demonstration reported in the SCA handbook, even fully degassed coffee, stored for a long time, still formed some crema.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The handbook attributes this to carbon dioxide formed when coffee acids neutralise bicarbonate in the water, and to carbon dioxide already present in the water.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The brew-methods and espresso machine entries cover brewing.

## Formation chemistry and amounts

The SCA handbook names the reactions that form carbon dioxide: Strecker degradation, which runs alongside the Maillard reaction and turns amino acids into aldehydes and carbon dioxide, and pyrolysis, the thermal breakdown of carbohydrates late in the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the handbook, the Maillard route contributes mostly up to and around first crack; pyrolysis adds carbon dioxide only in darker roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In one laboratory fluid-bed study, reactions began to produce carbon dioxide and water in relevant amounts at 180–200 °C bean temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to the roasting chapter, gas formation in isothermal roasting is slow at the start and accelerates strongly in the second half of the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**How much forms.** The same fluid-bed study measured carbon dioxide online in the exhaust and then in closed flasks. Per 100 g of green coffee, 0.4–0.5 g of carbon dioxide left during roasting and a further 0.83–0.99 g during 63 days of storage at 23 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A further 0.1 g or less was estimated for cooling.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The fast roast lost less carbon dioxide in the roaster and more in storage; assuming that release had levelled off by day 63, the authors read the totals as almost equal.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Pore structure and bound gas

The SCA handbook notes that micropores in the cell walls can be measured only indirectly, by mercury porosimetry.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Roast conditions change the pore structure. In one study, at the same roast degree, high-temperature roasted beans had greater bean volume, greater pore volume and larger micropores in the cell walls than low-temperature roasted beans.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Not all of the carbon dioxide is free gas. The roasting-and-storage study above describes part of it as bound to polar sites of the bean's polymers or dissolved in the coffee oil or in the residual moisture; part may be held in collapsed amorphous zones of the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to the SCA handbook, carbon dioxide dissolved in oil can reach far higher concentrations than the volume of the coffee could hold as gas.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Release kinetics

The SCA handbook lists several kinds of diffusion as involved, including Knudsen and surface diffusion.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In one study, carbon dioxide release from roast and ground coffee followed Fick's law for unsteady diffusion with one or two effective diffusivities, and the authors concluded that pressure flow and Knudsen diffusion both take place.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In whole beans, the gravimetric (weighing) study found the degassing of a fast-roasted dark coffee best described by two first-order processes: a fast one that dominates early but carries a small share of the gas, and a slow one that carries most of it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The authors attribute the difference between roasts probably to porosity, and suggest that surface diffusion and diffusion of carbon dioxide dissolved in coffee oils are likely the dominant processes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Release rates measured in the gravimetric study reached up to 40 mg/h for 12 g of freshly ground coffee, against up to 2 mg/h for 15 g of whole beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Measuring degassing

The SCA handbook groups the methods as below.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the gravimetric study, each measures something slightly different, which is one reason published amounts are spread over a large range.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

| Method | What it measures | Limits | Used in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chemical | Carbon dioxide driven out of the sample and captured on a sorbent; the sorbent's weight gain gives the residual CO₂ | Complex and slow; no kinetics unless repeated at different storage times<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> | Residual CO₂ by roast and grind<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> |
| Volumetric | Volume change of a sealed container as gas is released (eudiometer) | Simple to build, but not accurate<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> | – |
| Pressure increase | Pressure and temperature in a closed rigid container; mimics a capsule | The most common method; treats the released gas as one gas<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> | Storage release in closed flasks<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> |
| Infrared | CO₂ concentration in the headspace by its infrared absorbance; specific for CO₂ | Precise, but needs expensive custom-built equipment<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> | Degassing kinetics, whole and ground<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> |
| Gravimetric | Weight loss of a sample in a container with a capillary outlet; mimics a valve bag | Measures all gas lost, not only CO₂; needs very stable balances, constant temperature and low humidity<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> | Kinetics by roast degree, speed and grind<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |

In the gravimetric set-up, 15 g of whole beans or 12 g of ground coffee sat in a basket in a container with a stainless-steel capillary outlet, on a semi-micro balance in an oven at 35 °C, with silica gel to buffer the release or uptake of water and volatiles.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The authors note that earlier methods were either specific to carbon dioxide or assumed that the released gas is pure carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Two cautions apply when comparing numbers. First, the basis: some studies report per gram of roasted coffee<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>, others per 100 g of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Second, curve fitting: the gravimetric study fitted its curves with a Weibull model, but for a light roast measured for 800 hours instead of 400 the fit gave quite different parameters, and the authors warn that the model does not allow correct extrapolation of the total amount.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Carbon monoxide and safety

Carbon monoxide emitted from roasted coffee is described as a potential occupational respiratory exposure hazard for workers in the coffee industry.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In NIOSH evaluations of coffee facilities, high levels were found where beans were stored or ground, and the biggest source was not the roaster, a combustion source, but the roasted beans themselves.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Grinding gives a larger surface for the carbon monoxide to leave.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

**What was measured.** Tested loose and unpackaged, as a worst case, commercially available whole and ground coffee was shown to emit carbon monoxide rapidly.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Freshly ground coffee emitted significantly more than whole beans. Roast degree made no significant difference for whole beans, while ground medium-dark and dark roasts emitted more than medium and light ones.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> At workplaces, average exposures over a shift stayed below the NIOSH recommended exposure limit of 35 ppm in one plant (<0.1–12.3 ppm); a NIOSH letter on coffee facilities reports 7–30 ppm.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Short peaks were much higher: above 200 ppm near grinders, two roaster operators with maxima above the NIOSH ceiling of 200 ppm, and 584 ppm for a worker standing next to an open hopper of ground coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> High exposures to carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide can cause headache, dizziness, fatigue, nausea and altered mentation.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**What the authorities recommend.** In the words of the peer-reviewed emission study: carbon monoxide concentrations "should be monitored in storage spaces in service and manufacturing facilities as well as transport vessels to ensure exposures do not exceed occupational exposure limits", and "Storage spaces may need to be ventilated to control CO concentrations to safe levels."<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The NIOSH evaluation recommended carbon monoxide monitors near the roasters and grinders to alert employees if levels exceed the 200 ppm ceiling, and covering hoppers of whole and ground coffee to limit the release of carbon monoxide and alpha-diketones into the production space; it also recommended local exhaust ventilation at all grinders, there to reduce alpha-diketones.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> A grinding chapter recommends ventilating the area around a grinder and, in industrial grinding, exhausting gas from grinders and from storage containers of ground coffee in a controlled way.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Silos and closed rooms.** Tempering and degassing silos exist to let coffee release its gas before later steps.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry gives silo measurements; the monitoring and ventilation advice above applies to storage spaces generally.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Carbon dioxide itself is heavier than air and can displace oxygen at the bottom of a space; the SCA handbook considers releases large enough for suffocation unlikely with coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Open questions

- Which mechanisms control release from each kind of pore was not fully understood when the SCA handbook was written.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- Whether some gas is released only on grinding, or by a very slow mechanism in whole beans, is not resolved in the SCA handbook.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- According to the SCA handbook, released carbon dioxide can form carbonic acid in the brew, which could make fresh coffee taste more acidic; the handbook notes that this had not been studied systematically in coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- How roast degree and profile change the carbon monoxide share of the released gas was not known, according to the SCA handbook.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- Why coffees of different origin and processing degas at different rates at the same roast degree is left open in the SCA handbook; green coffee density is one suggestion.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Smrke et al., 2018 – Gravimetric degassing.</strong> Weighed whole and ground coffee continuously in containers with a capillary outlet at 35 °C, for nine roast profiles of one arabica coffee and for ground arabica and robusta. Darker and faster roasts released more gas, faster; grinding released up to three quarters of the trapped gas at once; and a two-process model described dark, fast-roasted beans best.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 66(21), 5293–5300 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.7b03310">doi:10.1021/acs.jafc.7b03310</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Geiger et al., 2005 – CO₂ during roasting and storage.</strong> Followed carbon dioxide and water vapour in the exhaust of a laboratory fluid bed during a fast and a slow roast, then gas release from the beans in closed flasks for 63 days. The fast roast lost less carbon dioxide in the roaster and more in storage; the totals were almost equal.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 70(2), E124–E130 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb07084.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb07084.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Wang & Lim, 2014 – Roast conditions and grind.</strong> Measured residual carbon dioxide chemically and its release by infrared spectroscopy in arabica coffee roasted fast and slow to four degrees, whole and at three grind sizes. Residual CO₂ rose with roast degree up to dark, grinding released 26–59% of it, and finer grinds degassed faster.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Research International 61, 144–151 · <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0963996914000337">ScienceDirect</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Anderson et al., 2003 – Diffusion kinetics.</strong> Modelled carbon dioxide release from freshly roasted and ground coffee during tempering with Fick's law. Effective diffusivities came out at 0.5–10 × 10⁻¹³ m²/s, and the authors concluded that both pressure flow and Knudsen diffusion are at work.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Engineering 59(1), 71–78 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/S0260-8774(02)00432-6">doi:10.1016/S0260-8774(02)00432-6</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>LeBouf & Aldridge, 2019 – Carbon monoxide from coffee.</strong> Measured carbon monoxide emission from 64 samples of 36 brands of commercially available coffee, loose, whole and ground. Ground coffee emitted significantly more than whole beans, and darker ground roasts more than lighter ones; the authors call for monitoring carbon monoxide in storage spaces and transport vessels and, where needed, ventilating storage spaces.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of the Air & Waste Management Association 69(1), 89–96 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/10962247.2018.1515125">doi:10.1080/10962247.2018.1515125</a></p>
</div>
