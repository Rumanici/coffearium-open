## What the dry mill does

Drying ends with coffee that still wears its outer layers. Washed coffee leaves the patio as parchment coffee; a natural leaves it as dried whole cherry, called husk coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The dry mill turns both into green coffee.

**Hulling** is the removal of the dried endocarp of parchment coffee – the parchment – to produce green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For parchment coffee, the huller takes off the parchment and silverskin.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> For dried cherry, the dry pulp and the parchment come off in a single operation.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> That dried husk is made up of skin, pulp and parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

Hulling is done by machine, or on small farms with a wooden mortar and pestle.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In Kenya, central private hulling companies hull farmers' coffee for a fee; hulling plants and equipment are expensive and technically demanding.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## The sequence

The Codex code of practice lists the steps after drying: the coffee can be stored, then hulled, and then passed through sizing (grading), sorting, polishing, cleaning and bagging.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In practice the order varies by mill.

1. **Reception and storage** – dry coffee is received and often held in parchment or cherry until it is sold.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
2. **Hulling** – the parchment or dried husk is broken and separated from the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In friction hullers an air current carries the loose shell away.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
3. **Polishing** – residual silverskin is removed by purely mechanical means.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Some silverskin already comes off in the huller; if it does not, a polisher is used.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
4. **Grading and sorting** – beans are separated by size, density and colour, by machine and by hand; hand sorting is still widely used.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Sorting removes foreign matter, fragments and defective beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
5. **Cleaning and bagging** – the finished lot is cleaned and bagged for export.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Screen sizes, defect counts and sorting machines are covered in grading and sorting. This entry deals with the milling itself.

## Hullers

The sources consulted for this entry describe two main designs.

**Ribbed-cylinder (friction) hullers.** An 1890 US patent by Evaristo Conrado Engelberg describes a ribbed cylinder turning inside a sectional casing, with an adjustable hulling plate and a slotted screen.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The ribs carry the grain continuously past the plate and towards the discharge end.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The gap between plate and cylinder is set wider at the feed end than at the delivery end.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The machine was built for rice, but the inventor reports using it for coffee by widening the plate's range of adjustment.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A 1912 coffee huller and polisher uses the same idea with ribs that are partly helical: the ribs hull the coffee and feed it forward, and an air current removes the loose shell.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Rubber-roll hullers.** A Philippine national standard for coffee hullers states that rubber-roll hullers shall be used for wet-processed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The same standard also lists steel hullers as the other type, and defines a coffee huller as a machine that removes the hull from dried parchment coffee or dried cherry.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Yield: how much green coffee comes out

Mills and buyers work with conversion factors.

- **Parchment to green.** The husk is about 20% of the weight of dry parchment coffee, a ratio of 1.25:1.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Cenicafé found the same 1.25 ratio in Colombia (1.26 for unselected coffee), a conversion factor of 0.80 (0.79 for unselected coffee), and reports hulling losses of 17.75–18.40%.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The trade factor multiplies parchment weight by 0.8 to get green weight.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Dried cherry to green.** The standard ratio is 2:1.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The trade factor multiplies dried-cherry weight by 0.5.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Dry processing gives about 1 kg of husk for every kilogram of coffee bean produced.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

These are rounded standards. Specialty chains that sort hard lose more: all five Ethiopian cooperatives in one study had post-farm-gate conversion rates, from fresh cherry to clean coffee, of 8:1 or higher (up to 11:1) when producing specialty coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The authors note that the cooperatives did not keep records that would allow these rates to be measured precisely.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Mechanics of friction hulling

A ribbed-cylinder huller rubs the parchment off by friction rather than cutting it.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The ribs co-operate with a stationary hulling plate to remove the hulls, and the plate can be adjusted, one side more than the other.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Engelberg set the gap wider at the feed end and narrower towards the delivery end.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Broken husk leaves through a perforated screen whose slots taper or flare outwards.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The same machine carried a polishing section with flexible leather fingers acting as whips on the grain.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

The 1912 design adds two things that matter for coffee. Helical ribs move the coffee forward while it is hulled, and an air current draws the loose shell and dust away before the beans pass to a second casing, where air again separates the remaining shell.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Cool air is also let in between the rotating sleeve and the hollow cylinder to cool the working parts.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Heat.** A review notes that hullers which rub the parchment off by friction might create heat.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It warns that beans should not be heated during hulling because heat affects the colour and taste of the coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry measured bean temperature in a huller or set a limit, so no number is given here.

## Polishing and silverskin

Polishing removes the residual silverskin from green coffee by purely mechanical means.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 1991 patent for a polishing machine gives two reasons for doing it: silverskin left on the bean can burn during roasting and give a bad smell to the roasted coffee, and patches of silverskin can make colour sorters reject sound beans as defective.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> These are the patent's stated reasons; no independent study consulted for this entry tested them.

No study consulted for this entry measured the heat produced by polishing or compared the cup of polished and unpolished lots from the same coffee.

## Risks and defects

- **Broken and crushed beans.** A badly set huller or over-dry, brittle coffee damages beans; coffee that is too wet can be crushed.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Over-dried coffee has a faded colour and damages easily in hulling.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A São Paulo good-practice manual likewise warns against over-drying to avoid weight loss and broken beans at hulling.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> A huller standard allows at most 10% mechanically damaged beans.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Heating.** Heat during hulling can affect colour and taste.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Loss of shelf life.** Hulled coffee loses quality faster in storage. In a Brazilian trial with Arabica ('Catuaí Amarelo') dried to 11% and stored in permeable bags, hulled natural and washed coffee kept its initial quality for up to 12 months at 10 °C and 50% relative humidity, but only up to 3 months at 25 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The authors attribute part of the loss to mechanical damage from hulling and to the missing fruit layers.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> A São Paulo good-practice manual therefore advises storing coffee in cherry or parchment rather than hulled.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **Residual silverskin.** A polishing-machine patent states that silverskin left on the bean can burn in the roaster and cause off-odours; this has not been tested in the sources consulted.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Defective beans passed through.** Black and sour defects carried the highest ochratoxin A levels in lower-grade coffee, so defects should be removed or screened before they enter the food chain.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Hulling losses.** In Kenya, competition between private hulling plants reduced hulling losses and charges.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Measurement

The Codex code expresses moisture on a wet basis and asks for moisture meters to be calibrated against the ISO 6673 method.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Huller performance is measured as hulling recovery, hulling efficiency, purity and the share of mechanically damaged beans.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In storage trials, electrical conductivity and tetrazolium tests have been used alongside cupping to track damage to the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Open questions

- How hot beans get in friction hullers and polishers, and at what bean temperature cup quality suffers. No measured values were found.
- Whether polishing changes cup quality once roasting differences are controlled.
- How moisture at hulling, within 11–12%, changes the share of broken beans.
- How much the moisture content of green coffee shifts between hulling and bagging.

<p><strong>Abreu et al., 2023 – Hulling and storage.</strong> Natural and washed Arabica dried to 11% moisture were stored for 12 months either hulled or unhulled, refrigerated or at 25 °C. Refrigeration preserved quality; at room temperature hulled coffee held its quality for only about 3 months, and the authors link part of the loss to hulling damage.</p>
<p class="meta">Anais da Academia Brasileira de Ciências 95(4) · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1590/0001-3765202320190612">doi:10.1590/0001-3765202320190612</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Montilla-Pérez et al., 2008 – Conversion factors in Colombian processing.</strong> Cenicafé measured physical properties and conversion factors at each stage of wet processing. The parchment-to-green ratio came out at 1.25, as in the older reference, with hulling losses of about 18%.</p>
<p class="meta">Cenicafé, Avances Técnicos 370 · <a href="https://biblioteca.cenicafe.org/bitstream/10778/358/1/avt0370.pdf">biblioteca.cenicafe.org</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Schuit et al., 2021 – Specialty coffee economics in Ethiopia.</strong> A study of five Ethiopian cooperatives that also records the conversion ratios used along the chain. It gives the standard 2:1 for dried cherry and 1.25:1 for parchment, and shows that specialty sorting pushes overall cherry-to-export ratios to 8:1 or more.</p>
<p class="meta">PeerJ 9, e10621 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.10621">doi:10.7717/peerj.10621</a></p>
</div>
