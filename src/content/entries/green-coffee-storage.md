## From dry mill to roaster

Green coffee is the dried, hulled seed that leaves the dry mill in bags. Between that mill and a roaster it is stored, shipped and often stored again. It travels from producing to consuming countries in bags or in bulk, usually in containers that hold 18–22 tonnes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The guideline standard ISO 8455 covers this whole stretch.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It deals with green coffee in bags, big bags, bulk and silos, from packing for export until arrival in the importing country.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Two rules from the Codex code of practice on ochratoxin A (OTA), a mould toxin, run through the whole journey.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Dried coffee must be protected from rewetting, degradation and contamination.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its moisture should not exceed 12.5% anywhere from the loading area to unloading, storage or roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**What this entry does not cover.** Holding parchment or dried cherry before hulling is resting, a separate step on the farm or at the mill. Keeping roasted coffee fresh is covered under freshness and storage. The physics of moisture, water activity and sorption is explained in the entry on moisture and water activity.

## Coffee and the air around it

Green coffee is hygroscopic: it takes up or gives off water until it is in balance with the air around it. A large stow of beans acts as a moisture reservoir that supplies water vapour to the air.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In air at 60% relative humidity, about one tenth of the weight of green coffee is water, almost all of it inside the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The Codex code gives the working rule. Below 60% relative humidity coffee keeps drying; above 80% it starts to absorb water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2021 study puts beans with 8.5–10% water (basis not stated) in balance with air at 50–65% relative humidity.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The balance is not a straight line. In the same study, seven green coffee lots took up water sharply once water activity passed 0.75, or 75% relative humidity at balance.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A lot is a batch of coffee from one origin and process, traded as a unit. The authors read this as the start of capillary condensation, water condensing in the fine pores of the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Above that point, a little more humidity adds much more water.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The effect shows in practice. A year-long trial in Colombia used stores averaging 72–81% relative humidity.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> At three of the four stores, coffee in natural-fibre bags rose from about 11.8% to an average of 15.0% moisture (wet basis).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> At these stores its water activity reached about 0.74.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The Codex code treats 0.67–0.70 as the ceiling that protects parchment coffee from fungi.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## The warehouse

**The building.** The Codex code asks for a store that keeps coffee dry and even. That means a cement floor with a damp-proof course, no risk of flooding, a waterproof roof and windows, and a high ceiling for air circulation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Moisture in a store can come from damp floors and walls, wind-driven rain, leaks, still air and wet coffee mixed with dry.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In long-term storage, humidity should be kept under strict control.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Stacking.** Bags go on pallets and away from the walls so that air can circulate.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Stored coffee should not stand in direct sun or near a heat source; the temperature differences that follow make water move within the stow.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The resting entry gives the stacking distances used at origin.

**Temperature.** Cool stores keep coffee longer. In one trial, hulled coffee in permeable bags kept its quality for 12 months at 10 °C and 50% relative humidity.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> At a constant 25 °C without humidity control, it kept it for only 3 months.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> In a Polish trial, coffee stored at 20 °C aged fastest, and coffee at −10 °C and 10 °C kept similarly well.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> A German insurers' cargo guide sums up the storage climate as "cool, dry, good ventilation".<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Odours and taints.** Green coffee is described by the same guide as extremely sensitive to foreign odours.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The Codex code says that non-food materials should not be stored with coffee, to avoid contamination or taint.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Insects.** The coffee bean weevil (*Araecerus fasciculatus*) is mostly considered a pest of stored products, although it also attacks coffee berries in the field.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The Codex code asks for checks on the weevil in the warehouse, using integrated pest management.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the Colombian trial, weevils appeared in coffee kept a year in fique bags at one store.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Bags, liners and vacuum packs

**Jute and sisal.** Green coffee is usually shipped in jute or sisal bags, which let air circulate freely.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Natural-fibre bags also let water vapour and oxygen pass, so the coffee follows the humidity of the store.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Hermetic bags.** These bags keep water vapour and gases out, unlike permeable bags.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> In a 12-month commercial trial, coffee in hermetic big bags kept its quality, and its green colour even intensified.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In a second trial, coffee in big bags flushed with carbon dioxide kept its specialty grade over 12 months.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In jute, undesirable flavours and aromas dominated.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Impermeable packaging also kept the original colour of the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Vacuum packs.** In Colombia, vacuum packs of polyethylene and nylon were stored for 365 days at four sites. At the coolest site the cup quality held for the whole year.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> At the hottest site, two thirds of the samples showed sensory defects from 120 days.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> From 240 days, all of them had the aged *reposo* defect.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Bulk and liners.** For coffee shipped loose, the Codex code recommends a sealable plastic liner that allows aeration, kept away from the container roof.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A marine insurers' guide describes loose coffee loaded straight into lined containers to save packaging costs.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Desiccants.** Desiccants are sachets of water-absorbing material. A 2023 study simulated a 42-day shipment from Brazil to Italy.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Compared with jute sacks alone, both hermetic packages and desiccants helped keep moisture, water activity and colour.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Desiccants were ineffective without hermetic packaging, and the amount used made no significant difference.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## In the container

**Container rain.** Even well-dried coffee contains water. When the temperature changes during the voyage, part of it can condense and rewet the coffee locally.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The water that moves can lead to mould growth and OTA.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The warm stow gives off water vapour. Where the roof cools, the air beneath it reaches 100% relative humidity and dew forms on its underside.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Drops then fall onto the top of the load, which a 1996 study called "internal raining".<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In this entry it is called container rain.

**Where it happens.** Coffee shipped from a hot to a cold climate meets large temperature differences.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> For box containers, the 1996 study found the greatest risk in winter, after the container is unloaded onto the dockside.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to a 2021 study and an insurers' guide, coffee should travel at 10–20 °C and never below 0 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Precautions.** The Codex code lists the main ones:<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

1. Load only lots that are uniformly dried and below 12.5% moisture.
2. Check that containers are clean, dry and free of damage that lets water in.
3. Stack bags crossed over, so that no empty vertical columns ("chimneys") form.
4. Cover the top and sides of the bags with material that absorbs condensed water, such as silica gel or cardboard.
5. Keep the ventilation holes of the container free.
6. Stow away from the weather, not on the open deck, and away from boilers and heated tanks.

According to the 2021 study, more than 90% of European coffee imports travel in ventilated containers.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A marine insurers' guide recommends lining containers with heavy-duty kraft or corrugated paper.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In its container trials with black pepper, the 1996 study found pallets and roof insulation the most practicable remedies.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## What ageing does to green coffee

Green coffee changes slowly even in a good store. The sources describe four kinds of change: aroma, fats, cell membranes and colour.

**Aroma.** In a German study, Colombian arabica beans with 13.5% water (basis not stated) were kept for nine months at 40 °C in airtight bags.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Clove-like 2-methoxy-4-vinylphenol rose from 390 to 15,000 µg/kg, the fruity methyl 2-methylbutanoate from 9 to 500 µg/kg, and (E)-β-damascenone (cooked apple) from 0.8 to 29 µg/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> A smoky odorant new to coffee, 2-methoxy-5-vinylphenol, appeared at 250 µg/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Cooling to 12 °C prevented the smoky compound from forming, and lower water content was the most critical factor.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> At 11.75% water and 25 °C it had not formed even after 18 months.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The authors suggest drier beans combined with lower temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Fats.** Coffee oil is broken down by lipase, an enzyme that frees fatty acids.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Lipase activity was found in every green coffee tested, even in 10-year-old Brazilian coffees.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> At 25 °C, free fatty acids in coffee at its original moisture rose from 1.8 to about 3.8 g/kg in 18 months; in moistened coffee they reached almost 4.8 g/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In dried coffee the rise was small.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> A 10-year-old Brazilian coffee contained more than 30 g/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Under accelerated storage at 30–50 °C and 50% relative humidity, free fatty acids in the oil ranged from 3.82 mg KOH/g oil at the start to 9.51 within 20 days, and the peroxide value from 0.86 to 3.63 meq/kg oil.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Higher temperature lowered quality, and hermetic bags slowed oxidation more than woven plastic or polyethylene bags.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Whether these changes create the flavour of aged coffee is disputed (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Cell membranes.** When membranes break down, soaked beans leak more ions and potassium. The rising electrical conductivity of stored beans shows this disorganisation.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> In hulled coffee, mechanical damage from hulling and the missing fruit layers add to the loss of quality in store.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Colour.** Impermeable packaging kept the original colour of green coffee over 12 months.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In hermetic big bags the green colour even intensified.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to the German insurers' guide, fading colour that starts at the tips of the beans points to long storage.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The Colombian authors read colour change in store as a sign of oxidation and biochemical breakdown.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Hidden damage.** Damage can start before cuppers taste it. In an 18-month trial, natural coffee in permeable bags showed volatile markers of later sensory damage from 3 months of storage.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> This latent damage was more marked in natural than in pulped natural coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> In a Polish trial at −10 °C and 10 °C, the processing method had a greater effect on the results than the package.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Container physics

Green coffee in a closed space sets the humidity of the air between the beans; it works as a moisture reservoir.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In an airtight enclosure of green coffee, a 10 °C rise raises the equilibrium relative humidity by about 3%.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> When the roof cools, for example on a winter dockside, the air just beneath it rises to 100% relative humidity and dew forms.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The 1996 study advised that the air between the beans should never exceed 70% relative humidity during or after the voyage, and that the coffee should be drier than is needed at the port of export.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It recommended open-sided or naturally ventilated containers for coffee, cocoa and pepper.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Field trial.** In the same study, coffee travelled from Côte d'Ivoire to southern England in summer in a prototype container with continuous vents near floor and roof.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> No moisture problems were seen when the doors were opened, and the surface samples were drier than the interior.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The floor was damp where the sacks had stood.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The study judged the container suitable for summer shipments on that route.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Simulated voyage.** In a 2005 laboratory simulation in Brazil, condensation formed mainly on the container ceiling.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Water activity of beans in the upper region rose only from about 0.59 to 0.60–0.61, and the oven-method moisture gain was less than 0.3%.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The simulation used no solar radiation, which the authors suggest kept the moisture rise small.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The half-full container showed a stronger tendency towards defects than the others.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

## Pests in storage

The coffee bean weevil has a worldwide distribution.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Green coffee with insect damage counts as a defect and lowers its grade.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> A 2018 paper cites Brazilian reports from 1957 and 1973 of about 30% losses in coffee stored for 6 months and 20% in coffee stored for 9 months; the kind of loss is not stated.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> According to the German insurers' guide, containers and holds must be free of insects.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Monitoring by integrated pest management is the Codex recommendation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Measurement

Moisture is measured with the ISO 6673 oven method or calibrated meters, and water activity with dew-point or capacitance instruments; the moisture and water activity entry describes both. Storage trials add tests of the seed itself: electrical conductivity and potassium leaching for membrane damage,<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> instrumental colour,<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> cupping by certified panels,<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> oil oxidation indices such as free fatty acids and peroxide value,<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> and aroma compounds by gas chromatography.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

Methods can disagree. In the 2005 simulation, a weight-gain method gave a larger moisture rise in the upper layer, from about 10.3% to 11.9% (basis not stated), than the oven and capacitance methods.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The same coffee thus gave different answers by method.

## Risks and defects

- **Rewetting.** Condensation and water redistribution in containers can lead to mould and OTA.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Container rain.** Drops from a cold roof rewet the top layer.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Aged flavour.** Long or warm storage gives ageing defects, called *reposo* in Colombian trials.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Taint.** Green coffee takes up foreign odours; non-food goods do not belong in a coffee store.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Insects.** The coffee bean weevil damages stored beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>
- **Heat and cold.** Stowage near boilers or heated tanks is to be avoided,<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> and temperatures below 0 °C as well.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Loading moisture.** The Codex ceiling, a peer-reviewed 12% limit and an older 13% guide value do not agree (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Real voyages.** The sources consulted here give simulated voyages<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> and one older field trial.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Peer-reviewed logs of container climate and bean moisture on commercial coffee voyages were not found.
- **Fats and flavour.** Whether fat oxidation causes the flavour of aged green coffee is unresolved (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **Trade terms.** Terms such as "past crop" and "baggy" are not defined in any peer-reviewed or standards source consulted for this entry.
- **ISO 8455.** Only the scope of the standard could be read; its detailed requirements are not quoted here.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

<p><strong>Gallego et al., 2025 – Packaging and storage location.</strong> Green Arabica was stored for a year in fique, paper and six kinds of polymer packs at four Colombian sites, from cool highland stores to a hot coastal one. At the two cool sites several barrier packs held cup quality for the full year; at the hot site quality fell after 60 days in every pack.</p>
