## What the cup does

A cup is more than a container. It takes heat from the coffee, holds the air above it and shapes how a drinker meets the aroma.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Its material also touches the hot drink, so food-contact rules apply to it.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

This entry covers four things the vessel does: how it changes cooling, how its shape changes aroma, what the cupping standard specifies, and what the safety rules say about cup materials. How hot people like their coffee, and how flavour changes as it cools, is covered in *Serving temperature*. How colour, weight and shape change expectations is covered in *Crossmodal effects*.

## How the cup changes cooling

Coffee starts losing heat as soon as it is poured. A 2019 review states that pouring coffee or tea into a cup takes off 2–5 °C, based on experiments with several cup types.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Part of this loss happens because the liquid meets cooler cup walls.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The open surface then loses heat to the air by convection and evaporation.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

A cap, or lid, slows this down. The same review states that a cap roughly doubles the time a drink needs to cool.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2016 burn study compared drink volume, starting temperature, cooling time, cup material, a cap and a corrugated paper sleeve. The cap, the volume and the cooling time had the most influence.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

A 2017 review tabulates the effect. Drinks served at 95 °C in a room at about 20 °C were followed for 25 min, with and without a cap.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> After 10 min, 237 mL had fallen to 65.5 °C uncovered and to 75.3 °C with a cap.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A larger 473 mL drink cooled more slowly: 73.9 °C uncovered and 82.3 °C with a cap.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Warming a cup before pouring targets the loss at the wall, which the review names as one cause of cooling.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured how much a preheated cup changes the temperature of coffee.

## How the shape changes aroma

Aroma compounds leave hot coffee more readily than cool coffee. A coffee science textbook states that the aroma above the cup rises with temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Whether the cup’s shape changes what reaches the nose has been tested in one coffee study.

A 2018 study tested this with coffee. At a specialty coffee event in Brazil, 276 people tasted one pour-over coffee from one of three white ceramic cups.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The cups were the same height but differed in shape: a tulip with a narrower opening, an open cup and a “split” cup with an indentation.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Coffee in the tulip cup was rated more aromatic.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Coffee in the split cup was rated sweeter and more acidic; the consumers liked it least, the professionals did not.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

The vapour above a drink is not even. A 2015 study of wine imaged ethanol vapour above three glasses. In a wine glass, the vapour was strongest in a ring near the rim.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> No comparable imaging study of coffee cups was found for this entry.

How the colour, weight and texture of a cup change what people expect and perceive is a separate question. It is covered in *Crossmodal effects*.

## The cupping vessel

Cupping is the one setting where the vessel is specified. The SCA standard requires cupping vessels of tempered glass or ceramic, holding 200–350 mL, with a top diameter of 75–90 mm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> All cups in a session must be identical in volume, dimensions and material.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The SCA’s explanatory CVA document adds that they have lids.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The vessel also sets the dose. The volume to the rim can be found by weighing the water the cup holds.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The coffee dose is then 8.25 g per 150 mL of that capacity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> For a 240 mL vessel this gives 13.20 g.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How the cupping itself is done is covered in *Cupping*.

No standard consulted for this entry specifies the cup for espresso or other drinks. The traditional Italian espresso specification sets the amount in the cup, 13–26 g, not the cup itself.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Materials and safety

**Glazed ceramics.** Coloured glazes on ceramic tableware can contain heavy metals such as lead or cadmium, according to the BfR.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> How much is released depends mainly on the glaze, the firing temperature, the food and the contact time.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The law therefore limits the release, not the content.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In German law, deep ceramic vessels may release at most 4.0 mg of lead and 0.3 mg of cadmium per litre in the test.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Melamine resin and “bambooware”.** Some reusable coffee-to-go cups are made of melamine formaldehyde resin with bamboo fibres as filler.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The BfR found that about a quarter of such articles released exceptionally high levels of formaldehyde in its tests.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> It advises against hot meals and drinks from melamine resin tableware, whether conventional or bambooware.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Paper cups.** Disposable paper cups for hot drinks have a thin plastic lining; in a 2022 study it was low-density polyethylene.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Two studies found plastic particles released from such cups into hot water.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Their methods, particle sizes and counts differ (see the Deep dive). Contaminants in coffee itself are covered in *Contaminants and food safety*.

**Reusable or single-use.** No life-cycle study of reusable and single-use cups could be checked for this entry. The footprint of the coffee itself is covered in *Carbon footprint of coffee*.

## Heat flow in a cup

The 2019 review names the losses: the cooler wall takes heat when the drink is poured, and the open surface loses heat by convection and evaporation.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A cap covers the open surface. In the 2017 tables, a capped 237 mL drink was still at 68.8 °C after 15 min; the uncovered one was at 65.5 °C after 10 min.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Volume also matters. In the same tables, an uncovered 473 mL drink cooled about as slowly as a capped 237 mL one over the first 10 min (our reading of Tables 3 and 4).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The 2016 study, which included cup material and a paper sleeve among its variables, found cap, volume and time most influential.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Only its abstract was read for this entry, so its values for single cup materials are not given here. The 2017 review, drawing on the same work, reports that the cup’s construction and an insulating sleeve had a very small impact on its results, and leaves them out of its tables.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Warm cups.** The 2019 review names the cooler wall as one reason why poured drinks lose heat.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Warming the cup removes part of that loss. No study consulted for this entry measured the size of the effect for coffee cups, and no sourced heat capacities for cup materials were found, so we give no calculation here.

**Table data and real cafés.** The 2017 tables start at 95 °C, hotter than most coffee in the cup. They do not say which values were measured and which calculated.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> They are useful for comparing cap and volume, not for predicting a particular café cup.

## Shape, headspace and aroma

**Physics.** Volatile compounds dissolve better in cooler liquid. A hot brew therefore releases more of them into the air above the cup, according to the textbook chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> This air above the liquid is called the headspace.

**Coffee study.** In the 2018 cup-shape study, the three cups were white, smooth ceramic, 6.5 cm high and close in weight.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The tulip cup had a 6.0 cm opening; the open and split cups had 8 cm openings.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Each participant received about 60 mL of a V60 brew, served at a mean of 58.2 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Of the 276 analysed participants, 151 worked in specialty coffee and 125 were experienced consumers. Each person rated one cup only.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Aroma was rated highest in the tulip cup; sweetness and acidity were higher in the split cup, which the consumers liked less than the professionals did.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

The authors name limits of their design. Cup weights were not matched; taste expectations were not measured; and only one single-origin Arabica was tested.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The study measured ratings, not the vapour above the cups. Whether the tulip cup concentrated aroma or changed expectations is not settled by it.

**Vapour imaging.** The 2015 wine study shows that headspace vapour can be uneven and shaped by the glass.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> It imaged ethanol, not coffee aroma compounds, and wine at about 13 °C, not hot coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> It is a pointer to a method, not evidence about coffee cups.

## Food-contact rules for cup materials

**Ceramics.** In the EU, lead and cadmium release from ceramic articles is limited by Council Directive 84/500/EEC of 1984, amended in 2005.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In Germany the limits are set out in Annex 6 of the consumer goods ordinance.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Limits depend on the article: per area for flat or shallow items, per litre for deeper ones.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> For a ceramic container with a ceramic lid, the container and the inner lid surface are tested separately under the same conditions; the two results are summed and related to the container alone.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A single test item may exceed a limit by up to 50% if at least three other items of the same material, form, size, decoration and glaze average at or below it, and none of them exceeds it by more than 50%.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In 2005 the BfR recommended lowering the legal limits, noting that a vessel at the limit could release up to 4 mg of lead per litre of food.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Melamine formaldehyde resin.** The BfR’s 2020 opinion found that the release of melamine and formaldehyde at levels relevant for health occurs only at high temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Its hot-fill tests ran for 2 hours at 70 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Melamine release from bambooware was about twice the average of conventional melamine tableware.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The material appears to degrade with repeated contact with hot liquids, according to the BfR. It considers such tableware unsuitable for the microwave but suitable for food at room temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Plastic-lined paper cups.** The two studies differ in what they measured:

- **A 2021 study from India.** Water at 85–90 °C stood in disposable paper cups for 15 min. About 25,000 particles of 10–1,000 µm were counted per 100 mL.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The paper itself was not consulted for this entry; these values come from the summary published by its authors’ university.
- **A 2022 study from the United States.** Hot beverage cups lined with low-density polyethylene released nanoparticles at above 10¹² per litre when exposed to water.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Mean diameters were 30–80 nm, with few particles above 200 nm. Release depended on water temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

Neither study, as read for this entry, assessed health effects in people. No food-safety authority consulted for this entry has set a limit for particles released from cups.

## Open questions

- **Cup material.** No study consulted for this entry compares the cooling of coffee in porcelain, stoneware, glass, double-walled glass and paper under the same conditions with published values.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> One review reports only that cup construction had a very small impact on its results.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Reusable or single-use.** No life-cycle comparison of cups was checked for this entry; how such studies set functional units and boundaries is explained in *Carbon footprint of coffee*, and rules for packaging in *Packaging*.
- **Warm cups.** The effect of warming a cup beforehand on coffee temperature, and on flavour, was not measured in the sources consulted here.
- **Aroma in the headspace.** Cup shape changed aroma ratings in one coffee study.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured coffee aroma compounds above cups of different shapes.
- **Revised ceramic limits.** The limits in the German ordinance are those of the 1984 directive.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Whether lower EU limits have since been adopted was not established from the sources consulted for this entry.
- **Particles from cup linings.** The two paper-cup studies counted different size ranges with different methods.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Their results cannot be compared directly, and exposure from drinking was not assessed.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Carvalho & Spence, 2018 – Cup shape and specialty coffee.</strong> At a specialty coffee event in Brazil, 276 professionals and experienced consumers each tasted one pour-over coffee from a tulip, open or split ceramic cup. The tulip cup gave higher aroma ratings; the split cup gave higher sweetness and acidity but lower liking, mainly among consumers.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Quality and Preference 68, 315–321 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.04.003">doi:10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.04.003</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bourdon et al., 2017 – Scald injuries and cooling.</strong> A review on scald burns that tabulates the cooling of 237 and 473 mL drinks served at 70–95 °C, with and without a protective cap. A cap and a larger volume both kept the drinks hotter for longer.</p>
<p class="meta">World Journal of Dermatology 6(2), 17–26 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.5314/wjd.v6.i2.17">doi:10.5314/wjd.v6.i2.17</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Abraham et al., 2016 – Predicting scalds from beverage spills.</strong> Cooling experiments varied drink volume, starting temperature, cooling time, cup material, a cap and a corrugated paper sleeve, and fed a model of burn depth. The cap, the volume and the cooling time had the largest effect on drink temperature.</p>
<p class="meta">International Journal of Hyperthermia 32(8), 900–910 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/02656736.2016.1211752">doi:10.1080/02656736.2016.1211752</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>BfR, 2020 – Melamine resin and bambooware.</strong> The German risk assessment institute evaluated migration tests on melamine formaldehyde resin tableware, including coffee-to-go cups with bamboo filler. About a quarter of bambooware articles released exceptionally high levels of formaldehyde, and the institute advises against hot food and drinks from this material.</p>
<p class="meta">BfR Opinion No 046/2019, 25 November 2020 · <a href="https://www.bfr.bund.de/cm/349/fillable-articles-made-from-melamine-formaldehyde-resin.pdf">bfr.bund.de</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Zangmeister et al., 2022 – Nanoparticles from single-use products.</strong> Scientists at the US National Institute of Standards and Technology measured particles released into water by single-use products, including hot beverage cups lined with low-density polyethylene. The cups released more than 10¹² particles per litre, mostly 30–80 nm across, with release depending on water temperature.</p>
<p class="meta">Environmental Science & Technology 56(9) · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.1c06768">doi:10.1021/acs.est.1c06768</a></p>
</div>
