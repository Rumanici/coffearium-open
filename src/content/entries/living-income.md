## What a living income is

A **living income** is the net annual income a household in a particular place needs for a decent standard of living for all its members.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> This is the definition of the Living Income Community of Practice, a network that works on the concept.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A decent standard of living includes food, water, housing, education, healthcare, transport and clothing.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It also includes other essential needs, with a provision for unexpected events.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The concept looks at the household, not at one crop. Its income can come from several sources: coffee, other crops, livestock, wages or money sent home by relatives.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> “Net” means after the costs of farming are paid.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

A living income is not the same as the poverty line. The ICO-commissioned benchmark for Honduras lies above both the national and the international poverty lines.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## How a benchmark is built

A **living income benchmark** puts a money value on a decent standard of living in one place and year. Fairtrade sets the cost of a decent living with the Anker methodology, and the ICO has commissioned Anker benchmarks for coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The method was designed for living wages and is also applied to living income.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It adds up four costs:<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

1. **Food:** a nutritious, low-cost model diet that fits local food preferences, priced at local markets.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
2. **Housing:** the cost of healthy housing that meets local and international standards of decency.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
3. **Other essential needs:** healthcare, education, transport, clothing and communication, from household expenditure surveys.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
4. **Unexpected events:** a small margin added to the other three.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The total applies to a **reference household**, whose size the method sets from local data.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Household size changes the result a great deal. Fairtrade’s benchmark for Colombia assumes four people; its benchmarks for Uganda and Ethiopia assume seven.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Not every published coffee benchmark is an Anker study. For Uganda, Ethiopia, Honduras and Aceh, Fairtrade combined earlier estimates from other methods and labelled some results provisional.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In June 2023 the International Coffee Organization (ICO) announced that it was working with the Anker Research Institute on benchmarks for Ethiopia, Indonesia, Mexico and Vietnam.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Measuring the gap

The **living income gap** is the difference between the benchmark and a household’s actual total net income.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Actual income includes net farm income, off-farm income and other income such as remittances.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The value of food the household grows and eats itself can be counted too.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

A gap can be given for the average household or for the middle one. The guidance for the Community of Practice asks researchers at least to report the gap of the **median** income, as a share of the benchmark.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The median is less affected by a few very rich or very poor households.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Many studies estimate income from coffee alone. A 2021 report that compared ten producing countries did so, and states that it leaves out other household income.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Such figures show how far coffee covers the benchmark, not the full household gap.

## What coffee studies have found

Measured coffee data are scarce. The studies consulted for this entry cover a few countries, years and samples; none of them speaks for “coffee” as a whole.

- **Kenya.** A 2024 working paper surveyed 340 arabica households in Kenya (July 2020 to June 2021); 9% earned a living income.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> A separate 2025 study of 181 coffee farmers in cooperatives in Nyeri and Murang’a (2021/22 season) found average earnings of 35% of the benchmark, with about 93% of farmers below it.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>
- **Vietnam.** The 2024 working paper also surveyed robusta households in Dak Lak and reports a much larger share with a living income than in Kenya.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> No independent study consulted for this entry checks its Vietnamese values, so they are not given here.
- **Ten producing countries.** A 2021 model at 2018–2019 prices found that, of ten major producing countries, only in Brazil did the average producer’s net coffee income exceed some living income estimates.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>
- **Colombia.** A study of over 600 farmers in two Fairtrade cooperatives found that most did not break even on coffee, whatever their certification.<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup>

In the 2024 working paper the mean Kenyan household earned USD 1,235 a year against a mean benchmark of USD 3,395, about 36% of it (our calculation from the study’s means).<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> This is close to the 35% the independent 2025 study found in central Kenya.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Means hide the spread: in both studies more than nine in ten households were below the benchmark.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

## Living Income Reference Prices

Fairtrade publishes **Living Income Reference Prices** for coffee. Such a price is what a typical household would need for a living income from its crop. It assumes a viable farm size and a sustainable yield.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It combines four parameters: the cost of a decent living, a target yield, the cost of sustainable production and a viable farm size.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

As of October 2026, Fairtrade lists coffee reference prices for Colombia, Ethiopia, Honduras, Indonesia, Nicaragua, Peru and Uganda, from 2022 and 2023.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Each is set at the farm gate in local currency, per kilogram of cherry or parchment coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Several notes add an indicative FOB (free on board) price per pound of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

A reference price is not a minimum price. Fairtrade calls it “just one tool” and does not count the Fairtrade Premium towards it.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The binding Fairtrade Minimum Price for conventional washed arabica is USD 2.00 per lb FOB from 1 December 2026; the organic differential adds USD 0.40, giving USD 2.40 for organic (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> How the minimum price and Premium work is explained in Certifications and labels. How the export price is shared out along the chain is covered in Who earns what along the chain.

## Benchmarks: methods and limits

**Household model.** A benchmark is the cost of living of one reference household divided among the earners the method assumes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The Anker methodology divides the total cost by the number of workers in a typical family, a number between 1 and 2.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The ICO’s Honduras study assumes 1.5 full-time workers for a household of two adults and 2.6 children.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> A seven-person benchmark, as in Fairtrade’s notes for Uganda and Ethiopia, cannot be compared directly with a four-person one.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Method and age.** Fairtrade’s Colombian, Peruvian and Nicaraguan benchmarks come from Anker Research Institute studies.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Its Ugandan, Ethiopian, Honduran and Indonesian benchmarks combine earlier estimates and are marked provisional or preliminary in three cases.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Older benchmarks are brought forward with inflation rates.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The ICO’s guidance first asks whether an existing benchmark can be used for a region before a new study is made.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Measuring actual income.** The gap of the mean is the mean benchmark minus the mean total household income; the same is done with medians.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The guidance also defines an index that averages each household’s shortfall below the benchmark, counting zero for households above it.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Income is net of agricultural production costs, including inputs and paid labour.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The guidance consulted for this entry does not say whether the household’s own labour is given a value.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Coffee-only estimates.** The 2021 ten-country report modelled coffee income from prices and assumptions of 2018–2019 and left out other income.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Its figures are therefore not measured household gaps, and this entry does not chart them.

## Levers studied for closing the gap

Studies discuss four levers: price, yield, costs and other sources of income. The evidence below comes only from the studies consulted for this entry; we do not rank the levers.

**Price.** In central Kenya, doubling both farm-gate price and yield, together with 50% more non-coffee income, cut the share of households below the benchmark from about 93% to about 67%.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The IFPRI authors find it “virtually impossible” for many smallholders to reach a living income from coffee alone.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The authors of the 2024 working paper estimate, for their Kenyan sample, a theoretical farm-gate price several times the 2020 price, at which many households would still fall short.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> They argue that large price increases paid at scale need to be coupled with supply management.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

**Costs.** A peer-reviewed study compared costs and earnings of coffee farms in Costa Rica and Guatemala in 2008/09 and 2019/20, in US dollars with 2008 values adjusted for inflation.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> The 2019/20 survey returned to farms of the first survey, replacing some with similar nearby farms.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> Its abstract reports that agronomic costs per hectare rose by 31% in Costa Rica and 62% in Guatemala, mainly through labour costs.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> For the farms surveyed in both periods, its results give rises of 64% and 66% and attribute Guatemala’s rise mostly to inputs.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> Net income per hectare (EBITDA) rose by about 30% in Costa Rica, where farm-gate prices rose by 13.6%, and fell to about a third of its 2008 level in Guatemala, where they fell by 13.8%.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>

**Yield and farm size.** Each reference price assumes a target yield and a viable farm size; actual farms can be smaller.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2021 study of cocoa and tea households, not coffee, found that farms would have to be four to almost eight times larger to reach a living income, other things equal.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> The same study found that the poorest farmers gain least from price rises, because they produce the least.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> How yields are measured in coffee is explained in Coffee yields.

**Other income.** Coffee made up on average 46% of household income in the Kenyan sample of the 2024 working paper and 45% in the 2025 central Kenya study.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The authors of the working paper call other income opportunities that pay as well as coffee “essential”.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The 2021 cocoa and tea study concludes that there are “no silver bullets”.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup>

## Living wage for hired workers

A **living wage** is pay for a standard work week that lets a worker in a particular place afford a decent standard of living for the worker and the family.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It applies to hired workers, on farms or elsewhere; living income applies to any earner, such as self-employed farmers.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Both start from the same cost of a decent living, because that cost does not depend on how the income is earned.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A living wage is stated per worker, gross, before deductions; a living income is stated per household, net, from all sources.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In the ICO’s Honduras study, the gross living wage is HNL 12,775 per month per full-time worker, above the agricultural minimum wages the study lists.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> A coffee household can need a living income itself and also hire seasonal workers who need a living wage. Fairtrade’s Colombian reference price, for example, counts the pay of harvest workers as a production cost.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Open questions

- What share of coffee-producing households earns a living income in most producing countries. No peer-reviewed study consulted for this entry measured it for a whole country.
- How the provisional benchmarks for Ethiopia and Honduras compare with the Anker benchmarks the ICO planned with the Anker Research Institute.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- How many buyers pay Living Income Reference Prices, and for what volume. No source consulted for this entry reports it.
- Whether, and how, the household’s own labour should be valued in the gap; the guidance consulted for this entry does not settle it.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Waarts, Suter and van Asten, 2024 – Living income in Kenya and Vietnam.</strong> Compared arabica households in Kenya and robusta households in Dak Lak, Vietnam, with household-specific benchmarks. Fewer than one in ten Kenyan households earned a living income; the paper also expresses the benchmark per working day. A working paper, not stated to be peer reviewed, with two of three authors from a coffee trading company; its Kenyan results agree with the IFPRI study below.</p>
<p class="meta">Wageningen University &amp; Research, working paper, October 2024 · <a href="https://edepot.wur.nl/675187">edepot.wur.nl/675187</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Boukaka and Benfica, 2025 – Living income gaps in central Kenya.</strong> Surveyed coffee farmers organised in cooperatives in Nyeri and Murang’a. Average earnings were about a third of the benchmark; doubling prices and yields narrowed the gap, but most households stayed below it. Not externally reviewed.</p>
<p class="meta">IFPRI Discussion Paper 2342 · <a href="https://hdl.handle.net/10568/175180">hdl:10568/175180</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Leiva et al., 2024 – Coffee economics in Costa Rica and Guatemala, 2008–2019.</strong> Revisited farms surveyed in 2008/09 and compared their costs and earnings in 2019/20. Rising costs were absorbed in Costa Rica, where farm-gate prices rose, but net income fell sharply in Guatemala, where they fell.</p>
<p class="meta">Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems 8, 1376051 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fsufs.2024.1376051">doi:10.3389/fsufs.2024.1376051</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Waarts et al., 2021 – Pathways to a living income for tree-crop farmers.</strong> Analysed cocoa households in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire and tea households in Kenya. Most were below a living income; no single lever closed the gap, and farms would need to be four to almost eight times larger on their own. Not a coffee study, but often cited for the method.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Security 13(6), 1467–1496 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s12571-021-01220-5">doi:10.1007/s12571-021-01220-5</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Dietz et al., 2020 – Multiple certification in Colombia.</strong> Surveyed over 600 coffee farmers in two Fairtrade cooperatives. Additional certificates brought limited economic gains, and most farmers did not break even, whatever their certification.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Development Studies 56(6), 1141–1160 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/00220388.2019.1632433">doi:10.1080/00220388.2019.1632433</a></p>
</div>
