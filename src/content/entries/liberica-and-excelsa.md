## Two coffees from one old name

Most coffee comes from two species, arabica (*Coffea arabica*) and robusta (*C. canephora*).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Liberica and excelsa are two further coffee trees from the African lowlands. Until 2025 most botanists treated them as one species, *C. liberica*.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

A 2025 study of their genomes, leaves, flowers, fruits and seeds treats them as separate species. Liberica keeps the name *C. liberica*. Excelsa becomes *C. dewevrei*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A third, little-known species, *C. klainei*, was split off at the same time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Liberica is native to Upper West Africa, from Sierra Leone and Liberia to Nigeria. Excelsa is native to Central Africa, from Cameroon to South Sudan and Uganda.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the trade, the name “excelsa” is often given to small-seeded liberica, especially in Asia.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> So a coffee sold as excelsa is not always *C. dewevrei*.

## Trees with large fruit

Unlike arabica and robusta, excelsa grows into a medium-sized tree, around 10 m or more in Uganda.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Its leaves are large: 29.5 cm long and 14 cm wide on average. Liberica leaves average 22.8 × 8.2 cm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Liberica fruits are large. They measure up to 3.4 cm long, with a pulp (the fleshy layer under the skin) 4–9.5 mm thick.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Excelsa fruits are smaller, with a thinner pulp and smaller seeds. They grow in tight clusters along the branches.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The fruits also take longer to ripen. In Uganda, excelsa fruit develops for longer than arabica and robusta fruit. Its main harvest, from January to April, does not overlap with theirs.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## From rescue crop to niche

In the late 19th century, coffee leaf rust devastated arabica growing. The coffee industry turned to liberica from about 1875 to 1900, then to robusta from the early 1900s.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2026 study of liberica in Indonesia dates its wide spread to the 1870s, as an answer to the rust.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to a 2000 plant handbook, liberica also proved susceptible to leaf rust there, and robusta replaced it.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Liberica stayed in South-East Asia. It is grown in the lowlands of Malaysia, the Philippines and Indonesia.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the Philippines, liberica is known locally as barako.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Excelsa has a newer story. In lowland Uganda, farmers have shifted to it, perhaps since at least the 1980s. The 2023 review links this to disease, pests and drought in robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A similar increase is reported in South Sudan.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> As of 2025, liberica is seeing a renaissance in Malaysia, Indonesia and Fiji, and attention on excelsa centres on Uganda, South Sudan and Guinea.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Heat and drought

According to the 2025 study, excelsa can crop under higher temperatures and longer dry spells than robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Uganda, the places where it is recorded have a modelled mean annual temperature of 24.4 °C. Robusta records there average 21.8 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The review says excelsa may be more heat tolerant than robusta, but field trials are needed.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Liberica is farmed mostly in warm, wet lowlands, at 10–500 m.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

According to the 2025 study, liberica cultivation is increasing as climate change puts pressure on arabica and robusta. Excelsa production in India is rising for the same reason.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, runs a research project on excelsa in Uganda with Ugandan partners.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Processing and the cup

Liberica fruits are hard to pulp (strip of skin and pulp), because they are large and have a hard skin.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Excelsa can be processed with standard procedures. Its outturn, the weight of clean coffee from a given weight of fruit, is much better.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2023 review gives 7 kg of fresh fruit for 1 kg of clean coffee in Uganda.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

No controlled sensory study of the two species was consulted for this entry. The sources describe their flavour in general terms only. A 2025 study says liberica and excelsa have contrasting flavour profiles.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2023 review describes Ugandan and South Sudanese excelsa as smooth, with low to medium acidity and low bitterness.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> An older handbook describes liberica as more bitter than arabica and robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## From two varieties to three species

The species name carries the author citation *C. liberica* Hiern.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Until 2025 the consensus of taxonomic study was one species with two botanical varieties, var. *liberica* and var. *dewevrei*.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> An alternative view treated *C. liberica* as one species with no subdivisions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The 2025 study combined genome-wide data, morphology and cleaned distribution records. It supports three species: *C. liberica*, *C. dewevrei* and *C. klainei*. *C. dewevrei* and *C. klainei* were reinstated as older names, which raised the count of known *Coffea* species from 131 to 133.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same group published a perspective on liberica as a crop in 2022, “The re-emergence of Liberica coffee as a major crop plant”, in Nature Plants.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> That paper was not consulted for this entry; facts from it are cited through the 2023 review that summarises it.

The “excelsa” name causes confusion in trade. A 2023 review restricts it to plants of var. *dewevrei* from eastern Cameroon, the Central African Republic, eastern DR Congo and western Uganda. It notes that it is often applied to small- to medium-seeded liberica, particularly in Asia.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In Uganda, excelsa is mostly exported as robusta or mixed with it, partly because it is taken for a large-leaved robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The same review records a historical case of the confusion: field trials of “excelsa” in Uganda in 1915 and 1916 gave poor yields. The material, imported from Java in 1914, was liberica, judging by its large seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Production as of 2026

Online sources often state that liberica and excelsa supply 1–2% of world coffee. The 2025 study calls this incorrect: those shares date from the late 19th century, when *C. liberica* stood with arabica as the second most important coffee of commerce.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its authors put present production of the two species at probably less than 1,000 tonnes. Against about 10 million tonnes of arabica and robusta exported in 2024, that is about 0.01% of world exports.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No newer global estimate was found for this entry as of October 2026.

Excelsa production is rising in India, and in Vietnam and Indonesia, where it supplements robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Uganda, a separate market for excelsa did not exist in 2023.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Climate tolerance in detail

Field observations suggest that *C. dewevrei* has considerable drought tolerance, particularly compared with *C. canephora*. Its precipitation needs in cultivation have not been established in trials.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2023 review describes excelsa as more heat tolerant than wild and cultivated arabica, which grows at a mean annual temperature of 18–22 °C. It adds that its own climate analysis does not support such observations with confidence.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Excelsa has replaced robusta in some areas of Uganda, probably as a result of climate change.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Excelsa also yields more than liberica, because each tree bears more fruit. Its thinner pulp and parchment improve the outturn and the profitability of the harvest compared with liberica.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Diseases and breeding

According to a 2017 textbook chapter, *C. liberica* was planted less than *C. canephora* because of its sensitivity to *Fusarium xylarioides*, the cause of coffee wilt disease.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Coffee wilt disease was first reported on excelsa (var. *dewevrei*) in the Central African Republic in 1927. It later caused widespread damage to liberica and robusta across large areas of tropical Africa.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> As of 2023, no coffee wilt disease had been reported on excelsa in Uganda, but its level of resistance still needs research.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

According to the 2000 plant handbook, liberica is susceptible to leaf rust, though much less so than the other coffees.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Its genes have served arabica breeding. In Indonesia, crosses of liberica with arabica gave tetraploid and octoploid hybrids, notably “Kalimas” and “Kawisari”, with a high degree of leaf rust resistance. In India, such hybrids showed good yield potential and rust resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Grafting results differ by study (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Conservation status

According to the 2025 study, the IUCN Red List assesses *C. liberica* as Least Concern, in an assessment that reports natural occurrence in 17 countries. The study suggests that the narrower *C. liberica*, native to five countries, may warrant reassessment as Vulnerable. By its own assessment, *C. dewevrei*, with an extent of occurrence of 2,464,990 km² and an area of occupancy of 456 km², falls into Least Concern.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Uganda, a draft national assessment in 2023 placed excelsa as Endangered.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Precipitation needs of excelsa.** Its drought tolerance rests on field observations; trials of its water needs are still required.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Coffee wilt resistance in excelsa.** No disease reports from Uganda as of 2023, but its level of resistance still needs research.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Flavour of the two species.** No controlled sensory comparison of *C. liberica* and *C. dewevrei* was consulted for this entry; published descriptions are general.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
