## Two principles: percolation and immersion

Most non-espresso brew methods move soluble coffee into water in one of two ways.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Percolation (flow-through).** Fresh water flows into the grounds, and extracted brew flows out the other side.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a pour-over or drip machine, the water passes through the coffee bed mainly by gravity, and the filter holds the grounds back.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Because new water keeps arriving, the liquid around the particles is continually replaced.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The balance of the brew changes for as long as water keeps passing through the bed.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Full immersion (steeping).** All the water is added to the coffee at once.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The French press is the classic example; cupping, toddy-style cold brewers and Japanese vacuum (siphon) brewers are full immersion methods too.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a French press, there is no pressure driving extraction: solubles simply diffuse from the floating particles into the solution.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> As the liquid grows stronger, extraction slows, and the brew approaches an equilibrium.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The difference matters for control. Full immersion gives precise control of strength (TDS) through the ratio, but little control of extraction yield; flow methods such as drip brewing give more flexibility.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A lower yield in full immersion can only be reached by cutting the brew short.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> How strength and yield are measured and calculated for each principle is explained in the extraction yield and TDS entry.

**Hybrids.** Some methods combine the two. The AeroPress starts as an immersion brew; a piston then adds pressure at the end, and the brew is filtered through paper.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Some brewer baskets hold the water back at first, so the grounds steep, and then open the drain: in one patented design a valve blocks the drain hole until the rising water reaches a helical bimetallic spring, which extends and lifts the valve.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Boiled coffee (Turkish or cezve coffee) is a separate case: the coffee is heated with the water to boiling, and the grounds are left to settle rather than being filtered out.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## What you control

The same variables apply to every method, but they act differently in each.

- **Ratio.** Stronger coffee at the same extraction yield is made by using more coffee per litre; it is not more bitter, it simply contains more solids.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In full immersion, the ratio sets the TDS almost directly.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Grind.** Each method needs its own grind size. In a pour-over, the grind sets how fast water percolates and how long it stays in contact with the coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Finer particles extract more efficiently, but too fine a grind can hinder flow and lead to over-extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Grinding is covered in its own entry.
- **Time.** In percolation, contact time follows from the grind and the shape and depth of the bed.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In immersion it is chosen: in a French press, longer steeps may enhance bitterness and intensity, shorter ones acidity and sweetness.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Temperature.** Hotter water extracts faster.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In full immersion between 80 and 99 °C it changed how quickly equilibrium was reached, not the final strength.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Agitation and pour pattern.** In a pour-over, water is added by hand or by a machine, some of which deliver set amounts at set temperatures over time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured the effect of pour pattern, pulse pouring or stirring on extraction, so this entry gives no rules for them. How the pour moves the bed is covered in [Pour-over technique](/entries/pour-over-technique/).
- **Bloom.** No source consulted for this entry tested the effect of a pre-wetting (bloom) step on extraction. Why fresh coffee releases gas is covered in the freshness entry; what the gas does during the bloom is set out in the [pour-over technique](/entries/pour-over-technique/) entry.
- **Water.** Hardness, acidity and cation composition can directly influence the result.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The water entry covers this.

## Pour-over and drip devices

In a pour-over, the coffee is ground at coarser particle sizes so that water passes through by gravity alone (the same textbook's table lists the pour-over grind as medium), and placed in a holder with a filter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Filters come in many sizes, shapes and materials, which lets the brewer control the shape of the bed and the degree of filtration.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Cone or flat bed.** A deep, narrow bed extracts differently from a wide, shallow one, because the contact time between water and coffee differs.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Many filter systems use a cone-shaped filter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Modelling shows that flow through a cylindrical bed is close to one-dimensional, while a truncated cone causes non-uniform flow and significant variation in local extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Where the cone is narrow, water spends less time in contact with the grains.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Filter paper.** Paper absorbs oil very efficiently, so paper-filtered brews are low in fat.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same holds for the cholesterol-raising diterpenes cafestol and kahweol (see Filtration and body).<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Bypass.** Over-extraction can be avoided by stopping percolation after a planned time and adding hot water to the brew, instead of letting all the water pass through the grounds.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Materials and preheating.** No study consulted for this entry measured how dripper material or preheating changes the slurry temperature, so no figures are given.

## Automatic drip and batch brewers

An automatic drip machine does the pouring: it heats the water and delivers it over the bed, and some can be programmed to deliver set amounts at set temperatures over time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In one comparison, a filter machine brewed 100 g of coffee with 1,800 mL of water at about 90 °C in 348 s, through a paper filter.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Its coffee was relatively weak but very well balanced.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**What certification asks for.** The SCA home-brewer standard (SCA 310-2021) sets test conditions and requirements for electrical filter brewers for home use.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

- A test ratio of 55 g of coffee per 1.000 kg of fresh, cold water at 17 ± 3 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- A slurry temperature of 90–96 °C, reached by the moment 33% of the water has been sprayed and held for the rest of the brew cycle.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- A target brew strength of 1.15–1.55% TDS at the test ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- A test grind defined by sieve fractions. For conical baskets: less than 12% (by mass) above 850 µm, at least 68% between 425 and 850 µm, and 20% or less below 425 µm; flat baskets have their own, coarser specification.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- If the holding receptacle (carafe) is heated: 80–85 °C during the first 30 minutes of holding, and a heating element must never raise the coffee above 85 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

The standard sets no limit on total brew time.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Immersion methods

**French press.** Coarse coffee and hot water are mixed and left in contact for a set time, chosen by the brewer; a plunger with a filter then separates the grounds from the brew.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the nine-method comparison, the steep lasted 240 s.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Fines and the relatively inefficient metal mesh leave more sediment than drip filtering, and pressing squeezes oil from the bed into the brew.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the nine-method comparison, the French press had more body and more bitterness than the two other filter methods.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Cupping.** Cupping is a full immersion brew.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Its cupping-style yields are discussed in the extraction yield and TDS entry; the protocol belongs to the cupping entry.

**AeroPress.** A press that starts as immersion and ends with piston pressure through a paper filter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its patent describes a hollow cylinder with a perforated cap; pressing the plunger pressurises the air above the liquid, which forces the brew through the filter.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Siphon (vacuum brewer).** Classed with full immersion methods in the equilibrium study.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry gives its brewing parameters.

**Cezve or ibrik (Turkish coffee).** Freshly roasted beans are ground to a fine powder; the coffee, cold water and sugar go into a pot and are brewed slowly on a stove to produce foam.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Extraction relies mainly on diffusion at high temperature, which also extracts less soluble compounds; the textbook links these to the typical intense, bitter, dark-chocolate flavours; the grounds must settle, and some sediment reaches the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Turkish coffee culture and tradition was inscribed on UNESCO’s list of intangible cultural heritage in 2013.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Cold brew

Cold brew uses water at room temperature or lower; pour-over, immersion and French press versions exist.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Typical steeping times run from 8 to 24 hours at 20–25 °C or colder.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Without heat, fewer of the low-polarity compounds, such as coffee oil, are extracted, while highly polar compounds still extract rather well.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Cold brew needs a long time to reach a sufficient yield, but loses fewer volatiles by evaporation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At 21–25 °C, 3-CGA (a chlorogenic acid) rose quickly over the first 180 minutes, and both caffeine and 3-CGA reached equilibrium between 6 and 7 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Cold brew is often called less acidic. Measurements are more nuanced: cold and hot brews had comparable pH in two studies<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> but higher pH in cold brew in a third<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>, and in two studies cold brew had lower titratable acidity.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Long brewing can also give oxidised flavours unless exposure to air is controlled.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> More in the [cold brew](/entries/cold-brew/) entry.

## Extraction kinetics: why the principles diverge

**Two stages inside each grain.** Models of coffee extraction describe two processes: rapid dissolution of coffee from the grain surfaces, and much slower diffusion through the pore network inside the grain.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The slow stage is attributed to the greater resistance to mass transfer in the intact cells of larger grains.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In a packed bed, as described in the group’s earlier work, extraction comes from two domains: easily accessible coffee in fines and on the broken surfaces of larger grains, and less accessible coffee in intact cells.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Immersion: a closed system.** In a well-mixed suspension, the model passes quickly through the surface-dissolution stage and then slowly towards a stable equilibrium.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Experiments agree. The TDS of full immersion coffee rises with time and then plateaus: at a brew ratio of 25 (water to coffee) after about 20 minutes, at a ratio of 5 only after about 45 minutes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> At equilibrium, the extraction yield is about 21% over a wide range of brew ratios, and TDS is roughly inversely proportional to the ratio; the authors conclude that higher yields cannot be obtained with a single full immersion brew.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Cupping-style brews in the same paper nevertheless reached 23.9 ± 0.6% (22.2 ± 0.4% for decaffeinated coffee).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Percolation: an open system.** In a drip filter, hot water flows down through the bed under gravity, leaching soluble content.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The brew leaves the bed as it forms, so its composition keeps shifting as more water passes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> This is why percolation is the more flexible principle, and why bypass is one way to stop it before the late, bitter fraction dominates.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Uniformity.** A single yield figure hides how evenly the bed was extracted. Fines have a larger surface-to-volume ratio than coarse grains, so they reach a different yield.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> For a fine grind in the cylindrical chamber, the model predicted a variation of about 5 percentage points in yield across the bed, with the average in the target range.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The authors expect a high variation in yield to add bitter flavours; the study did not test this.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the conical bed, flow deviated significantly from one-dimensional, and local extraction varied significantly.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

What the two principles mean for yield arithmetic – retained liquid, beverage-based versus equilibrium formulas – is set out in the extraction yield and TDS entry.

## Filtration and body

The filter decides what besides dissolved solids reaches the cup. A metal mesh lets fines and oil through; paper absorbs oil very efficiently.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Mouthfeel depends partly on suspended fines, particularly in French press and espresso.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> But oil alone does not explain body: French press coffee is high in oil yet is not generally perceived as fuller, probably because it is served as a long, less concentrated cup.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Diterpenes.** Cafestol and kahweol are cholesterol-raising diterpenes found in unfiltered coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A 2025 study measured them across brew methods:

| Brew | Cafestol (mg/L) | Kahweol (mg/L) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Home-brewed, paper-filtered (five brands, two filter types) | 12 (4–24), range | 8 (3–19) | Orrje et al.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> |
| Percolator | 91.2 | 69.2 | Orrje et al.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> |
| French press | 86.8 | 68.7 | Orrje et al.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> |
| Boiled coffee | 939 | 678 | Orrje et al.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> |
| Boiled coffee poured through a fabric filter | 28 | 21 | Orrje et al.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> |
| Workplace brewing machines (n = 11) | 176 (24–444), median and range | 142 (18–434) | Orrje et al.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> |

One of four espresso samples reached 2,447 mg/L cafestol; the authors could not explain the variation between them.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Espresso cups are small, so concentrations per litre do not translate directly into intake per cup. The health side belongs to a future health entry.

## Slurry temperature

The SCA home-brewer standard specifies the temperature of the slurry in the basket, not of the water supplied: it must reach 90–96 °C by the time 33% of the water has been sprayed and stay in that range for the rest of the cycle.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In the nine-method comparison, the gravity methods were brewed with water at about 90 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the Karlsbad pot, the water was poured in small portions and reheated in between.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

No peer-reviewed measurement of slurry temperature in manual pour-over brewing was found among the sources consulted for this entry. Figures that circulate for hand brewing are therefore left out.

## Cold and hot brew: what the measurements show

Two studies brewed cold and hot coffee from the same beans at the same or a similar ratio.

- **Rao & Fuller, 2018.** Six light-roast coffees, 35 g in 350 mL. Cold brew steeped for 7 h at 21–25 °C; hot brew used boiling water in a French press for 6 min; both were filtered through paper.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> pH was comparable, 4.85–5.13. Titratable acidity was lower in every cold brew, and so was antioxidant activity; the authors suggest that hot brewing extracts more non-deprotonated acids.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Cordoba et al., 2019.** Two Colombian coffees, 60 g in 700 g water at 20 °C, medium or coarse grind, 14 or 22 h.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> All cold brews had lower titratable acidity than their hot counterparts.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Coarse grind and 22 h gave the highest TDS; the medium grind caked in the filter bags.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Panellists scored the coarse-ground 14 h brews highest.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Titratable acidity to pH 8 (mL of 0.10 N NaOH per 40 mL of brew, mean) from Rao & Fuller<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>:

| Ethiopia (Ardi) | 7.08 | 5.25 |
| Ethiopia (‘Yirgz’, vendor label) | 7.45 | 5.18 |
| Myanmar | 6.40 | 5.32 |
| Colombia | 7.85 | 5.52 |
| Mexico | 6.68 | 4.75 |

## History of the devices

The dates below come from patent documents, a patent office and UNESCO; they show when a design was registered, not who first brewed that way.

- **1908 – Paper filter.** Melitta Bentz of Dresden registered a coffee filter as a utility model with the Imperial Patent Office on 20 June 1908.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> It had a base curved and recessed on the underside, with inclined flow holes; her first prototype was a perforated brass cup lined with blotting paper.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The company was entered in the commercial register on 15 December 1908.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **1928–1931 – Plunger pot.** A US patent filed on 2 April 1929, with Italian priority of 2 April 1928, granted on 24 March 1931 to Ugo Paolini and assigned to Attilio Calimani and Giulio Moneta, describes a vessel with a tight-fitting, sliding filter that is pushed down a rod and traps the grounds at the bottom.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Earlier plunger designs may exist; none was consulted for this entry.
- **1939–1941 – Chemex.** Peter Schlumbohm filed a US patent for a “filtering device” on 13 April 1939, with German priority of 13 April 1938; it was granted on 6 May 1941.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> It describes a conical funnel and flask in one piece, a paper filter cone, and a groove that serves as both pouring spout and air passage.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **2005 – AeroPress.** Alan Adler filed a patent for a coffee or tea filtering press on 18 May 2005; it was granted on 14 December 2010.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **2013 – Turkish coffee** culture and tradition inscribed by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Pour technique.** How pour rate, pour height, pulses and stirring change extraction in a pour-over has not been tested in any study consulted for this entry. Bed-shape modelling suggests that flow uniformity matters.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Bloom.** The effect of pre-wetting fresh coffee on extraction yield and flavour lacks a peer-reviewed measurement among the sources consulted here.
- **Slurry temperature by hand.** No published measurement of slurry temperature in manual pour-over, or of the effect of dripper material and preheating, was found.
- **Cold brew at fridge temperature.** The equilibrium study did not examine cold brew, and its authors call for experiments on full immersion cold brewing.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Method versus recipe.** It is difficult to link a brew method to a flavour direction for a given coffee, because pressure, filter and ratio change together.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The nine-method comparison used one recipe per method.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
