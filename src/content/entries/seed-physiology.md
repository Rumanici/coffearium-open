brief:
  - The coffee seed is alive during processing; germination markers peaked about two days after the start of wet processing and about one week after the start of dry processing.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
  - Drying puts the living seed under drought stress; the stress metabolite GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid) was always markedly higher in natural than in washed arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## A seed, not only a bean

A coffee "bean" is the seed of the coffee fruit. When the cherry is picked, the seed inside it is alive.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It holds a tiny plant, the embryo, only 3–4 mm long, which sits near the convex surface of the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Around it lies the endosperm, which is living tissue too.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Processing changes the seed's surroundings. The pulp is removed, the seed may sit under water, and then it dries. The living seed responds with its own metabolism.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> That metabolism changes the chemistry of green coffee, and it differs between processing methods.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Germination starts at the mill

Coffee seeds start to germinate during processing. A 2007 study followed markers of germination in arabica seeds of the cultivar Acaiá from Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The markers rose, peaked and fell again while the coffee was processed and dried.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The timing depended on the method. In washed coffee (also called wet processing), germination activity peaked about two days after processing began. In natural coffee it peaked only about one week after the start.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The activity is transient: it fades again as the seed dries.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Drying is stress for the seed

As the seed dries, it loses water and its water potential falls. A seed that is still alive reacts to this with strong drought-stress responses.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> One sign is GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid), an amino acid that plants build up under stress.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

In a 2017 drying trial, natural coffee, dried as whole fruit, took longer to dry than washed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In a comparison of processed arabica coffees, GABA in natural coffee was always markedly higher than in washed coffee or in untreated seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors proposed GABA as a marker of how a coffee was processed.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Drying natural coffee also left more hydrogen peroxide, a sign of oxidative stress, than drying pulped natural coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## When the seed dies

**Heat.** Hot air can kill the embryo. In a Brazilian trial, natural coffee dried at 60 °C did not germinate at all, while sun-dried natural coffee reached 80% germination.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Washed coffee survived better: 97% germinated after sun-drying and 61% after drying at 60 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Under the microscope, cell membranes in the endosperm stayed intact at 40 °C but ruptured at 60 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Too little water.** Coffee seeds tolerate drying only down to a point. Seed scientists class their storage behaviour as "intermediate".<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Arabica seeds were damaged when dried below about 0.10 g of water per g of dry matter.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Time.** Dried seeds die slowly in storage. At ambient temperature, arabica seed viability falls rapidly after 4–6 months.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Seeds kept in their parchment stay alive longer than hulled seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The resting entry covers storage in parchment.

## Why it matters for quality

The seed's own metabolism changes the chemistry of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Washed green coffee holds less glucose and fructose than natural coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2010 drying study concluded that at least part of this gap comes from the seed using the sugars, not from sugars washing out.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Damage to the living seed can also go with a poorer cup. In the Brazilian drying trial, sun-drying gave both the best physiological performance and the best cup.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Damaged cells leak potassium and other ions when beans are soaked in water. Measuring this leakage is a check for membrane damage.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Germination metabolism

**Markers.** The 2007 study used three markers.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Isocitrate lyase (ICL) is the key enzyme of the glyoxylate cycle; coffee seeds are rich in fat, and ICL marks the transition from late embryogenesis to germination.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> ICL transcripts were measured in whole seeds by competitive RT-PCR.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> β-Tubulin, a marker of cell division or elongation, was measured in excised embryos by Western blot, and the ratio of 4C to 2C nuclei by flow cytometry as a measure of cell-cycle activity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Timing.** In wet processing, ICL expression and β-tubulin both peaked 2 days after the start.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In dry processing, ICL peaked about one week after the start and β-tubulin after 6 days.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In both methods β-tubulin declined during further drying, and the maximum frequency of 4C nuclei was markedly lower in dry-processed seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The authors attribute the chemical differences between wet- and dry-processed coffee to this differential germination activity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Under water.** A 2019 trial in Yunnan, China, saw the bean's own metabolism stay active at several stages of wet processing, through changes in carbohydrates, organic acids and free amino acids.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2017 field study in Ecuador linked the fall of endosperm sugars during fermentation to the lack of oxygen under water, which triggered germination and anaerobic sugar consumption by the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Stress metabolism during drying

Falling water potential in the still-living seed induces massive drought-stress responses.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2010 study followed GABA and the expression of dehydrin genes during drying. The first GABA peak coincided with germination activity; two later peaks matched maxima of dehydrin expression and are thought to be induced by drought stress in the embryo and in the endosperm.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The paper's abstract and main text give the order of the two tissues differently.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

GABA itself is a well-known plant stress metabolite. Its content in natural arabica was always markedly higher than in washed arabica and in untreated seeds, which the authors took as evidence of significant metabolism in the seed during postharvest treatment.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> They proposed GABA as a tool to characterise the processing method.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Drought stress also produces reactive oxygen species. In a 2013 study, natural coffee showed lower activity of the antioxidant enzymes superoxide dismutase, guaiacol peroxidase and glutathione reductase during drying, and more hydrogen peroxide, than pulped natural coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The authors read this as oxidative stress, controlled more efficiently in pulped natural coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Sugars: metabolism or leaching

Washed arabica held only low amounts of glucose and fructose, while natural arabica processed in parallel held significantly more.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In washed coffee the two sugars fall during processing; in natural coffee they stay the same or rise.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Evidence for metabolism.** In a 2010 drying study, arabica from Costa Rica was pulped, demucilaged mechanically and fermented without water, then dried in an oven at 30 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Fructose fell from nearly 110 to about 40 mg/100 g dry weight within the first day of continuous drying, and to about 30 in beans dried with pauses.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Glucose started at about 55 mg/100 g dry weight, also fell within the first day and, in the pause-dried beans, rose again from under 15 to nearly 40 before ending at about 20, while sucrose (about 6000 mg/100 g) stayed roughly constant.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> With no process water present, the authors concluded that the lower hexose content of washed coffee is, at least in part, due to metabolic processes and not to leaching.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Evidence for leaching.** In the Ecuador study, fructose and glucose in the beans fell further during a 24-hour soak, and the authors proposed that osmotic pressure helped monosaccharides leave the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In the Yunnan trial, fructose was among the most abundant compounds in the soaking water of depulped coffee at the end of soaking.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

The two mechanisms are not exclusive. The washed process entry discusses them in the context of the method; see Contested for both positions.

## Desiccation tolerance and embryo death

**Storage class.** Coffee seeds were first classed as recalcitrant but are now considered intermediate.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Cited minimum water contents without damage are about 0.09 g H₂O/g dry weight for *C. arabica* and 0.10–0.12 for *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Low temperature is harmful: the critical water content rises as temperature falls.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Drying rate.** Seeds dried rapidly in silica gel tolerated lower final moisture than seeds dried slowly over saturated salt solutions, and rapid drying damaged the endosperm more than the embryo.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> In the seed-storage literature, slow equilibration drying lowered the antioxidants glutathione and ascorbate and raised free fatty acids.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Heat.** In the 2015 Brazilian trial, natural coffee dried with heated air (60 °C throughout, or 60 °C down to about 30% moisture, wet basis, and then 40 °C) showed a small amount of root protrusion but no germination; the authors concluded that the seeds were already dead or far into deterioration.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Washed coffee from the same treatments still germinated at 85% and 61%.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A 2017 study likewise found that natural coffee is more sensitive to heated-air drying than washed coffee (pulped, fermented and washed), with poorer physiological performance.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Electron microscopy shows the damage: at 40 °C plasma membranes and oil-body vesicles stayed intact, at 60 °C they ruptured.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Storage.** Hulled washed arabica lost viability within months at 22 °C and 63% relative humidity, while seeds in parchment stayed alive for more than a year.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The authors suggest that the loss of viability is relevant to the flattening of cup quality in stored coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Measuring the living seed

**Germination test.** Seeds are germinated without parchment, because the endocarp strongly inhibits germination.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Two Brazilian studies used four replicates of 50 seeds on moist paper at 30 °C with a final count after 30 days.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Germination is slow and uneven, which makes the test long.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Tetrazolium test.** Staining with tetrazolium is a quicker alternative for assessing viability.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In a method study, seeds were soaked for 36 or 48 hours at 30 °C, embryos were excised, stained in 0.5% tetrazolium at 30 °C in the dark for 2 hours, cut lengthwise and scored at 10× magnification.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A 48-hour soak made the embryos easier to extract without changing the result. Parchment had to be removed by hand: sodium hypochlorite did not remove it fully and lowered the test results on low-moisture seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Electrical conductivity and potassium leaching.** Fifty beans soak in 75 mL of deionised water at 25 °C for 5 hours, and the conductivity of the water is read.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Potassium in the water is measured by flame photometry.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Higher potassium leaching with higher conductivity is a strong indicator of membrane and cell-wall damage.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In a 2007 study, conductivity rose from 136.03 µS/g in "mole" (soft) coffee and 137.98 in "dura" coffee to 195.23 in "rio" coffee, the lowest of the three Brazilian cup classes.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Histology matched: "mole" coffee had well-defined lipid bodies inside the cells, while in "dura" and "rio" coffee the lipids were scattered and sometimes leaked into the tissue.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Dead embryos after hot drying** – natural coffee dried with heated air at 60 °C did not germinate, and across both processes drying at 60 °C gave lower cup scores than sun-drying.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Membrane rupture** – at 60 °C, membranes break and oil leaks between cells,<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> which raises conductivity and potassium leaching.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Oxidative stress in slow-drying naturals** – more hydrogen peroxide and lower antioxidant enzyme activity than in pulped natural coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **Loss of viability in storage** – hulled coffee dies within months, and the authors suggest the loss is relevant to the flattening of the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Metabolism and flavour.** Germination and stress metabolism change sugars and amino acids,<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> but no study consulted here traces a specific flavour compound in the cup back to one metabolic pathway in the seed.
- **Honey and pulped natural coffee.** No study consulted here has timed germination markers in coffee that is pulped but dried with its mucilage.
- **Robusta.** The germination and GABA studies consulted here used arabica only.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Embryo or endosperm.** Germination tests score the embryo, while cup quality comes from the endosperm, and rapid drying damaged the endosperm more than the embryo.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> How well embryo viability predicts endosperm quality is not established.
