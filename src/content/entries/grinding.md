## Why coffee is ground

A whole roasted bean gives up little to water. Grinding breaks it into small particles with a large surface, open pores and short distances for the soluble substances to travel.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> These substances are then accessible for extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Surface area.** The smaller a particle, the larger its surface per unit of volume (its specific surface).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For an idealised sphere, surface divided by volume equals 6 divided by the diameter, so halving the diameter doubles the specific surface (geometry; our arithmetic). Small particles expose more surface to water and extract more efficiently, according to a coffee science textbook.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Opened cells.** Roasted coffee is made of cells. In a 2016 model of coffee extraction, the surfaces of ground particles consist mostly of broken cells, whose contents are released quickly.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The core of a larger particle still consists of intact cells, which release their contents much more slowly.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The finest particles, called fines, are fragments of broken cells.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Two speeds of extraction.** In the same model, extraction runs in two stages. A rapid start from the broken surfaces is followed by much slower diffusion out of the intact grains.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In one espresso study, shots of under 10 s and of 15 s already reached 17–18% extraction, more than 80% of the highest yield observed.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The authors take the maximum yield for their recipe to be reached with shots longer than 40 s.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## What grind size controls

Grind size changes how fast coffee extracts. In a percolation brew, it also sets how long the water stays in contact with it.

**Flow through the bed.** In a pour-over, adjusting the grinder changes how fast water percolates and how long it is in contact with the coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A finer grind leaves smaller spaces between the particles, which raises the resistance of the bed and slows the flow.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Too fine a grind may hinder an efficient distribution of water, possibly leading to over-extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Fines can also clog the pores between larger particles or in filters, and pass into the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Contact time.** According to the grinding chapter of a coffee science textbook, the longer the contact time, the coarser the coffee should be ground.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It states that very fine particles may be over-extracted, which gives higher bitterness, and that a short contact time, as in espresso, limits this.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Full immersion.** In immersion brews left to reach equilibrium, grind size changed the result only slightly. In one equilibrium study (brew ratio 15, 99 °C), the strength of full immersion brews was 1.36 ± 0.09% total dissolved solids (TDS) over median particle sizes of 579–1,311 µm.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Finer grinds gave slightly higher extraction yields, but the spread stayed within about 19–23%.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> See Contested.

**Not size alone.** To match the same flow time, different grinders need different settings, average sizes and size distributions, according to the grinding chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The shape and spread of the distribution are covered in the particle size and fines entry.

## Grind size by method

Each brew method needs its own grind size.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Textbooks describe sizes in words; studies report them as measured particle sizes.

- **In words.** A textbook lists boiled (Turkish) coffee as fine; pour-over, espresso and moka pot as medium; French press and cold brew as coarse.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> For Turkish coffee, freshly roasted beans are ground to a fine powder.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Espresso.** The grinding chapter names espresso as possibly the only method where a certain share of fines is deemed necessary, for pressure build-up, body and crema.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Measured.** In a nine-method comparison, the espresso grind peaked at about 400 µm. A setting close to the one used for lungo peaked at 600 µm.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The coarsest setting, used for French press, Karlsbad pot and filter machine, peaked at 1,000–1,025 µm.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> An aroma study chose 400–800 µm as suitable for paper drip brewing.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Grinder settings are not sizes. Grinders differ, so a setting number on one says little about the particle size on another.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How grinders and burrs work is covered in the grinder types and burrs entry.

## Freshness after grinding

Ground coffee loses gas and aroma far faster than whole beans.

**Gas.** In one study, up to 75% of the trapped gas escaped from freshly roasted coffee during grinding and the next 90 s.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The ground coffee then went on releasing gas, up to 3.0 mg/g for arabica and 3.9 mg/g for robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Why roasted coffee holds gas is explained in the degassing entry.

**Aroma.** Evaporation of aroma compounds, aroma reactions and oxidation all run faster once beans are ground, according to a freshness chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The chapter reports an earlier headspace study in which several key aroma compounds fell markedly shortly after grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In a 2003 study, volatiles captured during grinding itself were richer in nutty and smoky-roast aromas than the headspace of ground coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Grinding on demand.** Grinders in cafés and homes usually grind at the moment the coffee is needed, which preserves its freshness best.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At a point of sale, and even less at home, there is no protective nitrogen atmosphere during or after grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For a barista grinding small amounts, the grinding chapter sees no need to let the grounds rest.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How to store coffee is covered in the freshness and storage entry.

## Extraction kinetics: surfaces and cores

**Two domains.** An asymptotic analysis of coffee extraction attributes the fast early stage to fine particles and to broken cells, both with low mass transfer resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> After grinding, the surfaces of the grains consist mostly of broken cells.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The kernels of larger grains are intact cells with a much higher resistance, and their coffee leaves by slow diffusion through the tortuous grain.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors report a significantly higher initial outflow concentration for fine grinds than for coarser ones.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**What this means for grind size.** Because the fast stage comes from fines and broken cells, a finer grind extracts faster at the start.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The textbook view that small particles extract more efficiently<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> describes this rate effect. At equilibrium, in full immersion, the end point depended little on grind size.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Bed properties.** According to the grinding chapter, a finer grind packs more densely, lowers the porosity of the bed and raises the pressure drop during percolation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A wide or bimodal distribution lowers porosity further than uniform particles do.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Smaller particles also release gas faster and become more sensitive to oxidation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The behaviour of espresso beds under pressure is covered in the espresso machine entry.

**Fines in espresso.** In a 2024 espresso study with six grinder settings (burr spacings of 160–250 µm), most of the extraction happened early: shots under 10 s and of 15 s gave 17–18% yield, and the maximum came only above 40 s.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> For the one coffee tested, the authors judged the flavour profile best at about 30 s and 19–20% yield; this was not a systematic sensory test.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The role of fines in detail belongs to the particle size and fines entry.

## Heat and bean temperature

**Heat during grinding.** According to the grinding chapter, warmer ground coffee gives faster flow times.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It was once explained by thermal expansion of the grinding mechanism; the chapter states that more recent studies found modern grinders dimensionally stable, and that the bean itself deforms more plastically when warmer or more humid, giving a coarser grind.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2016 study proposes that burrs warming over a working day explain why baristas adjust the grind, through warming of the particles at the burr surface; it did not measure burr temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> An aroma study could not exclude a temperature rise during grinding as a cause of its results.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured the temperature rise in a coffee grinder, so no figure is given.

**Bean temperature.** The 2016 study ground the same coffee at room temperature, from a freezer, from dry ice and from liquid nitrogen, on one flat-burr grinder.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Colder beans gave a narrower particle size distribution and a smaller most common particle size (mode).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The authors suggest a shattering transition in some components of the bean between room and freezer temperature, and call this a possibility still under study.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> They expect the finer grind to extract faster and the more uniform one to waste less of the bean, but did not test extraction or flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> According to the grinding chapter, the finest industrial grinding preferably adds liquid nitrogen, which makes the bean more brittle and protects aroma.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Whether grinding frozen beans improves the cup has not been tested in any study consulted for this entry (see Open questions). Storage in the freezer is covered in the freshness and storage entry.

## Moisture and roast degree

**Moisture.** In a 2008 study, roasted coffees with a high moisture content gave coarser particles on grinding and needed an equilibration time before grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Coffees with low water content extracted more effectively, and percolation through them was slower.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Higher moisture also lowered aroma stability during closed storage.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> According to the grinding chapter, water used to quench a roast first wets the bean surface and needs a resting time to spread evenly before grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Roast degree.** The grinding chapter names roast degree as the most decisive property of the beans for grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Darker roasts, especially fast ones, are more porous and brittle and grind easily, but give a wider distribution with more fines.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Slowly roasted beans are denser and more homogeneous; they need more energy to grind but give a more even result.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Static and retention

**Static.** Grinding charges coffee particles electrically, and charged boulders and fines cling together.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In a 2024 study, the charging of whole-bean coffee depended mainly on the moisture inside the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Lighter roasts with more than 2% internal moisture (basis not stated) charged positively; drier coffees, which tended to be darker roasts, charged mainly negatively.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> A small amount of water added to the beans before grinding reduced the charge and immediately reduced clumps of boulders and fines.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In the same study, an espresso (18.0 g in, 45.0 g out) ground with added water reached 8.9% TDS against 8.2% without, and the shot ran nearly 50% longer.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Retention.** The grinding chapter lists, among the requirements for grinders, avoiding segregation and the build-up of fines, for example by reducing electrostatic charging.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured how much ground coffee stays in a grinder between doses.

**Dosing.** Point-of-sale grinders dose in three ways: pre-weighed beans, a timer, or a load cell that stops the grinder at a set mass.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The chapter describes grinder development moving towards weight dosing, to lock the ratio of coffee to beverage.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## History of grinding

Only dated facts with a source that meets our rules are given; the history of grinder designs is incomplete here.

- **Mortar and pestle.** Before the coffee mill, roasted beans were pulverised with a mortar and pestle, according to a coffee grinder museum.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The museum describes this method as still used in the Ethiopian coffee ceremony (as of 2026).<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **Fine powder and stones.** Turkish coffee is made from beans ground to a fine powder.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> According to the grinding chapter (2017), stone grinders are traditionally used for Turkish coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Mills.** Even the earliest coffee mills had a manually adjustable grinding mechanism, according to the museum.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> A cast-iron coffee mill with a steel grinder in the Smithsonian collection, made in Philadelphia between 1876 and 1898, carries patent dates of 21 October and 9 December 1873.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **From home to factory.** A history chapter describes coffee in the United States as once sold mostly green, for roasting and grinding at home, and later pre-roasted and usually ready-ground.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>
- **Industrial and on-demand grinding.** According to the grinding chapter (2017), the roller grinder can still be considered state of the art for industrial grinding, while café and home grinders grind on demand.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry dates the first electric coffee grinder.

## Open questions

- **Frozen beans in the cup.** Grinding cold narrowed the particle size distribution in one study, which did not test extraction or flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No peer-reviewed comparison of brews from frozen and room-temperature beans was found.
- **Heat in practice.** How much a grinder warms in service, and how much that changes the grind, has not been measured in any study consulted for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Retention.** No published measurement of grinder retention was found.
- **Grind and flavour.** The grinding chapter calls for more scientific studies of the effect of grinding on extraction and cup quality, which it describes as empirically perceived.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Moroney et al., 2016 – Dominant mechanisms in coffee extraction.</strong> Analyses a multiscale extraction model mathematically. Ground particles have surfaces of mostly broken cells and kernels of intact cells; extraction starts fast from the broken cells and fines and then slows to diffusion out of the intact kernels.</p>
<p class="meta">SIAM Journal on Applied Mathematics 76(6), 2196–2217 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1137/15M1036658">doi:10.1137/15M1036658</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Uman et al., 2016 – Bean origin and temperature.</strong> Grinds coffees of different origins and processes, and one coffee at four temperatures down to liquid nitrogen, and measures the particle size distributions. Origin and processing did not change the distribution, while colder beans gave a narrower, finer grind.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 6, 24483 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/srep24483">doi:10.1038/srep24483</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Smrke et al., 2018 – Gravimetric degassing.</strong> Weighs whole and ground roasted coffee continuously in containers with a capillary outlet. Grinding freshly roasted coffee to espresso fineness released up to three quarters of the trapped gas within 90 s.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 66(21), 5293–5300 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.7b03310">doi:10.1021/acs.jafc.7b03310</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Méndez Harper et al., 2024 – Static in coffee grinding.</strong> Measures the charge on ground coffee across many coffees. Charging depended mainly on the moisture inside the beans, and a little water added before grinding reduced both charge and clumping and raised espresso strength in the authors’ test.</p>
<p class="meta">Matter 7(1) · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matt.2023.11.005">doi:10.1016/j.matt.2023.11.005</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Liang et al., 2021 – Full immersion at equilibrium.</strong> Models and measures full immersion brews to equilibrium. Grind size changed strength and yield only slightly across a wide range of median particle sizes.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 11, 6904 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-85787-1">doi:10.1038/s41598-021-85787-1</a></p>
</div>
