## Two parts: futures and differential

Much green coffee is not sold at a single posted price. Its price is commonly built from two parts: a reference price set on a futures exchange, and a differential agreed between buyer and seller.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

For arabica, the reference is the Coffee “C” futures contract of ICE Futures U.S. in New York. It is quoted in US cents per pound (lb), and one contract (a lot) is 37,500 lb of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For robusta, the reference is the Robusta futures contract of ICE Futures Europe in London. It is quoted in US dollars per tonne, and one lot is 10 tonnes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How these markets work is explained in Coffee futures.

The differential is the gap between the futures price and the price of one particular coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It can be a premium (plus) or a discount (minus). Unlike the futures price, it is not published anywhere. It is negotiated for each deal and reflects quality, origin and supply and demand at origin.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Each differential belongs to one lot: a batch of coffee from one origin and process, traded as a unit. Quality is part of what the differential prices (see Green coffee grading).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## What moves the differential

The exchange itself treats origins differently. Coffee delivered against the “C” contract from Colombia, Costa Rica or Kenya earns a fixed premium, while coffee from Brazil is delivered at a discount.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The rule behind this is stated in the contract: coffees judged better are at a premium; those judged inferior are at a discount.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In physical trade the differential carries more than quality. It blends origin, quality and supply and demand in the producing country, and it is the outcome of a negotiation.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Certification can add to the price. Fairtrade sets a minimum price, and its Premium is paid on top of the minimum or the market price, whichever is higher.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Differentials usually move less than the futures price, but not always.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

When each origin harvests and ships is shown in Harvest calendars.

## Fixed now or fixed later

There are two common ways to set the price in a contract. In an outright contract, buyer and seller agree one complete price at once. In a price-to-be-fixed (PTBF) contract, they agree the quantity, quality, shipment month and differential first. The futures part stays open.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The open part is fixed later against the futures market, for example when the buyer buys the physical coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The final price is the futures price on the fixing day plus the agreed differential.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Forward contracts like these let producer organisations know what they will be paid and use the contract as collateral for loans.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Units: cents per pound, dollars per tonne, price per bag

Prices for the same coffee appear in several units. One pound is exactly 0.45359237 kg.<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup> So one US cent per lb equals USD 22.046 per tonne, or about USD 0.022 per kg (our arithmetic). Production statistics are often given in bags of 60 kg; the textbook chapter gives world arabica production this way.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

| Price | USD per kg | USD per tonne | USD per 60-kg bag |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 US cents per lb | 2.20 | 2,205 | 132 |
| 200 US cents per lb | 4.41 | 4,409 | 265 |
| 300 US cents per lb | 6.61 | 6,614 | 397 |

Conversions are our arithmetic from the definition of the pound.<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup> For a contract that names another bag weight, such as 69 or 70 kg, multiply the price per kg by that weight instead.

## From the port of origin to the cup

Most export contracts state a price “free on board” (FOB): coffee loaded on the ship at the port of export.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> Buyers who buy FOB arrange and pay for sea freight and insurance themselves.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Then come import duty, port and clearance costs, inland transport and the interest on money tied up in stock. The sum is the landed cost.

Import duty on green coffee is low in the main European markets. As of October 2026, green coffee that is not decaffeinated enters the European Union (EU) free of customs duty.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Roasted coffee pays 7.5%.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> As of October 2026, green coffee imported into Switzerland carries a guarantee fund contribution of CHF 0.12 per kg, which finances the country’s compulsory coffee stocks.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

Roasting removes weight. According to a 2020 multi-study paper, beans lose 15–22% of their weight in the roaster, mostly water.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> A kilo of roasted coffee therefore needs more than a kilo of green coffee, and its green-coffee cost is higher per kilo (see Roast loss).

## Where prices stood in October 2026

The International Coffee Organization (ICO) publishes daily indicator prices for four groups of coffee and a composite of them, the ICO composite indicator price (I-CIP).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> As of October 2026, the latest complete month is September 2026. That month the I-CIP averaged 262.18 US cents per lb, down from 287.34 in July 2026.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The groups differ widely, as the chart shows.

## Who earns what

Green coffee is a small part of what consumers pay for coffee. In 2022 an ICO overview put green coffee exports at about USD 20 billion a year. That is at most 10% of the over USD 200 billion generated at retail level.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> A 2025 review reports that producing countries once kept about 30% of the total value and now keep less than 10%.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Studies differ on the exact share, depending on the chain they measure (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a>,<a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>

Producers usually have to accept the market price, even when it does not cover their costs.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Their income in local money also depends on the exchange rate, because coffee is priced in US dollars.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Exchange differentials and physical differentials

Two kinds of differential should be kept apart. The first is fixed by the exchange: it sets what a delivered coffee is worth against the contract. In the “C” contract, Mexico, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Papua New Guinea, Panama, Tanzania, Uganda, Honduras and Peru are deliverable at par; Colombia, Costa Rica and Kenya at a 1,000-point premium; Guatemala at a 500-point premium; Burundi, Rwanda, Venezuela and India at a 100-point discount; the Dominican Republic and Ecuador at a 400-point discount; and Brazil at a 600-point discount.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Prices are quoted in cents and hundredths of a cent per lb, and a point is one hundredth, so 1,000 points equal 10 cents.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> From the May 2027 expiry, Vietnam becomes deliverable at a 600-point discount.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Delivery ports also carry differentials: New Orleans, Miami and Houston 0.50 cents per lb under New York, and Bremen/Hamburg, Antwerp and Barcelona 1.25 cents under.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The second kind is the physical differential that buyers and sellers negotiate for a real lot. It is the relationship between the cash price and the futures price, also called the basis.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It is not published as a price reference and contains a blend of quality, origin and supply and demand in the domestic market.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to the same textbook, most of the time it is a buyer’s market, with a few large buyers dominating the trade.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Fixing a PTBF contract

In the textbook’s worked example, a roaster agrees with a Brazilian cooperative on quantity, quality and shipment month, while the contract price stays open except for the differential.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Both sides hedge on the “C” market. When the coffee ships, the roaster’s long futures position is passed to the cooperative as an exchange of futures for physicals (EFP). This closes both hedges and fixes the physical price at the futures level of that day plus the differential.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Hedgers in coffee include growers, exporters, importers and roasters; according to the textbook, growers and importers tend to hedge by selling futures, roasters by buying them.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The textbook warns of the risk for cooperatives: if a fixed price is later overtaken by a rising market, members may sell to other buyers and the cooperative can default.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Mechanics of margins, spreads and options are in Coffee futures.

## Currency

Coffee futures are priced in US dollars<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>, so a producer’s price in local currency is the dollar price times the exchange rate. Coffee prices are volatile partly because exchange rates and futures prices fluctuate.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2009 study of the Brazilian domestic coffee price during the 2002–2003 crisis found that the international price had more influence than the exchange rate: the international price dominated in 24 months, the exchange rate in 8 and domestic factors in 3.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> According to a 2021 panel analysis by the Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC), the Brazilian real–US dollar real exchange rate is among the drivers of arabica prices in Latin America, with green coffee stocks, the oil price and the Colombian peso exchange rate also significant.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Neither source gives a size of effect that can be applied to a single contract; we give none.

## Specialty prices outside the futures logic

Not every lot is priced against the exchange. Second- and third-wave roasters often buy FOB from exporters or cooperatives, and some buy directly from producers, organising freight and insurance themselves; the third wave often calls this direct trade.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The Specialty Coffee Transaction Guide collects such prices. Support organisations, exporters, importers and roasters give it detailed contract data on a confidential basis, and it summarises FOB prices, which most export contracts report consistently.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> Following the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA), it counts a coffee as specialty from a score of 80 points.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> In its first data brief, on harvest years from October 2016 to September 2019, combined Fairtrade and organic (FTO) certification kept the lowest prices from falling, while the highest prices went to non-certified coffees.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

Fairtrade sets a price floor. From 1 December 2026 its minimum price for washed arabica is USD 2.00 per lb FOB, with a Premium of USD 0.20 per lb paid on top of the minimum or the market price, whichever is higher.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Details are in Certifications and labels.

## Open questions

- **Differential risk.** Futures hedges cover the reference price; no exchange instrument covers the physical differential, and no source consulted for this entry measured how much of producers’ price risk sits in it.
- **Size of currency effects.** The direction is established for Brazil, but the studies consulted here do not give an effect size usable for a single contract.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **Value shares.** Recent, origin-specific measurements of the farmgate share of retail prices are scarce; the commonly quoted shares rest on studies from 2017–2018.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a>,<a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>
