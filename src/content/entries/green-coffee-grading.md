## What grading is for

Green coffee is bought and sold long before anyone roasts it. A grade tells the buyer what the coffee is like without seeing the whole lot. A lot is a batch of coffee from one origin and process, traded as a unit. The grade summarises a sample: how many beans are defective, how large the beans are, and how moist they are.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Several bodies set grading rules. Producing countries set export grades; Brazil’s agriculture ministry and Colombia’s national coffee committee are examples.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The International Coffee Organization (ICO) sets a voluntary minimum for exports.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Futures exchanges grade the coffee that may be delivered against their contracts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) describes a physical assessment of green coffee for specialty buyers.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Grading here means a judgement on a sample. The machines that sort beans by size, weight and colour belong to the dry mill, and are described under grading and sorting.

## How a sample is graded

**The sample.** A fixed weight of green coffee is taken from the lot. Brazil uses 300 g, the SCA 350 g, and Colombian export rules 500 g.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Defects.** A grader spreads the sample on a black mat and looks at every bean.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Defective beans are picked out and grouped by type. Some defects matter more than others. One full black bean counts as one full defect, but five broken beans are needed for one.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The total of these “full defects” is the defect count of the lot.

**Size.** The sample is shaken through a nest of screens with round holes. Each screen number names its hole diameter in 64ths of an inch.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Flat beans are sized on round holes, peaberries on oblong slots.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A peaberry forms when one ovule develops instead of the usual two.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The result is the share of the sample, by mass, left on each screen.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Moisture, colour and odour.** Moisture is read with a meter calibrated against ISO 6673, a method that measures loss in mass at 105 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Colour is matched to named categories, from blue-green to brownish.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A foreign or mouldy smell can rule a lot out entirely under the Brazilian regulation.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**The cup.** Many systems also taste the coffee. Colombian export coffee must have its characteristic flavour and aroma, free of off-flavours.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Exchange graders cup at least six cups of each sample.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How the SCA scores the cup is explained under cupping.

## Grades at the exchange

A futures contract specifies the grade of coffee that may be delivered against it, and where.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> When New York’s first coffee exchange set up its contract, it defined grades of deliverable coffee and priced them all against one basis grade.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The ICE arabica contract (Coffee “C”) works the same way today. It accepts washed arabica of listed growths.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Three graders, drawn at random from a list licensed by the exchange, grade each sample.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The coffee must be sound, of good roasting quality, greenish and free of foreign odours.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It must also cup free of unwashed and aged flavours.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Each growth has a price differential to the contract, and so does each full imperfection below the basis.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How these differences enter a price is explained in coffee futures and how a coffee price is made.

The ICE robusta contract in London grades by weight instead of by bean count. It sorts deliveries into five classes by their share of defects and foreign matter and by bean size.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Reference values: defect equivalents (SCA)

The SCA’s physical assessment counts defects in 350 g of green coffee on a black mat, groups them by type and converts each type into full defect equivalents, rounded down to whole numbers.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Category 1 and category 2 totals are reported separately.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The same weights appear in a 2017 textbook chapter on cupping and grading.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The table below gives the number of beans of each type that make one full defect.

| Category 1 defect | Beans per full defect | Category 2 defect | Beans per full defect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full black | 1<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> | Partial black | 3<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> |
| Full sour | 1<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> | Partial sour | 3<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> |
| Dried cherry / pod | 1<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> | Parchment | 5<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> |
| Fungus damaged | 1<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> | Floater | 5<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> |
| Foreign matter | 1<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> | Immature / unripe | 5<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> |
| Severe insect damage | 5<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> | Withered | 5<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> |
| | | Shell | 5<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> |
| | | Broken / chipped / cut | 5<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> |
| | | Hull / husk | 5<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> |
| | | Slight insect damage | 10<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> |

The textbook describes category 1 (primary) defects as those that harm cup quality, and category 2 (secondary) defects as those that harm appearance, cup quality or both.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The defects themselves are identified against the criteria of the SCA’s Washed Arabica Green Coffee Defect Guide, which the CVA document cites.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> That guide and the older SCAA Arabica Green Coffee Defect Handbook were not openly available for this entry; we describe them and do not reproduce them. Older SCAA weights reproduced by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), and the Brazilian weights, are compared in grading and sorting.

**What the CVA changed.** The CVA physical assessment largely mirrors the 2004-based guide, but water activity and the quaker count are no longer required tests, and moisture must be read with meters calibrated against ISO 6673.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> It applies only to washed arabicas.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Physical results are kept from tasters until the sensory work is finished.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Only the affective assessment produces the coffee score; physical results feed value discovery case by case.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## National systems in detail

**Brazil.** The ministry’s regulation sorts green coffee into two groups, arabica and robusta (*C. canephora*).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Flat beans (*chato*) are sized on round holes: large on screens 19/18 and 17, medium on 16 and 15, small on 14 and below.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Peaberries (*moca*) are sized on oblong holes: large on 13/12 and 11, medium on 10, small on 9 and below.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Coffee that has not been sieved, or that spreads over four or more screens, is “bica corrida”.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Defects are converted by equivalence tables and summed to give the type.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Coffee with more than 300 defects, not counting broken, poorly filled, shell and cleanly bored beans, is “fora de tipo” (out of type).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The cup class for arabica runs from strictly soft, soft and only soft to hard, and then to the phenolic “riado”, “rio” and “rio zona”; robusta has its own four classes.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The ICO export rule names the “New York green coffee classification/Brazilian method” as the counting method for arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Colombia.** Excelso for export is mainly flat, even beans above screen 14, with a tolerance of 1.5% below it, all retained by screen 12 and at least 50% retained on screen 15.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Supremo is above screen 17 and Extra above screen 16, each with a 5% tolerance and retained by screen 14.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 500 g sample may hold up to 72 defective beans, of which no more than 12 from the first (more serious) group.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Moisture is measured with dielectric meters calibrated against ISO 6673, on at least 400 g.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**East Africa.** EAS 130 defines grades by the screens a bean passes and stays on, citing ISO 4150.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> AA beans pass screen 21 and stay on screen 18; AB pass 18 and stay on 16; PB (peaberries) pass 17 and stay on 12; C pass 16 and stay on 10.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> E (two beans joined together) stay on screen 21, and T, the smallest and broken pieces, pass screen 7.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> TT is a density grade: light beans blown out of AA, AB and E by air.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A further ten classes limit insect, fungal and rodent damage.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The 1999 edition was the one openly available.

**Ethiopia.** Coffee arriving at the central market is graded by the Ethiopian Commodity Exchange (ECX), and prices are often set on the results of this assessment.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> For export, the grade combines a raw (green bean) score with a cup score; for the domestic market only the green beans are scored.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The top grades go on to a specialty assessment.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> No openly published ECX or Ethiopian Coffee and Tea Authority rule was found that gives the point bands of each grade, so they are not given here.

**Vietnam.** TCVN 4193:2014 covers both arabica and robusta green coffee and replaces the 2005 edition.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It sets grades 1 to 3, limits defective beans and foreign matter as a share of mass, and names round-hole screens including 18, 16, 13 and 12.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Its test methods are the national adoptions of ISO 4149, ISO 10470, ISO 4150 and ISO 6673.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Only a preview was open, so the limits per grade are left out.

## Exchange grading in detail

**Arabica (ICE “C”).** Samples of 5 lb are drawn from at least 10% of the bags.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At least 50% of the sample must stay on screen 15 or above, and no more than 5% may pass screen 14.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Graders cup at least six cups per sample for soundness and unwashed or aged flavours.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The count is the average of the three graders, and the owner may appeal to a new panel within two business days.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Imperfections are weighted much as at origin. One full black, sour or pod counts as one imperfection, as do five shells or five broken beans; a stone above screen 20 counts as three.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Most growths have a basis of 8 full imperfections and Colombia a basis of 13.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Coffee may hold no more than 15 imperfections below its basis (10 for Colombia), and each imperfection below the basis carries a differential of 10 points.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The rulebook states growth differentials in points too; a point is one hundredth of a US cent per lb. As of October 2026, from the March 2026 contract onwards, Colombia, Costa Rica and Kenya carry plus 1,000 points, Guatemala plus 500 and Brazil minus 600.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> From the May 2027 contract, Vietnam becomes deliverable at minus 600, the same as Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

A certificate of grade stays valid for later deliveries, unless the coffee is moved to an unlicensed warehouse or another port.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Coffee already graded generally cannot be resubmitted.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Robusta (ICE Europe).** As of October 2026, the contract grades five classes by defects and foreign matter by weight and by screen retention per 300 g.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Class 1 is delivered at the contract price; it allows up to 3.0% defects and 0.5% foreign matter, with at least 90% over screen 14 round.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Class P earns a premium of USD 30 per tonne; classes 2, 3 and 4 carry discounts of USD 30, 60 and 90 per tonne.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Coffee also carries an age allowance per calendar month after grading: USD 5 per tonne in months 13–48, USD 10 from month 49.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2005 FAO manual shows that the London contract then counted defects per 500 g; one tender type, for example, allowed 151–250 defects at a discount of USD 15 per tonne.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Robusta grading

According to a 2017 textbook chapter, physical grading of robusta is very similar to that of arabica, because the defects are very similar.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The chapter describes a robusta defect table developed by the Coffee Quality Institute (CQI) and the Uganda Coffee Development Authority (UCDA).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Their “fine robusta” classification separates high-quality robustas from commercial grades by cup score.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The ICO export rule allows robusta at most 150 defects per 300 g, counted by the Vietnam or Indonesia method or an equivalent.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Brazil grades robusta in its own group with four cup classes, and Vietnam’s standard covers both species.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## International standards

ISO standards supply the methods that national rules refer to. The titles below are from the ISO catalogue; their full texts were not openly available for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

- **ISO 10470** – green coffee defect reference chart.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Vietnam’s standard uses it as its defect reference.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **ISO 4149** – olfactory and visual examination and determination of foreign matter and defects.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **ISO 4150** – size analysis by manual and machine sieving.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **ISO 6673** – loss in mass at 105 °C, the reference for moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **ISO 9116** – guidance on how to specify green coffee for sale and purchase, including sampling, packing, marking, storage and shipping. The third edition was published in September 2025 and replaces the 2004 edition.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

The ICO’s quality programme is voluntary: members are asked not to export coffee below its minimum, and to mark conforming lots on the certificate of origin.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Open questions

- The sensory weight of each physical defect: a research programme announced in 2025 aims to set detection thresholds for defects such as partial blacks, chipped or broken beans and minor insect damage.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>
- Grading for naturals, honeys and other non-washed coffees: the SCA physical assessment applies only to washed arabicas, and the SCA calls for new research covering other processing methods.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- Limits for density and water activity: none of the grading standards consulted for this entry sets a numeric limit for density, and the CVA no longer requires water activity.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- Current consolidated texts of several national systems (Kenya, Ethiopia, Central America) were not openly available.
