## What germination is

Germination is the step from a resting seed to a growing plant. It ends when the radicle, the embryonic root, breaks out of the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Everything after that is seedling growth.

A coffee seed is mostly endosperm, a hard storage tissue. Its walls are built of cellulose and hemicelluloses, mainly insoluble mannans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The embryo is small: 3–4 mm long, an axis with two heart-shaped cotyledons, or seed leaves.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The part of the endosperm in front of the root tip is called the endosperm cap.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> See [Seed anatomy and storage](/entries/seed-anatomy-and-storage/) for the seed itself.

Coffee germinates slowly and unevenly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Seed for planting is sown in its parchment, the shell around each seed.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The parchment holds germination back strongly, so laboratories remove it before germination tests.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Water comes first. The seed absorbs water, swells and turns whitish.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Then the embryo grows inside the seed, and the endosperm cap in front of the root tip softens.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> When the cap gives way, the radicle breaks through and turns downwards.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Coffee germination is epigeal: the seed leaves rise above the soil.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The hypocotyl grows upwards and lifts the seed, still in its parchment, out of the ground.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Nursery manuals call this the matchstick stage.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Shortly afterwards the parchment drops and the two cotyledons open: the butterfly stage.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

The cotyledons look quite unlike adult leaves. They are oval, with wavy edges, and 20–50 mm across.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> After germination they grow by absorbing the endosperm, which takes them 3–4 weeks.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Then the terminal bud opens the first pair of true leaves, opposite each other.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Their nutritional role done, the cotyledons later die.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## How long it takes

Laboratory tests remove the parchment and keep the seeds at 30 °C in the dark. Under these conditions the first radicles appear around day 5–6, half the seeds have germinated by day 10, and most by day 15.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

In soil, with the parchment on, it takes weeks. A Colombian growth-stage bulletin describes the radicle after three weeks, about 90% of seedlings above ground at seven weeks and fully open cotyledons at nine.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2006 review, citing a 1961 study, gives the start of arabica emergence as 50–60 days after sowing in the warmer periods of the year. In cooler periods this can stretch to 90 days.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Temperature sets the pace. A 1991 Thai paper on temperature and arabica gives about three weeks at a constant 30 °C and about three months at 17 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A coffee manual for Laos gives 30–50 days before shoots appear, depending on soil temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Colombian guidance gives 50–70 days for seed in parchment and 20 days less without it.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Seed age matters too. Coffee seed does not keep for long, and older seed germinates less well (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Raising seedlings for planting is covered on the [Farm stage](/explore/farm/), under nurseries.

## Inside the seed: embryo against endosperm

Germination in coffee is a contest between the embryo's push and the endosperm cap's resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The lateral endosperm is extremely hard, because mannan is laid down there as thick cell walls.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The cap in front of the radicle is the only route out.

**The embryo grows before the radicle emerges.** In a 2004 study on cv. Rubi (parchment removed, 30 °C, dark), the embryo grew inside the endosperm before radicle protrusion. Its length increased by 1.06 mm, 35% of its initial length, up to day 10 of imbibition.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The axis grew by about 0.73 mm (36%) and the cotyledons by 0.33 mm (34%), but the cotyledons grew only during the first 5 days.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The embryo's pressure potential rose from 0.11 MPa at day 2 to 1.62 MPa at day 5 and fell to around 0 MPa at day 6; the authors read this as cell-wall relaxation that allows further growth.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to a doctoral thesis from the same group, the embryo first grows in width, then in length, and cell division starts before radicle protrusion.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2015 study confirmed that the embryo grows inside the seed before the radicle protrudes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**The cap weakens in two steps.** Measurements of the force needed to puncture the cap show a first decrease, then a second.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the 2004 study the force fell from 1.36 N at day 2 to 0.57 N at day 9, with the first phase between days 0 and 4 and the second between days 6 and 9.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A rise in cellulase activity coincided with the first step and a rise in endo-β-mannanase activity with the second.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Endo-β-mannanase activity was detectable in the cap from day 2 and appeared there before it appeared in the rest of the endosperm.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Earlier studies disagreed on whether this enzyme acts before radicle protrusion at all; a 2017 review notes that the method of one early study may not have been sensitive enough to detect low activity.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Gene expression of β-mannosidase and endo-β-mannanase increases during imbibition.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Hormones: ABA and gibberellins

Abscisic acid (ABA) slows coffee germination at two points. It reduces the embryo's growth potential and blocks the second step of cap weakening, together with endo-β-mannanase activity in the cap.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In that study, 1,000 µM ABA prevented germination completely; the puncture force stopped falling after day 7 and endo-β-mannanase activity in the cap stayed below 10% of the water control.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The embryo's own ABA content rose at day 2 of imbibition and peaked again around day 5, then fell to near zero at day 8, when radicles protruded.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Fluridone, which blocks ABA synthesis, shortened the time to 50% germination from 9.9 to 8.9 days. The authors conclude that ABA is made anew in the embryo during imbibition and that this may contribute to the slow rate of radicle protrusion.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> ABA inhibits both embryo growth and radicle protrusion; α-galactosidase expression is less affected by it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Gibberellins (GAs) are needed: endogenous GAs are required for embryo cell elongation and for cap weakening.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Applied GA<sub>3</sub>, by contrast, inhibits radicle protrusion in coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Growth stages on the BBCH scale

The extended BBCH scale gives every stage a two-digit code. Cenicafé, the Colombian coffee research centre, published the coffee version in 2001.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Its timings are written for average ambient conditions of 23 °C by day and 17 °C by night.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

| Code | Stage | Timing (bulletin, 23/17 °C) |
|---|---|---|
| 00 | Dry seed, 11–12% moisture (basis not stated); beige in parchment | At sowing |
| 01–03 | Imbibition: seed swollen, whitish, no radicle | Complete after about 1 week |
| 05 | Radicle protrusion and hooking | About 3 weeks |
| 06 | Radicle lengthens; root hairs and lateral roots | – |
| 07–09 | Hypocotyl breaks through the seed coat; seedling emerges with cotyledons still enclosed in the parchment | Nearly 90% emerged at about 7 weeks |
| 10 | Cotyledons completely unfolded; first pair of true leaves separating | About 9 weeks |
| 11–19 | One to nine or more leaf pairs unfolded | – |

Source for the table: Arcila-Pulgarín et al. 2001.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> We group neighbouring codes where the bulletin's descriptions overlap.

## Light, parchment and seed age

**Parchment.** The endocarp drastically inhibits coffee germination.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The Colombian figure of 20 days saved without parchment is a practical guide value, not a controlled comparison.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Light.** Colombian guidance states that light inhibits root formation and delays germination.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> No controlled study of light and coffee germination was consulted for this entry.

**Seed age and storage.** Coffee seed has intermediate storage behaviour: it does not survive complete drying, or drying combined with cold.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a 2021 Colombian trial with var. Castillo, seed stored at 7.5 °C or 10 °C kept an emergence probability above 75% for six months; at 25 °C, for four months.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The sources give different keeping times (see Contested). Storage itself is covered in [Seed anatomy and storage](/entries/seed-anatomy-and-storage/).

## Germination during processing

The seed is alive in the cherry and in processing. Studies in 2006 and 2007 showed that germination processes start in coffee seeds during standard post-harvest treatment.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In the 2006 study, fresh ripe cherries from Brazil or Tanzania were processed both ways in the laboratory. It used two markers: expression of isocitrate lyase, a germination-specific enzyme of the glyoxylate cycle, and β-tubulin in the embryo, a marker of resumed cell division.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Fresh seeds showed no or only minor signals of either.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

In washed processing, isocitrate lyase expression was clear on the first day and highest on the second, when fermentation ended; it fell rapidly during drying.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In natural processing, clear signals appeared after 2–3 days and peaked at days 4–5; β-tubulin followed the same pattern.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The 2007 study put the natural-process maximum at about one week (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The authors propose that ABA and the osmotic potential of the fruit flesh hold germination back inside the fruit, and that removing the pulp lifts this brake.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A 2017 review reads the earlier start in washed processing the same way.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

The authors link these differences to differences in green-coffee chemistry and quality.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> See [Drying](/entries/drying/) and [Coffee cherry anatomy](/entries/coffee-cherry-anatomy/).

## Open questions

- **Canephora seedlings.** Almost all the mechanistic work cited here used *C. arabica*, mostly cv. Rubi. No study consulted for this entry measured germination timing for *C. canephora*.
- **Temperature response.** No study consulted for this entry gives a full germination curve across temperatures with stated minimum, optimum and maximum. The 30 °C and 17 °C values come from a paper abstract that does not name the underlying trials.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Why ABA rises during imbibition.** The 2004 study showed that the embryo makes ABA during imbibition and suggests that this slows germination; what triggers the synthesis is not established.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Fresh versus re-imbibed seed.** The 2006 study and a 2017 review note that most coffee germination studies used dried seed soaked again, not fresh seed from the fruit, so they may mix arrested and new germination.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The 2004 laboratory study also used seed dried to 12% moisture (fresh-weight basis).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **From germination to the cup.** The 2006 study states that a clear chain of cause and effect from processing biochemistry to coffee quality is still lacking; a 2017 review finds only a limited number of studies linking specific biochemical changes to specific sensory attributes.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>da Silva et al., 2004 – ABA, embryo growth and the endosperm cap.</strong> Germinated cv. Rubi seeds with and without ABA and followed embryo growth potential, endosperm cap weakening and enzyme activity. The embryo grew by 35% in length before the radicle emerged, and the cap weakened in two steps; ABA blocked the second step and the endo-β-mannanase activity in the cap, but not cellulase.</p>

<p class="meta">Plant Growth Regulation 75(2), 575–581 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s10725-014-9960-6">doi:10.1007/s10725-014-9960-6</a></p>
