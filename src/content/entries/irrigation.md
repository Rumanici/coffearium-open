## Why coffee is irrigated

Most coffee grows on rain alone. Irrigation fills the gap where the dry season is long or the rain is unreliable. Without it, yields in some marginal regions may fall by as much as 80% in very dry years, according to a 2006 review.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

A coffee science textbook chapter lists irrigation among the main answers to drought, hot dry winds and water shortage while the fruit swells.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Drought reduces the number of fruits and the size of the beans. Light, scattered rain in the dry season causes several flowerings and uneven ripening.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The same chapter warns that practices such as irrigation never fully make up for an unsuitable site.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Which climates suit coffee is covered in Site selection: climate, altitude and terrain.

Irrigation has a second purpose: timing. Coffee flower buds rest until water returns after a dry spell (see Flowering and pollination). A producer who controls the water can choose when that happens. Brazilian trials use this to bring the whole field into flower at once (see below).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Where coffee is irrigated

**Brazil.** Two sources put the irrigated coffee area at about 200,000–240,000 ha.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Most of it lies in the Cerrado, the savanna of central Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The same source names western Bahia and northern Minas Gerais as new coffee frontiers.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In Espírito Santo state, more than half of the irrigated area is conilon, the Brazilian robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Vietnam.** Robusta in the Central Highlands depends on irrigation in the dry months. From January to April, rain supplies only 25% of the crop’s water demand.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Groundwater is the main source. The water comes mostly from open wells dug on farms or from nearby lakes.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**India.** In Chikmagalur district, Karnataka, most growers surveyed in 2008–09 had sprinklers: about 80% of small and all large farms.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> They used them to give “blossom” showers in February and March when the rain failed.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

No source consulted for this entry gives irrigated areas for Kenya, Colombia or Central America, or a reliable world total.

## How much water coffee uses

Water use is calculated in two steps. First, weather data give the reference evapotranspiration (ET₀): the water a standard grass surface would lose. Then a crop coefficient (Kc) scales it to the crop: crop evapotranspiration ETc = Kc × ET₀.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) guideline lists coffee at Kc 0.90–0.95 on bare ground and 1.05–1.10 with weeds between the rows.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Measured values vary with the canopy. A 2011 study found Kc rising in step with leaf area up to a leaf area index of 3.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Methods

**Drip.** Drip lines run along the rows and wet the soil only near the plants. This is called localised irrigation. Water needs filtering so the drippers do not clog, and fertiliser can be added to the water (fertigation).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Under each dripper the water spreads into a wet bulb.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Compared with sprinklers, an Incaper manual gives water savings of up to 20–30%.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Centre pivot.** Early centre pivots applied large volumes of water.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Low-pressure versions (LEPA, low-energy precision application) place water more precisely and use less energy.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Pivots are widely used in northern Espírito Santo.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Sprinklers.** Unlike localised systems, sprinklers wet the whole surface.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In conventional systems that are moved periodically, efficiency is 70–85%, according to the Incaper manual.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Basin.** In Vietnam, basin and sprinkler irrigation are the two traditional methods.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In a 2021 study in Lam Dong province, 95% of growers in group discussions used overflow (flood) irrigation.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Irrigation to line up flowering

In the Cerrado, the Brazilian Agricultural Research Corporation (Embrapa) tested a planned dry spell. Irrigation stopped on 15 June and resumed when the plants reached a set water stress.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The flowers opened fully about 12 days after watering resumed.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The stress level of −2.0 MPa gave a single, uniform flowering.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> More than 80% of the fruit was ripe cherry at harvest.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Harvest methods are covered in Harvesting and cherry sorting. How water stress prepares the buds is explained in Flowering and pollination.

## Crop coefficients and water demand

FAO-56 tabulates Kc for non-stressed, well-managed crops. For coffee it gives 0.90 (initial) and 0.95 (mid and late season) on bare ground, and 1.05 and 1.10 with weeds, for plants 2–3 m tall.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Field values differ. Under a centre pivot at Planaltina, Federal District, Brazil, the measured Kc of 3- to 5-year-old Catuaí was 1.0 in the cool months (June–August) and 1.25 from September to May.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2006 review reports a maximum Kc of 0.59 for an irrigated hedgerow in Hawaii at a leaf area index of 1.4, and about 0.80 at a much higher leaf area index.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> When water was withheld there, Kc began to fall within about 5 days and halved from 0.8 to 0.4 after 18 days.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

A 2011 Brazilian model ties Kc to leaf area index (L). Kc rises in proportion to L up to L = 3.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Above that, it falls from a peak of about 1.05 towards about 0.7 for L above 6.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The authors offer the curve between those points only as an initial guess, because no measurements covered L of 3.5–5.5.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> These differences are listed under Contested.

**When irrigation is needed.** The Incaper manual cites a 1996 Brazilian classification by annual water deficit.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> With a deficit of 150–200 mm, a region suits coffee but may need occasional irrigation. With 200–400 mm, supplementary irrigation is needed. At 400 mm, robusta can be planted only with irrigation.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Water use and groundwater

**What farmers apply in Vietnam.** A 2020 study followed irrigation on 558 robusta farms in four Central Highland provinces from 2008 to 2017.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Farms irrigated one to four times a year.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In years of average rain, most farms in Dak Nong and Lam Dong applied the equivalent of 455–909 L per tree. In Dak Lak and Gia Lai the usual range was 1,364–1,818 L per tree.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The provincial means were 918 L (Lam Dong), 973 L (Dak Nong), 1,345 L (Dak Lak) and 1,445 L (Gia Lai) per tree a year.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The study assumed 1,100 trees per ha.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**What is advised.** The Vietnamese Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development (MARD) advises 400 L per tree per round in three rounds, 1,200 L a year.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Its earlier advice was 650 L per round.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2015 study proposed 120 mm of irrigation from January to April after a wet November and December, and 150 mm after an average one.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> That is 364 or 456 L per plant per round in three rounds, about 70% of the locally recommended level.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The 70% matches the earlier advice of 650 L per round (456 ÷ 650; our calculation), so the proposal lies close to the current advice of 1,200 L a year.

**Over-irrigation.** The 2020 study estimated that in normal years irrigation could be cut by 273–536 L per tree (300–590 m³/ha) a year while average yields stayed above 3,000 kg/ha. In dry years the possible cut was 27–218 L per tree (30–240 m³/ha).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Separately, it estimated that extra irrigation could raise yields by up to 180 kg/ha in normal years and 410 kg/ha in dry years.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In the 2021 Lam Dong study, about 72% of coffee households irrigated twice the standard requirement.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Groundwater.** Groundwater is the main source of irrigation water in the coffee provinces of the Central Highlands.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The 2021 study notes that the groundwater level drops significantly in the dry season.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured the long-term decline of the water table under coffee.

**Water per kilogram.** No study consulted here reports irrigation water per kilogram of coffee directly. The 2020 survey allows a rough bound. At 1,100 trees per ha, 455–909 L per tree is about 500–1,000 m³/ha, or 50–100 mm; 1,364–1,818 L is about 1,500–2,000 m³/ha (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Divided by the average yields the study reports for these two groups, 2,149–3,177 and 2,190–3,203 kg/ha (basis not stated; the ranges span all irrigation levels), that is about 160–470 L/kg in Dak Nong and Lam Dong and 470–910 L/kg in Dak Lak and Gia Lai (our calculation, pairing the extremes).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> These are bounds from regional ranges, not farm measurements.

**Planaltina, Brazil (Embrapa, 2003–2005).** Arabica cv. Catuaí Rubi MG 1192 grew under a centre pivot, with 8 ha irrigated and 2 ha rainfed.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Yields ranged from 14 bags/ha of green coffee without irrigation to 82 bags/ha with moderate stress before rewatering.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> At 60 kg per bag that is 840 and 4,920 kg/ha (our calculation). The −2.0 MPa treatment gave 13 bags/ha more than irrigation all year.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Drip, five-year trial.** In a 2015 trial with six planting arrangements, drip-irrigated coffee averaged 2,623 kg/ha and rainfed coffee 1,026 kg/ha.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The irrigated plots had greater root concentrations.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Roots were more concentrated in the top 0.5 m where rows were closer together.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Water levels, Uberlândia, Brazil.** A 2008 drip trial replaced 0–210% of Class A pan evaporation over the first four crops.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Productivity peaked at 115 bags/ha at 164.1% of pan evaporation.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The effect depended on the year, and irrigation did not remove biennial bearing.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Timing.** The Incaper manual reports a trial published in 1998 (five-year average) in which irrigation from bud formation to flowering gave about 37% more than no irrigation.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The authors conclude that this period matters most.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Kodagu, India.** Across 113 coffee agroforests, irrigation increased berry production by 16% and liming by 20%.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Liming is covered in a planned entry, Soil acidity and liming.

## Deficit irrigation

Deficit irrigation supplies less water than the full crop demand, on purpose.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The Incaper manual suggests it may suit conilon, which tolerates water deficit better than arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2006 review suggests robusta responds more to irrigation than arabica, linked to weaker stomatal control of transpiration.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The trials consulted here give mixed results. In a 2020 Brazilian trial with 13 conilon clones, yield (“rendimento”) under irrigation at 50% of ETc did not differ statistically from rainfed plots, and irrigation at 100% of ETc gave the best value. The abstract does not say whether yield means output per hectare or the processing ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The Cerrado flowering strategy is a form of timed deficit: water is withheld for about 70 days in the cool season, then restored.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The 2015 Vietnam study argues similarly that the January–April season also calls for deliberately induced water stress, linked to bud dormancy.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Risks and side effects

- **Over-irrigation.** Many Vietnamese farms applied more water than needed.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The 2020 study adds that smarter watering technology could also save labour and energy.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Scattered flowering.** Light, scattered rain in the dry season causes several flowerings and uneven ripening, and a textbook chapter lists irrigation among the answers.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In the Cerrado trial, a planned dry spell followed by full rewatering gave a single flowering.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Clogging.** Drip systems fail without filtered water.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Biennial bearing.** In the Uberlândia trial, irrigation did not even out the swing between heavy and light crops.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **A world figure.** No source consulted here gives a reliable share of the world’s coffee that is irrigated.
- **Water per kilogram.** No study consulted here measured irrigation water per kilogram of green coffee on the same farms; the bounds above are calculated from regional ranges.
- **Deficit irrigation for arabica.** The trials consulted here test deficits in conilon or as a short pre-flowering stress; no study consulted measured a season-long deficit in arabica.
- **Groundwater trends.** The rate at which coffee irrigation lowers the water table in the Central Highlands is not established by the sources consulted here.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Guerra et al., 2005 – Water management to line up flowering.</strong> Compared year-round irrigation, two levels of dry-season stress and rainfed arabica under a centre pivot in the Brazilian Cerrado. Stopping irrigation from mid-June until −2.0 MPa gave one uniform flowering, over 80% ripe cherry at harvest and higher yields than irrigating all year.</p>
<p class="meta">Embrapa Cerrados, Comunicado Técnico 122 · <a href="https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/569841/1/comtec122.pdf">PDF</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Byrareddy et al., 2020 – Win-win irrigation in Vietnam.</strong> Analysed ten years of irrigation and yield records from 558 robusta farms in the Central Highlands. Many farms applied more water than needed; the authors estimate that irrigation could be cut by 300–590 m³/ha in normal years while average yields stay above 3,000 kg/ha.</p>
<p class="meta">Agricultural Water Management 241, 106350 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agwat.2020.106350">doi:10.1016/j.agwat.2020.106350</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Amarasinghe et al., 2015 – More coffee with less water.</strong> Examined irrigation in Vietnam’s robusta and proposed 120–150 mm from January to April, about 70% of the local recommendation. Combined with better input management, the authors expect average yields to rise from 2,400 to up to 4,000 kg/ha.</p>
<p class="meta">Agricultural Systems 136, 96–105 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agsy.2015.02.008">doi:10.1016/j.agsy.2015.02.008</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Sakai et al., 2015 – Drip irrigation and planting arrangement.</strong> Followed coffee in six planting arrangements with and without drip irrigation over five years. Irrigation raised average yield from 1,026 to 2,623 kg/ha and increased root concentration.</p>
<p class="meta">Agricultural Water Management 148, 16–23 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agwat.2014.08.020">doi:10.1016/j.agwat.2014.08.020</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Pereira et al., 2011 – Crop coefficient from leaf area.</strong> Related the coffee crop coefficient to leaf area index for precision irrigation. Kc rises linearly up to a leaf area index of 3, peaks near 1.05 and falls towards 0.7 in very dense canopies.</p>
<p class="meta">Bragantia 70(4), 946–951 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1590/S0006-87052011000400030">doi:10.1590/S0006-87052011000400030</a></p>
</div>
