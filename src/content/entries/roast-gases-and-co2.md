## What roast gases are

Green coffee contains no more carbon dioxide than normal air, according to the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) freshness handbook.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Roasting changes that. Heat drives chemical reactions that turn part of the bean into gas.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A roasting textbook chapter names **carbon dioxide** (CO₂) as the main gas formed, with carbon monoxide and nitrogen as other important ones.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Volatile organic compounds, the aroma molecules, leave the roaster too.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Water vapour** also fills the bean. Part of it is the green coffee's own moisture, part is water formed by the same reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> By weight, water is by far the largest thing leaving the roaster. In a 2005 laboratory study, 10.2–11.4 g of water left per 100 g of green coffee, against 0.4–0.5 g of carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Water in roasting has its own entry. This entry is about the other gases, above all carbon dioxide: how it forms, and how much of it the bean keeps.

## Where the carbon dioxide comes from

According to the SCA handbook, two reactions contribute to the formation of carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

- **Strecker degradation.** This reaction runs alongside the Maillard reaction. It turns amino acids into aldehydes, which are aroma compounds, and gives off carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A roasting chemistry chapter describes it as a deamination and oxidative decarboxylation of the amino acid.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The amino acid loses its amino group and its acid group.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Pyrolysis.** Pyrolysis is breakdown by heat alone. Late in the roast, carbohydrates break down this way and release carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Other sources are named as well. A 2005 study, citing a coffee textbook, lists the breakdown of chlorogenic acids and of sucrose and the decarboxylation of amino acids.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A roasting chemistry chapter states that almost all free sugars are lost in roasting, giving water, carbon dioxide, colour, aroma and taste.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> How much each source contributes was not measured in any study consulted for this entry.

## When it forms

Gas formation is slow at first. According to the roasting chapter, in isothermal roasting it accelerates strongly in the second half of the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In the 2005 study, a fine probe sat in the core of a single bean. Carbon dioxide rose in relevant amounts only once the core reached 180–200 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> First crack is reported in the same temperature range (see first crack).

According to the SCA handbook, the Maillard route gives most carbon dioxide up to and around first crack. Pyrolysis adds carbon dioxide only in darker roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the 2005 study, carbon dioxide in the exhaust rose sharply at the end of the fast roast. The authors suggested the roast was moving towards pyrolysis.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Released or retained

Not all the gas leaves in the roaster. According to the roasting chapter, part escapes during roasting, and a major part stays trapped in the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The 2005 study measured both parts. Carbon dioxide in the exhaust came to 0.4–0.5 g per 100 g of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Cooling added at most 0.1 g, as estimated.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Sealed whole beans then released 0.83–0.99 g in 63 days of storage.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

So about a third left in the roaster and cooler and about two thirds afterwards. This is our calculation from the study's values, and it treats the 63-day total as all the gas formed.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The trapped gas sits in pores, is bound to the bean's large molecules, or is dissolved in the oil and the remaining water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Its slow release after roasting is degassing, which has its own entry.

## Pressure, swelling and cracks

Trapped gas pushes outwards. According to the roasting chapter, gas pressure is the main force that swells the bean, while the thick cell walls hold against it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Water vapour adds to the push.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The walls stretch rather than break, so the bean swells steadily, not in one burst.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

In places the structure does give way. The roasting chapter describes small cracks that release a little gas in a sudden burst, heard as cracking and popping.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The SCA handbook explains first crack as pressure from water vapour and carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It explains second crack as a renewed build-up once the bean has become brittle.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry measured the pressure directly; only calculated values are cited (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The same pressure moves oil. According to the roasting chapter, it pushes coffee oil through tiny channels in the cell walls to the surface.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> First crack, porosity and cell structure, and the glass–rubber transition each have their own entry.

## Roast degree and roast speed

**Darker roasts form more gas.** A doctoral thesis and the roasting chapter describe gas formation as rising with roast degree.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In one study, carbon dioxide left in whole beans more than doubled from light to dark roast. It rose no further in very dark roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In a second study by the same group, the trend reversed above dark roast.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Roast speed matters less.** In a 2018 weighing study, roast degree had a stronger effect than roast speed on the gas that whole beans released after roasting (see figure).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Fast roasts released more in medium and dark roasts, but not in light roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Whether fast roasts also form more gas in total is disputed (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Fast roasts build a more open bean.** In a 2000 study, beans roasted at high temperature had greater volume, more pore volume and larger pores in the cell walls than beans roasted at low temperature to the same degree.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Formation chemistry

**Strecker degradation.** A roasting chemistry chapter describes it as part of the Maillard reaction network. An amino acid loses its amino group and is oxidatively decarboxylated, giving a Strecker aldehyde.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Carbon dioxide is a by-product.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The aldehydes include malty 3-methylbutanal, potato-like methional and honey-like phenylacetaldehyde.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Strecker degradation has its own entry.

**Pyrolysis.** According to the SCA handbook, carbohydrates decompose by heat late in the roast, during caramelisation. This forms carbon dioxide alongside a large number of other compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the handbook's account, this route matters only in darker roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Chlorogenic acids.** A doctoral thesis on hot-air roasting reviews an earlier model of bean expansion in which chlorogenic acids are key components.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In that model, the acids decompose sharply near the glass transition temperature of the bean, with substantial carbon dioxide release.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured how much carbon dioxide comes from chlorogenic acids in a roasting bean. Chlorogenic acids have their own entry.

**Reaction conditions.** A second doctoral thesis describes the roasting bean as a set of tiny closed reactors: thick cell walls and no spaces between cells.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> High temperature comes with considerable pressure, and reactions may run in the gas phase or on cell-wall surfaces.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The thesis warns that results from model reactions in water must therefore be read with care.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Timing.** In the 2005 study, carbon dioxide and reaction water were expressed per degree of bean core temperature, to compare the two roasts on one scale. Both rose only above a core of 180–200 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Over the whole roast, the slow roast released more carbon dioxide in the exhaust, because it lasted much longer.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Retention mechanisms

The 2005 study lists the forms in which carbon dioxide stays in the bean. Part is bound to polar sites of the bean's polymers, part dissolved in the oil or in the remaining water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Another part may be trapped in collapsed amorphous zones.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

A doctoral thesis summarises older work on where the gas sits. One earlier author concluded that most carbon dioxide is held in the fine pores of the tissue.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In that view, gas in large cavities is probably lost during roasting through relatively large fissures.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Other authors suggested that most carbon dioxide in ground coffee is held by sorption, and only a minor part in collapsed structure.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The thesis states that neither the mechanisms of trapping and sorption nor the driving forces of degassing were fully understood.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Some gas may not leave dry coffee at all. The 2018 weighing study measured less gas than a method that extracts carbon dioxide with water.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The authors suggest that part of the carbon dioxide is adsorbed in the coffee and released only when the coffee meets water.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> They call for studies of this “free” versus “bound” gas.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Structure decides how much can escape during the roast. One study found that roasting conditions have a major impact on the bean's microstructure.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> At the same roast degree, high-temperature roasting gave more pore volume and larger micropores.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A thesis reviewing the literature reports that, despite the pressure, scanning electron microscopy showed no evidence of cell walls breaking.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> It attributes this, probably, to the walls turning from glassy to rubbery at high temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Gas in the roaster exhaust

**Measuring it.** The 2005 study analysed the exhaust of an electrically heated laboratory fluid bed with an infrared gas analyser, for carbon dioxide and water vapour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Readings were corrected for the baseline of the hot air.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> With chaff weighed, the measured gases explained about 93–96% of the roast loss. The authors attribute the rest to abrasion, other gases and measurement inaccuracies.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Roast loss has its own entry.

**Aroma compounds.** In a 15.6 kg gas-heated drum, the off-gas was analysed online by mass spectrometry.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Volatile organic compounds stayed low for the first 500 s, then most rose strongly until the end of the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A change in the profile at about 730 s coincided with first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Fuel gases.** In production roasters, the beans are not the only source. The United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) expects carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide in roaster exhaust from burning the fuel, because roasters are typically natural-gas fired.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> For a batch roaster, its uncontrolled carbon dioxide factor is 180 lb/ton (≈90 kg/t) of green coffee, rated D (limited test data).<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The 2005 fluid bed, heated electrically, measured 4–5 kg/t from the beans during roasting (our conversion).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The two values come from different machines and methods. Roaster emissions has its own entry.

## Carbon monoxide

According to the SCA handbook, carbon monoxide forms during roasting together with carbon dioxide, in smaller amounts.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Like carbon dioxide, part stays in the bean. A 2019 emission study showed that roasted coffee can rapidly emit carbon monoxide.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Its authors recommend monitoring storage spaces and state that “Storage spaces may need to be ventilated to control CO concentrations to safe levels.”<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The degassing entry covers measured exposures and occupational limits; roaster emissions covers the exhaust.

## Measurement methods

| Method | What it measures | Used for | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Infrared gas analyser in the exhaust | CO₂ and water vapour leaving the roaster, online | Gas released during roasting | Geiger et al.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Pressure rise in a closed flask | Gas released by the beans after roasting | Storage release over 63 days | Geiger et al.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Chemical (sorbent) method | CO₂ left in the beans after roasting | Residual CO₂ by roast degree | Wang & Lim<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> |
| Gravimetric | Weight of all gas released by beans or grounds | Gas released after roasting, by roast degree and speed | Smrke et al.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> |
| Online mass spectrometry (PTR-ToF-MS) | Volatile organic compounds in the off-gas | Aroma formation over the roast | Wieland et al.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> |

Each method counts a different part of the gas: what leaves the roaster, what stays in the bean, or what leaves afterwards.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The gravimetric study notes that earlier methods were either specific to carbon dioxide or assumed the released gas was pure carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Bases differ too: per 100 g of green coffee<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> or per gram of roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Formation kinetics.** A 2017 heat and mass transfer model left carbon dioxide production out of its calculations, because there was not enough experimental data to fit the parameters.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Contribution of each precursor.** No study consulted for this entry measured how much carbon dioxide comes from amino acids, sugars, chlorogenic acids or polysaccharides in a roasting bean.
- **Pressure in the bean.** Only calculated values exist; none was measured directly in the sources consulted.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Trapping and sorption.** The mechanisms that hold carbon dioxide in the bean were not fully understood as of 2008.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The split between free and bound gas needs further study.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Other coffees and roasters.** The 2005 authors noted that their measurements need to be extended to other coffees, origins and roasting conditions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Geiger et al., 2005 – Carbon dioxide during roasting and storage.</strong> Measured carbon dioxide and water vapour online in the exhaust of an electrically heated laboratory fluid bed, for a fast and a slow roast to the same colour, then gas release from the beans for 63 days. Carbon dioxide formed from a bean core of 180–200 °C; the larger part left after roasting, and the authors read the totals of both roasts as almost equal.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 70(2), E124–E130 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb07084.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb07084.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Smrke et al., 2018 – Gas released by nine roast profiles.</strong> Weighed whole beans of one arabica coffee, roasted to three degrees at three speeds, for at least 400 h at 35 °C. Roast degree mattered more than roast speed; faster medium and dark roasts released more gas, and part of the carbon dioxide seemed to leave only with water.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 66(21), 5293–5300 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.7b03310">doi:10.1021/acs.jafc.7b03310</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Wang & Lim, 2014 – Residual CO₂ by roast degree.</strong> Measured the carbon dioxide left in arabica beans roasted fast and slow in a fluid bed to four degrees. Residual carbon dioxide more than doubled from light to dark, did not rise further in very dark roasts, and did not differ significantly between the two speeds.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Research International 61, 144–151 · <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0963996914000337">ScienceDirect</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Schenker et al., 2000 – Pore structure and roast conditions.</strong> Compared beans roasted at high and low temperature to the same degree. High-temperature beans had greater volume, more pore volume and larger micropores in the cell walls, a pore structure that, the authors state, controls mass transfer during roasting and storage.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 65(3), 452–457 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2000.tb16026.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2000.tb16026.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Wieland et al., 2012 – Off-gas monitored online.</strong> Followed volatile organic compounds in the off-gas of a 15–20 kg drum roaster in real time by mass spectrometry. Aroma compounds stayed low for the first 500 s and then rose strongly, with a change in the profile at first crack.</p>
<p class="meta">Analytical and Bioanalytical Chemistry 402, 2531–2543 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00216-011-5401-9">doi:10.1007/s00216-011-5401-9</a></p>
</div>
