## One species, several names

*Coffea canephora* is one of the two species that give almost all the world’s coffee. Its seeds are traded as robusta. Kew’s Plants of the World Online (POWO) lists it as a tree of the wet tropics.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

“Robusta” is not a variety in the botanical sense. A plant once described as its own species, *Coffea robusta*, is now listed as a synonym of *C. canephora*. So is the rank “subvariety *robusta*”.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The species was first divided into a Kouillou and a Robusta type; the Brazilian conilon comes from a single selection of the Kouillou type.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In trade, “robusta” stands for the whole species. The ICE robusta futures contract, for example, accepts robusta from any origin.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

## Where it grows wild

The species is native from West tropical Africa to South Sudan and northern Angola. POWO lists 18 countries and areas, from Guinea and Ivory Coast to the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Uganda and Tanzania.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2006 review places its home in the lowland forests of the Congo River basin, reaching Lake Victoria in Uganda. There it grew as a midstorey tree in dense equatorial rainforest.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The wild populations fall into two main genetic groups. The Guinean group comes from Guinea and Ivory Coast; the Congolese group comes mainly from Central Africa.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2013 study links this pattern to climatic refuges during the last ice age.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The coffee species index shows *C. canephora* among all the accepted species, with its native countries.

## A plant that needs a partner

Most arabica flowers pollinate themselves. *C. canephora* cannot: it is self-incompatible, meaning a flower rejects pollen from its own plant.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It is an outcrossing species, so its seedlings vary from tree to tree.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

This shapes how farms are planted. A clone is a group of plants grown from cuttings of one tree, so all are genetically identical. Planting clones that cannot pollinate each other can cost yield.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Farms therefore plant several clones that can pollinate one another, or seed from controlled crosses between selected parents.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Conilon in Brazil

Brazil grows *C. canephora* mainly as conilon. Significant production began in the 1960s, especially in Espírito Santo state.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Most commercial conilon fields are planted with clonal cultivars, each made of several genotypes.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Several of these cultivars came from mass selection, the choice of the best trees within a population.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> As of the USDA’s July 2026 forecast, Brazil is the second-largest robusta producer after Vietnam.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## Climate: less robust than its name

Robusta has long been seen as a warm-lowland coffee. A 2006 review gives an optimum annual mean temperature of 22–26 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A 2020 study of yields on 798 farms in South-East Asia found the optimum below 20.5 °C instead.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Above its thresholds, each 1 °C rise in growing-season temperature went with yields about 14% lower.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Arabica tolerates cold better than *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## How it differs from arabica in the cup

Green robusta holds more caffeine than green arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> A 2021 review gives 0.9–1.5% of dry weight for arabica and 1.2–2.4% for robusta (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Arabica holds more sucrose and trigonelline, two flavour precursors in roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Green arabica averages about 15% lipids (oils and waxes), robusta around 10%.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Genome and origin of arabica

*C. canephora* is diploid, with 2n = 2x = 22 chromosomes, and highly heterozygous. A 2014 study sequenced its genome of about 710 Mb.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> *C. arabica* (2n = 4x = 44) arose from a hybridisation between *C. canephora* and *C. eugenioides*, which makes *C. canephora* one of arabica’s two parent species.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How old that hybridisation is is treated in [The coffee genus](/entries/coffea-genus/).

## Genetic groups

Microsatellite studies divide the species into a Guinean and a Congolese group.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2013 analysis of 293 trees from 17 populations found six subgroups, two Guinean and four Congolese, and linked the structure to refuge zones during the Last Glacial Maximum.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The Guinean group, separated geographically by the Dahomey Gap, corresponds to diversity group D; groups A, B, C and E occur in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Cameroon and the Central African Republic.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

According to a 2017 textbook chapter, the subgroups are labelled D (Guinean), A or SG1 (conilon), B or SG2 (Central African Republic), E (Congolese), C (Cameroon), O (Uganda) and R (Democratic Republic of the Congo).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The same chapter describes the Kouillou type as a small group of low diversity, from which a single selection gave conilon.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Group letters differ slightly between studies; the A–E labels above are the ones both sources share.

## Self-incompatibility and planting design

The incompatibility in *C. canephora* is gametophytic: pollen is rejected when it shares an S-allele with the receiving plant.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2018 study in Rondônia, Brazil, made 80 controlled crosses in a balanced diallel and assigned 12 superior genotypes to three compatibility groups. It notes that planting incompatible clones together can compromise yield, and that knowing each clone’s group allows clones to be arranged in the field accordingly.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The authors caution that their results should not be used to justify planting only a few clones.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Breeding follows from the same trait. Programmes select clones for vegetative propagation, or clonal hybrids propagated by seed from controlled pollination.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The chapter describes mass selection, applied mainly in Brazil and Vietnam, and hybridisation between distant genetic groups, extended in Ivory Coast to reciprocal recurrent selection.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Rooted cuttings are used mostly for robusta clones, and grafting and in vitro somatic embryos also propagate them.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Arabica in turn is often grafted onto *C. canephora* rootstock to control nematodes, and grafted plants have shown better drought resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Plant habit and fruit cycle

POWO classes *C. canephora* as a tree.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Arabica, by contrast, evolved as a shrub of the forest undergrowth in south-western Ethiopia and northern Kenya.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In a 2025 Brazilian trial, each conilon plant was grown with three upright (orthotropic) stems.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

The time from flowering to harvest varies with clone and site. The same 2025 trial at a transitional altitude recorded 252–308 days from anthesis to harvest across conilon genotypes. It cites earlier studies giving 216–238 days for early-maturing genotypes at low altitude.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> For comparison, a Cenicafé study on arabica var. Colombia found the best cups from fruit picked 210–224 days after flowering.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> The two studies measured different things at different sites, so they do not establish a fixed gap between the species.

## Climate in detail

The 2006 review gives 18–21 °C as the optimum mean annual temperature for arabica, and for robusta 22–26 °C, or 24–30 °C after another author.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The review compiles these estimates from earlier authors.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The 2020 study instead modelled ten years of yield records from 798 farms in South-East Asia with hierarchical Bayesian methods.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> It found an optimum below 20.5 °C, or mean minimum and maximum temperatures of ≤16.2 °C and ≤24.1 °C. Above those values, every 1 °C rise in mean minimum or maximum temperature during the growing season corresponded to yield losses of about 14%, or 350–460 kg/ha.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

A 2017 textbook chapter describes climatic clusters for robusta growing (wet, hot-dry, dry and cool-wet) and states that fine robustas come mainly from the cool-wet cluster, with examples from mountainous regions of Uganda.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Climatic limits, frost and site choice for both species are treated in Site selection and climate.

## Production by country

As of its July 2026 report, the USDA forecasts world coffee production for 2026/27 at a record 189.7 million 60-kg bags, of which 83.8 million are robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> For 2025/26 it estimates 84.4 million bags of robusta out of 178.8 million, about 47% by our calculation.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Vietnam leads with a forecast 31.4 million bags of robusta, followed by Brazil (24.4 million), Indonesia (10.0 million), Uganda (6.0 million) and India (4.6 million).<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> These are forecasts and estimates by marketing year, not final harvests.

## Fine robusta and specialty robusta

Robusta has long been sold mainly as a commercial grade. In a series of workshops in Uganda, coffee professionals cupped robusta from several origins side by side. The outcome was a robusta cupping form, a cupping protocol and a green robusta grading handbook.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The Coffee Quality Institute (CQI) and the Uganda Coffee Development Authority (UCDA) drew up a classification system for fine robusta, and the name “fine robusta” was chosen to set these coffees apart from “specialty arabica”.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a>,<a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> According to the CQI, these standards and procedures are similar to those for specialty arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>

The robusta cupping form records attributes that include a bitter/sweet and a salt/acid aspect ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> According to the same chapter, robusta coffees show greater differences in cup quality than arabica coffees, which the authors attribute to cross-pollination.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Grading rules and cupping scores are covered in Green coffee grading and Cupping.

## Open questions

- How far the 2020 temperature optimum, measured on South-East Asian farms, holds for conilon in Brazil and for robusta in Africa is not established in the sources consulted for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- Whether *C. canephora* contains more chlorogenic acids than arabica in a consistent way remains disputed (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- No source consulted for this entry gives a sourced, species-wide flowering-to-harvest period for both species under the same conditions.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Kath et al., 2020 – Not so robust.</strong> Modelled ten years of yield records from 798 robusta farms in South-East Asia against temperature. Found an optimum below 20.5 °C, 1.5–9 °C below earlier estimates, and yields about 14% lower per 1 °C rise in growing-season mean minimum or maximum temperature above 16.2/24.1 °C.</p>
<p class="meta">Global Change Biology 26(6), 3677–3688 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.15097">doi:10.1111/gcb.15097</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Denoeud et al., 2014 – The robusta genome.</strong> Sequenced and assembled the genome of *C. canephora*. Compared its caffeine genes with those of other plants and concluded that caffeine biosynthesis in coffee evolved convergently.</p>
<p class="meta">Science 345(6201), 1181–1184 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1255274">doi:10.1126/science.1255274</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Cubry et al., 2013 – Diversity across the Guineo-Congolese region.</strong> Genotyped 293 wild and cultivated trees from 17 populations with 39 microsatellite markers. Found a Guinean and a Congolese group with six subgroups, shaped by ice-age forest refuges and later migration.</p>
<p class="meta">Genetic Resources and Crop Evolution 60(2), 483–501 · <a href="https://agritrop.cirad.fr/567363">agritrop.cirad.fr/567363</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Moraes et al., 2018 – Compatibility groups of superior clones.</strong> Made controlled crosses between elite genotypes in Rondônia, Brazil, and sorted 12 of them into three compatibility groups. Notes that planting incompatible clones can compromise yield and that known groups let growers arrange clones in the field.</p>
<p class="meta">Genetics and Molecular Research 17(1), gmr16039876 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.4238/gmr16039876">doi:10.4238/gmr16039876</a></p>
</div>
