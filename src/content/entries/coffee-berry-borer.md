## What the coffee berry borer is

The coffee berry borer (*Hypothenemus hampei*) is a small beetle. It is one of the few herbivores that feed on the coffee seed itself.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2020 review and a 2018 study both call it the most damaging insect pest of coffee worldwide.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In Spanish it is called *broca*.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The beetle spends almost its whole life hidden inside the coffee berry. Females spend most of their lives there.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Males never leave it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to a 2017 textbook chapter, coffee berries are its only host.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> It attacks and develops in both arabica (*C. arabica*) and robusta (*C. canephora*).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same book notes that cultivated coffee varieties lack genetic resistance to it.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## How the beetle lives inside the berry

A mated female flies to a berry and bores a hole, usually through the disc.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2017 textbook chapter places the hole at the tip of the fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> She tunnels through the pulp into one seed and digs galleries there. Along them she lays her eggs.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The larvae hatch and feed on the seed tissue.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> They pupate inside the seed and become adults there.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Far more daughters than sons are born: about ten females for each male, according to a Colombian review.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The young females mate with their brothers before they leave.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Males cannot fly and never leave the berry.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The mated females then leave through the entry hole and look for new berries.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> At 24.5 °C, one cycle from egg to adult takes about 27.5 days.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Depending on climate, the beetle completes between 2 and 13 generations a year.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Researchers describe four positions of the founder female, named A to D.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In A she is starting to enter a new berry but has not yet pierced the skin. In B she is in the pulp. In C she has begun to bore into the seed. In D she has made a gallery in the seed and breeds there.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The positions matter for control, as explained below.

## Which berries it attacks

Females can attack young berries, from about 70 days after flowering.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> They breed only in older fruit, when the seed has more than about 20% dry weight.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> That is roughly 120–150 days after flowering.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Berries left on the tree or fallen to the ground keep the beetles alive between crops.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In a Colombian trial, 30.4–58.3% of the fruit on the ground was infested.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> These berries are the source of the next attack.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## What it does to the crop and the cup

The beetle eats the part of the berry that is sold. According to a 2015 study, infestations can reduce yield by up to 80%.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> According to a Colombian review, borer damage cost dry parchment coffee 18.1% of its weight on average.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Losses worldwide have been estimated at more than USD 500 million a year (estimate cited in 2011 and 2020).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Damaged seeds also lower the grade. The beetle leaves holes and tunnels in the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) physical assessment counts insect damage as a defect.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> For severe insect damage, 5 beans count as 1 full category 1 defect.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> For slight insect damage, 10 beans count as 1 category 2 defect.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

According to a 2017 textbook chapter, the holes open a way for bacteria and moulds.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The chapter reports that heavily damaged beans can give fermented or mouldy off-flavours.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> For premium markets, it states, the tolerance is nil.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## From Africa to Hawaii

The beetle comes from Africa, but where exactly is unclear.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Its original host was probably robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It was first described in 1867, under the name *Cryphalus hampei*.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The first report from Africa came from Liberia in 1897.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

It was reported in Java, Indonesia, in 1908.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It reached Brazil in 1913, at Campinas in São Paulo state.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Colombia first reported it in 1988, in Nariño in the south.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Puerto Rico confirmed it officially in 2007.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

In August 2010 it was found in Kona, on Hawaii Island.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It was later confirmed on O‘ahu (2014), Maui (2016), Kaua‘i (2020) and Lāna‘i (2020).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Papua New Guinea detected it on smallholder farms in early 2017.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Warmer climates, higher farms

The beetle is sensitive to temperature. In a 2009 laboratory study, it developed from egg to adult only between 20 and 30 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Below about 15 °C and above about 32 °C it could not develop.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Cool, high farms were long spared. For decades, no attacks above 1,500 m were reported.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> A 2011 study dates the first such reports to the previous ten years.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2009 study cites researchers in Uganda and Indonesia who reported attacks as high as 1,864 m.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> On Mount Kilimanjaro, Tanzania, it moved about 300 m uphill in ten years.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

A 2011 modelling study projects more generations a year across East Africa's arabica zones by 2050.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The links between shade, altitude and the beetle are covered under shade and agroforestry and in site selection and climate.

## How producers control it

No single method controls the borer. Programmes combine harvest practice, biological control, traps and, where used, insecticides.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Clean harvesting.** Producers pick ripe berries often: every two to three weeks in one Colombian study.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> After the main harvest, a last pass collects every ripe, overripe and dry fruit from the tree and the ground. In Colombia this pass is called *repase*.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Colombian guidance aims for fewer than five fruits per tree after harvest.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Hawaii uses a similar end-of-season “strip picking”.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Fungus.** The fungus *Beauveria bassiana* infects the borer naturally in almost all regions of Colombia.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It is also sprayed as a biological insecticide.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> It reaches the female mainly while she is in position A or B, before she is protected inside the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Wasps.** Three small African wasps that attack the borer were brought to Colombia through quarantine.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> In a Colombian pilot in 2021–2022, released wasps cut infested berries per branch by about half or more.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Traps.** Traps baited with methanol and ethanol catch flying females.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> They are used mainly to see when females are flying.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Insecticides.** Control has relied mainly on synthetic insecticides, with mixed results.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Like the fungus, they reach the female mainly before she enters the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> We describe their use; we do not recommend products.

## At the mill

In a Colombian study, adult beetles escaped at every stage of wet processing.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Most escaped while floaters and low-grade beans were drying.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Citing earlier Cenicafé work, the publication recommends treating these with hot water or drying them under covered structures.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

A Cenicafé guide advises drying fruit collected from the ground in a mechanical dryer, or solarising it under plastic for 48 h.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Heated drying kills the borer stages left in parchment coffee, according to a Colombian review.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Flotation and sorting of damaged fruit are covered in harvesting and sorting.

## Biology and reproduction

**Classification.** *Hypothenemus hampei* (Ferrari, 1867) is a bark beetle (Coleoptera: Curculionidae: Scolytinae).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> It feeds on coffee endosperm, which few herbivores can, because it copes with caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Mating system.** Reproduction is highly inbred: almost all mating occurs between siblings inside the natal berry.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Males have atrophied wing muscles and cannot fly.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> They are smaller than females, with vestigial wings and rudimentary eyes, and never leave the berry.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The sex ratio is strongly biased towards females, but published estimates range from 5:1 to 494:1.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Egg laying.** A Colombian review gives an egg-laying period of about 20 days at two to three eggs a day.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2020 review states that females can lay more than 100 eggs.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the 2009 laboratory study, total fecundity of the founder female was highest at 20 °C (296.9 eggs) and lowest at 30 °C (64.3 eggs).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The values are not directly comparable (see Contested). No source consulted for this entry states the lifespan of the female with a measured value, so it is left out.

**Timing in the fruit.** Females start to search for berries about 100 days after flowering and usually lay eggs about 20 days later.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A Colombian review states that the beetle can attack fruit from 70 days after flowering but reproduces only in fruit older than 120 days with more than 20% dry weight.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2020 review states that females colonise berries when the seed reaches 20% dry weight, about 120–150 days after flowering.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Inside the berry: micro-CT

A 2018 study scanned infested berries with micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), which images the inside in 3D without opening the berry.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> One berry held 47 insects: the founder female, 26 larvae, 12 pupae and 8 unhatched eggs.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> All were in one of the two seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The female had entered from the side near the stalk, not through the disc, although the disc is the usual route.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Her galleries branched inside the seed. Second-instar larvae built pupal cells.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The authors suggest, as a hypothesis, that eggs are placed from the edge of the seed inwards.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Temperature limits and generations

The 2009 study reared the borer at eight constant temperatures from 15 to 35 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Egg-to-adult development succeeded between 20 and 30 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> With linear regression and a modified Logan model, the lower and upper thresholds were estimated at 14.9 and 32 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Completing the pre-reproductive phase needed 262.47 degree-days above 14.9 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

At 15 °C and 35 °C no eggs were laid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At 35 °C, founder females stayed in position B and did not reach the endosperm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At 33 °C females laid eggs, but 95% of first-instar larvae died after hatching.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The intrinsic rate of increase peaked at about 26.7 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The model predicts an average 8.5% rise in the maximum rate of increase for every 1 °C rise in the thermal optimum.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

From weather data, the study estimated 0–4.71 generations a year across sites in Colombia, Kenya, Tanzania and Ethiopia.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At Jimma, Ethiopia, the climate was too cold before 1984 for even one generation a year; after 1984, one to two were possible.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Hawaii, a cited study reported 2.1–4.9 generations a year.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Altitude and climate change

A 2011 study states that until ten years earlier, no attacks on coffee plantations above 1,500 m had been reported.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2017 textbook chapter says the same of the period until the 1990s.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The 2009 study cites personal communications from Uganda and Indonesia reporting attacks as high as 1,864 m.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> On Mount Kilimanjaro the borer moved about 300 m uphill in ten years.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

The 2011 study used the CLIMEX model with two emission scenarios (A2 and B2) for 2050.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> As of 2011 the borer completed 1–4.5 generations a year in East Africa.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> For 2050, the model projects 5–10 generations at 1,400–1,800 m and 11–16 at 900–1,300 m.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors suggest shade trees as one adaptation.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

In a 2015 study in eastern Uganda, the borer was more common on sun-exposed plantations.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The abstract reports that the shade effect depended on altitude only for another pest, the white stem borer.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

## Caffeine and the gut microbiome

Caffeine can be toxic to insects and is thought to defend the plant against herbivores.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A 2015 study showed that caffeine is broken down in the borer’s gut.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> When the gut bacteria were removed with antibiotics, the beetle lost this ability.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Borers from seven major coffee-producing countries and from laboratory colonies shared a core of gut microorganisms.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Several *Pseudomonas* species among them can live on caffeine as their only source of carbon and nitrogen.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Their caffeine demethylase genes were active in the gut. Reinoculating treated beetles with one *Pseudomonas* strain restored caffeine breakdown.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The plant side is covered in caffeine.

## Damage, losses and quality

**Yield and weight.** According to a 2015 study, infestations can reduce yield by up to 80%.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A Colombian review reports an average weight loss of 18.1% in dry parchment coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Global losses are estimated at more than USD 500 million a year.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> For Brazil, losses have been estimated at USD 215–358 million a year.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In Hawaii, the economy-wide loss in crop value for 2011/12 and 2012/13 was estimated at USD 12.7 million.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> These estimates use different bases and years.

**Grading.** The SCA’s Coffee Value Assessment (CVA) converts defects into full defect equivalents.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Severe insect damage is a category 1 defect, 5 beans to 1 full defect. Slight insect damage is a category 2 defect, 10 beans to 1.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The full grading system is explained in green coffee grading standards.

**Cup and safety.** According to a 2017 textbook chapter, larvae damage the structure and composition of the endosperm.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The same chapter reports that the holes give access to fungi linked to ochratoxin A, citing earlier studies.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> No primary study on this link was consulted for this entry.

## Integrated pest management

The 2020 review groups borer management into monitoring and sampling, and cultural, biological, chemical and physical control.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Published plans for Hawaii combine field monitoring, *B. bassiana* and other insecticides, field sanitation and post-harvest control.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In Hawaii, growers could claim 75% of the cost of *B. bassiana* from 2014 to 2016.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Cultural control.** On a small Colombian farm, ripe and dry berries were collected every two to three weeks for two years, plus one *repase* after each main harvest.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Green berries stayed at 1–3.9% infestation.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Citing earlier Colombian work, the authors describe the method as highly effective when fewer than 5 such berries per tree remain after harvest, and ineffective when more than 10 remain.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A Cenicafé guide sets the same target of fewer than five fruits per tree.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In its trials, collecting fallen fruit with a basket reduced infestation by up to 73.6% against plots without collection.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Biological control: fungus.** *B. bassiana* has been the most common biological control on coffee farms since the borer arrived, according to a 2017 textbook chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> In Hawaii, 20–70% of females were in the exposed A/B position early in the season.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Later they were protected inside the endosperm, which explains why applications after July were less effective.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In Colombian field trials cited in the same study, conidia applied to fallen berries infected about 30–40% of borers and reduced later tree infestation by up to 50%.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Biological control: parasitoids.** Colombia introduced *Cephalonomia stephanoderis*, *Prorops nasuta* and *Phymastichus coffea* through quarantine in England.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The two bethylid wasps attack all life stages inside infested berries; *P. coffea* is an endoparasitoid of adult borers.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In the Colombian pilot (2021–2022, published 2023), more than 1.5 million wasps of each of two species, *P. nasuta* and *P. coffea*, were released on a 61-hectare farm.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> *P. coffea* releases were timed to females entering fruit (positions A and B).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Infested fruits per branch fell by 77.5% in 2021 and by 51.1% in 2022 on the release farm, against plots without releases.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The authors judge the area-wide strategy effective in this pilot.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Traps and repellents.** In trap studies, 3:1 and 1:1 methanol:ethanol lures attracted borers equally well.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Some chemicals with repellent properties have been identified.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> According to a 2017 textbook chapter, repellent plants have been identified, with field tests under way at the time.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Chemical control.** Endosulfan became the most widely used insecticide against the borer in the 1960s. The borer developed resistance, and by 2013 the compound was banned in at least 70 countries.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2020 review reports chlorpyrifos as the only insecticide in use against the borer in Papua New Guinea.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2023 study states that in Colombia chemical control is not traditionally used in plantations in their first and second harvest, because borer numbers there are usually low.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A Colombian review gives a threshold of 2% infestation for the periods between harvests.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Post-harvest and mill hygiene

A participatory study on Colombian farms found adult borers escaping at every stage of wet processing.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Most escapes happened while floaters and low-grade beans (*pasillas*) were drying.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Citing earlier Cenicafé work, the publication recommends hot water or drying under covered structures for these fractions, and applying *B. bassiana* to the pulp.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In a 1998 participatory survey of 115 producers at the start of the study, fewer than 7% took any borer control measure during processing.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

Cenicafé advises drying collected fallen fruit in a mechanical dryer, or solarising it under plastic for 48 h.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Drying with heat kills the borer stages left in parchment coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry gives a drying temperature that kills larvae, so none is given.

## Open questions

- **Origin.** Where in Africa the borer and its first host come from is not settled.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Female lifespan.** Published values circulate, but no source consulted for this entry measures it, so it is left out.
- **Papua New Guinea date.** The 2020 review gives early 2017 in its text and 2018 in a figure caption; an earlier incursion in 2009 was eradicated.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Biological control at scale.** Parasitoid releases reduced infestation in a pilot on one 61-hectare farm<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>; no study consulted for this entry tests them across whole regions.
- **Current distribution.** No country list dated 2026 was consulted for this entry (as of October 2026); see Contested.
