brief:
  - Moisture content is how much water the coffee contains by weight; the Codex code and the ISO oven method express it on a wet basis (wb), as a share of the wet sample.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
  - Water activity (aw, 0 to 1) measures how available that water is to microorganisms and chemical reactions; it is not the same as moisture content.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
  - The Codex code of practice considers parchment coffee protected from fungi at below 12.5% moisture (wb) and a maximum water activity of 0.67–0.70.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
  - Moisture meters should be calibrated against the ISO 6673 oven method, which dries about 10 g of green coffee at 105 °C for 16 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Two different numbers

**Moisture content** tells you how much water the coffee contains. **Water activity** tells you how much of that water is free – unbound and available to microorganisms for growth.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Formally, water activity is the ratio of the partial pressure of water in a food to the vapour pressure of pure water at the same temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It runs from 0 to 1 and is directly related to the equilibrium relative humidity (ERH) of the air around the sample.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The two are linked, but not one-to-one. The curve that connects them at a given temperature is called the **sorption isotherm**, and it is specific to each food – its "fingerprint".<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It can even differ between lots of the same coffee: in parchment coffee, the ripeness at which the cherries were picked changed how available the water in the bean was.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Temperature matters too: at a constant water activity, warmer Arabica coffee holds less water.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Wet basis and dry basis

Moisture content can be expressed in two ways:<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

- **Wet basis (wb)** – mass of water per unit mass of the *wet* sample. This is the basis of the Codex code and of the ISO 6673 oven method, and the one a university extension guide uses for traded green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Dry basis (db)** – mass of water per unit mass of *completely dry* matter. Sorption research often reports this, for example as g of water per 100 g of dry solids.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

Conversion, with both values in per cent:

- M<sub>db</sub> = 100 × M<sub>wb</sub> / (100 − M<sub>wb</sub>)
- M<sub>wb</sub> = 100 × M<sub>db</sub> / (100 + M<sub>db</sub>)

So the Codex limit of 12.5% wb equals 14.3% db, and a freshly demucilaged bean at 50% wb holds 100% db – as much water as dry matter. The gap grows with moisture, which is why the basis must always be stated.

## How water leaves the seed

Coffee is hygroscopic: it loses or gains water until it reaches equilibrium with the surrounding air. It cannot be dried below the equilibrium moisture content (EMC) set by the temperature and humidity of the drying air.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The Codex code gives the practical consequence for storage and transport: below 60% relative humidity coffee keeps drying, above 80% it starts to absorb water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Washed coffee in particular can reabsorb moisture in humid environments.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Typical moisture along the chain:

1. **Harvest** – freshly harvested coffee holds about 50–60% moisture (wb), depending on the processing method and climate.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Across maturation stages, fruits are harvested at between 30% and 65% (wb).<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>
2. **Parchment after demucilaging** – about 45–55% moisture, whether the mucilage was fermented off or removed mechanically.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
3. **Dried** – reduced to 10–12% (wb) for safe storage.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Green coffee is generally dried to, and traded at, about 12%.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The Codex code says moisture should not exceed 12.5% anywhere from the loading area to roasting; where it states the basis, it is wet basis.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Why it matters: mould, toxins and quality

Above 12% (wb), green coffee can deteriorate through bacteria, mould or yeast, especially if the seed has died.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The Codex code ties protection from fungi to two limits together: moisture below 12.5% (wb) and a water activity of no more than 0.67–0.70.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Three different water-activity numbers are often confused. Below about 0.62, yeasts and moulds in foods in general usually do not grow.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The 0.67–0.70 figure is the Codex storage ceiling for parchment coffee, not a growth limit.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Below 0.76–0.78, the OTA-producing fungi of coffee cannot grow.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Rewetting dried coffee is to be avoided, because it favours rapid fungal growth and ochratoxin A production.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Even well below the mould threshold, water still matters. High temperature and high water content are the main reported causes of sensory change during storage.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## Sorption isotherms and hysteresis

An isotherm plots equilibrium moisture content against water activity at one temperature. For Arabica beans between 30 and 60 °C, the adsorption isotherms had a sigmoidal type II shape.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Raising the temperature at constant water activity lowered the equilibrium moisture content.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Models.** The Guggenheim–Anderson–de Boer (GAB) model fitted desorption data well for green coffee, pulped coffee and whole fruits at 10–50 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> It describes the isotherm with three parameters: the monolayer moisture content X<sub>m</sub>, a constant C for how strongly water binds to the monolayer, and a constant K for the multilayer.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In parchment coffee, K values close to one pointed to multilayer water behaving close to free water.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The GAB model is not always the best fit: for Arabica adsorption at 30–60 °C, the Peleg model performed best.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Hysteresis.** Drying follows a desorption isotherm, rewetting in storage an adsorption isotherm. The two studies consulted here measured one direction each – desorption in coffee fruits, pulped and green coffee,<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> adsorption in Arabica beans<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> – so the gap between the two curves is not quantified in this entry. In the desorption study, the differential enthalpy, entropy and Gibbs free energy rose as equilibrium moisture fell, and whole coffee fruits showed the highest values.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Ripeness.** In parchment coffee, cherry maturity changed water availability in the bean. Unripe stages lost water activity faster during drying, down to values (aw < 0.4) that the authors link to possible stalling after roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Glass transition

Green coffee is mainly a glassy structure that changes slowly during storage.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Water lowers its glass transition temperature (T<sub>g</sub>): as water activity rose from 0.155 to 0.512, T<sub>g</sub> of green coffee fell from 48.76 °C to 34.89 °C. Roasted coffee was less sensitive, falling from 45.73 °C to 40.15 °C over the same range.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Whether green coffee stored warm enough to pass above its T<sub>g</sub> ages faster has not been shown in the sources for this entry.

## Mould and ochratoxin A

In coffee, only *Aspergillus* species produce ochratoxin A (OTA): *A. ochraceus* and its relatives *A. westerdijkiae* and *A. steynii*, *A. niger* and relatives, and *A. carbonarius*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> These belong to *Aspergillus* sections *Circumdati* and *Nigri*.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> *Penicillium* also makes OTA, mainly in temperate and colder zones,<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> but the Codex code names only *Aspergillus* species for coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The water-activity bands below apply to these coffee *Aspergillus* species; they are not general limits for all moulds.

The Codex code describes water activity bands for OTA risk:<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **aw > 0.95** – fast-growing hydrophilic fungi and yeasts grow first; OTA producers are unlikely to take hold.
- **aw 0.80–0.95** – the range in which OTA-producing fungi can grow. The time coffee spends in this range on the drying yard is the key control point; in trials, 5 days or less prevented OTA accumulation.
- **aw < 0.80** – OTA producers can be present but do not produce the toxin.
- **aw < 0.76–0.78** – they cannot grow.

Laboratory work on stored green coffee agrees on the upper band: *Aspergillus westerdijkiae* produced most OTA at aw 0.95–0.97 and 30 °C. With elevated CO<sub>2</sub> (1000 ppm) and 35 °C, OTA production was stimulated at aw 0.90–0.95.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The authors note that the aw range of 0.90–0.97 used on the beans corresponds to roughly 40% down to 20–22% moisture (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Measurement

**Oven reference method (ISO 6673).** A test portion of about 10 g is heated at 105 °C for 16 h ± 0.5 h at atmospheric pressure.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Strictly, it measures loss in mass, which by convention and agreement between parties is used as water content. It reads about 1.0% lower than the ISO 1446 basic reference method.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> These parameters are from the 2003 edition. A third edition was published in 2025 and applies to both decaffeinated and non-decaffeinated green coffee;<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> its full text was not openly available to check whether the parameters changed. The Codex code asks for moisture measurements to be calibrated to ISO 6673.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The oven method is a convenient reference because it needs no calibration itself.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Capacitance meters.** The most common coffee moisture meters are adapted grain meters that measure capacitance; modern units also measure temperature and bulk density.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Any electronic device drifts with time, and the drift is corrected by calibration against a reference such as oven drying.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Repeatability – the spread of readings when the same sample is presented several times – is the real accuracy of a meter and cannot be removed by calibration.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Dielectric spectroscopy.** In research, the dielectric constant of parchment coffee predicted both moisture content and water activity (R² 0.82–0.88), with 0.3–0.9 GHz as the most useful range.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Water activity meters.** Chilled-mirror dewpoint instruments combine a dewpoint sensor, an infrared thermometer and a fan in one sensor block.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Capacitance aw sensors use a hygroscopic polymer between two plates whose ability to hold a charge rises as it adsorbs water.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Because the isotherm shifts with temperature, an aw reading should be reported with the sample temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Too wet** – above 12% (wb): deterioration by bacteria, mould or yeast; in live seeds, enzyme activity changes cup quality.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Slow drying** – too long in the aw 0.80–0.95 window gives OTA producers time to grow.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Rewetting** – favours rapid fungal growth and OTA production.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Washed coffee can pick up moisture in humid storage and become more brittle.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Humid storage and transport** – fluctuating temperature and relative humidity change moisture content, water activity, colour and sensory quality.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **Over-drying** – very low water activity (aw < 0.4) in unripe beans has been linked to possible stalling after roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **OTA above legal limits** – in the EU, roasted beans and ground roasted coffee may contain at most 3.0 µg/kg OTA, and soluble coffee at most 5.0 µg/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The regulation sets no OTA limit for green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>
- **Wrong basis** – a dry-basis value read as wet basis overstates moisture; at 12.5% wb the difference is 1.8 percentage points.

## Open questions

- There is no agreed single target water activity for green coffee in storage. The sources consulted here give upper limits for safety (Codex 0.67–0.70; about 0.65–0.70 in a 2020 study)<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> and, for specialty grade, the SCA's archived limit of below 0.70,<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> while the only lower target, 0.4–0.6 for preserving quality, appears in a 2023 study as a citation of earlier work rather than as its own finding (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- Adsorption and desorption isotherms measured on the same green coffee lot at storage temperatures are scarce in the sources used here, so the size of the hysteresis loop in practice is not quantified.
- How water activity is distributed across traded green coffee lots has not, to our knowledge, been reported in peer-reviewed work; the large datasets that exist come from the trade.
- The link between glass transition and flavour loss in storage is suggested by the physics but not yet demonstrated for coffee in these sources.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Goneli et al., 2013 – Water sorption of coffee fruits, pulped and green coffee.</strong> Measured desorption isotherms of three forms of coffee at five temperatures (10–50 °C) and 11–95% relative humidity. The GAB model fitted well, and whole fruits showed the highest thermodynamic values.</p>
<p class="meta">LWT – Food Science and Technology 50(2), 386–391 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lwt.2012.09.006">doi:10.1016/j.lwt.2012.09.006</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Velásquez et al., 2021 – Cherry maturity, sorption and dielectric sensing.</strong> Showed that ripeness stage changes the water availability of parchment coffee and its drying behaviour, with unripe beans reaching very low water activity. Dielectric measurements at 0.3–0.9 GHz predicted both moisture content and water activity.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Control 123, 107692 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodcont.2020.107692">doi:10.1016/j.foodcont.2020.107692</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Iaccheri et al., 2019 – Glass transition of green and roasted coffee.</strong> Used calorimetry and dielectric methods to track the glass transition at different water activities. In green coffee, T<sub>g</sub> fell by about 14 °C as aw rose from 0.155 to 0.512, a stronger effect than in roasted coffee.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry 301, 125187 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2019.125187">doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2019.125187</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Akbar et al., 2020 – <em>Aspergillus westerdijkiae</em> under climate stress.</strong> Tested growth and OTA production across water activity, temperature and CO<sub>2</sub> on coffee medium and stored beans. OTA peaked at aw 0.95–0.97 and 30 °C, and warmer, CO<sub>2</sub>-rich conditions stimulated production at slightly drier levels.</p>
<p class="meta">Microorganisms 8(9), 1268 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms8091268">doi:10.3390/microorganisms8091268</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Anokye-Bempah et al., 2023 – Desiccants in storage and transport.</strong> Examined how temperature and humidity swings during storage and shipping change the moisture, water activity, colour and sensory quality of green coffee, and tested desiccants as a way to keep beans in the recommended range. Over a simulated 42-day shipment from Brazil to Italy, hermetic packages with desiccants kept green coffee below 12% (wb) and below aw 0.6, while beans in jute sacks rose to 17.34% (wb) and aw 0.76.</p>
