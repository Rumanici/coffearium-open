## Two sources of water

Water enters the roaster with the green coffee. A roasting textbook chapter gives a typical 10–12% (wet basis, wb), and a 2002 roasting study gives the same range (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> By the end of the roast only a few percent is left.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> How this moisture is measured in green coffee is explained under moisture and water activity.

The roast also makes new water. Chemical reactions in the bean produce it, and it evaporates too.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> These reactions include Strecker degradation, the breakdown of chlorogenic acids and sucrose, and the Maillard reaction.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Researchers call this reaction water, to set it apart from the green coffee's own water.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

A 2005 Swiss laboratory study measured both. It roasted 100 g batches with 8.3 g water in a fluid bed, once fast and once slow.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> More water left the roaster than the beans had brought in: 10.2 g in the fast roast and 11.4 g in the slow one.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Reaction water made up 41% and 36% of it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## How water moves through the roast

**Heating and evaporation.** At first, much of the heat goes into warming the water in the bean. The early roast is therefore mainly endothermic: it takes in a lot of energy.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Evaporation then draws more heat. A 2025 single-bean model places the start of this evaporative cooling at 100 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Inside the bean.** Water turns to vapour inside the bean, not only at its surface. One 2017 model assumes evaporation inside the bean and predicts a drying front moving towards the centre.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The first stage of the roast, up to the visible colour change, is covered under drying phase.

**Water keeps leaving.** In the 2005 study, the green coffee's own water left first.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Reaction water and carbon dioxide rose clearly only once the bean core reached 180–200 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the slow roast, the original water had almost stopped leaving after about 300 s of 720 s; reaction water then dominated.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Pressure and first crack.** Vapour cannot escape the dense bean quickly, so pressure builds inside it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Together with carbon dioxide, this pressure makes the bean expand<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>; an SCA handbook adds that it starts the bean cracking.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2020 sensory paper describes first crack as accumulated steam pressure breaking the beans and expelling steam.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## What water does in the bean

**It carries the reactions.** According to a roasting textbook chapter, the speed of key flavour reactions depends on how much water is available.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Some reactions slow down once the moisture falls below a critical value.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A chemistry chapter in the same book describes the roasting bean as a low-moisture reaction system.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**It softens the bean.** The cell walls can be rigid ("glassy") or soft ("rubbery"), depending on moisture and temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the textbook chapter, the bean swells while rubbery, so the speed of drying helps decide how far it expands.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**It sets the profile's options.** Two roasts can reach the same colour in the same time by different paths.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the chapter, one path may evaporate more water early than the other. This creates different combinations of bean temperature and water activity, which change the reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Green coffee moisture and the roast

Wetter green coffee loses more water in the first phase of the roast. According to the textbook chapter, it ends at a similar final moisture, so its roast loss is higher.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Roast loss then varies between batches roasted to the same colour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The effect on flavour is described as strongest in light roasts. In dark roasts, most aroma differences from initial moisture level off.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The authors of a 2025 roasting model recommend a low initial moisture to make the roast easier to control.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Water can also be added deliberately. According to the chemistry chapter, steam-treated green coffee reaches its target colour faster than untreated coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In a 2019 study, robusta roasted in superheated steam heated up, lost moisture and darkened faster than in hot air.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Water left at the end

Roasted coffee is dry. A 2020 multi-study paper gives 1–2.5% moisture for roasted beans (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Darker roasts hold less. In a 2021 study of one Ethiopian arabica, moisture fell from 2.56 g/100 g at light-medium to 0.89 g/100 g at very dark roast (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Roast speed matters too. According to the textbook chapter, fast roasts end with slightly more water than slow roasts to the same colour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A doctoral thesis from a Swiss laboratory reported the same direction.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Physics: heat, vapour and pressure

**Energy.** Heating water and turning it into vapour is the main energy demand of the early roast.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> One single-bean model uses a latent heat of vaporisation of 2.3 × 10⁶ J/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In a 2025 lumped model, evaporation affected the modelled bean temperature more than the heat released by reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 2002 study describes the bean temperature rising again towards 170 °C once the beans reach about 6% moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Where the roast turns exothermic is covered under endothermic and exothermic phases.

**Where water evaporates.** The 2017 model assumes that liquid water evaporates only inside the bean, at the interface between liquid and gas.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The authors judge this local evaporation more realistic than earlier approaches, but name the lack of physical data as the model's main drawback.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The model tracks carbon dioxide but has no term for water formed by reactions (our reading of its equations).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Only the gas measurements of the 2005 study separate the two kinds of water.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Mass transfer.** According to the roasting textbook chapter, a higher air-to-bean ratio raises air speed at the surface and speeds up dehydration.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The chapter adds that water takes time to redistribute within the bean, which may limit dehydration in short roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The chapter gives this next to the slightly higher final moisture of fast roasts, which a doctoral thesis also reported.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Online evidence.** A 2002 study followed the roaster headspace by mass spectrometry, roasting 40 g of Colombian arabica isothermally at 180–190 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The water signal rose first, reflecting the drying of the beans, and then fell.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the 2005 fluid-bed study, the peak evaporation rate was higher in the fast roast, while more water evaporated in total in the slow one.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Pressure.** According to an SCA handbook, vapour builds internal pressure in the dense green coffee and is only released once the temperature is high enough.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to the textbook chapter, the gas together with the water vapour drives the expansion of the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured the vapour pressure inside a roasting bean.

## Chemistry: water made and water used

**Where reaction water comes from.** The 2005 study names Strecker degradation, degradation of chlorogenic acid and sucrose, decarboxylation of amino acids and the Maillard reaction as the sources of carbon dioxide and reaction water.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the chemistry chapter, roasting first drives off free water and then enters a dry heating stage with dehydration, fragmentation and pyrolysis reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**How it was measured.** The study roasted beans pre-dried to 1.1 g/100 g (wb) next to normal beans at 8.3 g/100 g.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Water from the pre-dried beans was taken as reaction water; the difference gave the green coffee's own water.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Late in the roast, reaction water evaporation declined. The authors suggest the substrate ran out, or the water was used up in other reactions inside the cells.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Water activity and reactions.** Temperature, water activity and pressure govern the kinetics of the flavour reactions inside the intact bean, according to the textbook chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A different early profile can therefore change the flavour at the same colour and time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Initial moisture is described as mattering mainly in light roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Steam before or during roasting.** According to the chemistry chapter, steam treatment of green robusta changes sugars and amino acids, reduces some undesired compounds and shortens the time to the target colour.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In the 2019 superheated steam study, steam-roasted robusta had a lower pH and more sucrose, glucose and arabinose than hot-air roasted beans.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A doctoral thesis built steam injection into its laboratory roaster for humid-atmosphere roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Its results on humid air were not in the part of the thesis consulted for this entry.

## Glass transition and moisture

Water lowers the glass transition temperature of coffee. In calorimetry on samples held at set water activities, it fell from 48.76 °C to 34.89 °C in green coffee and from 45.73 °C to 40.15 °C in roasted coffee as water activity rose from 0.155 to 0.512.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Both green and roasted beans are mainly glassy.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

During roasting, the evidence is descriptive. The textbook chapter describes a passage from glassy to rubbery and back, governed by moisture and temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> An SCA handbook places the transition around first crack, with cells inflated by carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> No measurement inside a roasting bean was found (see Contested).

## Measuring water before and after

**Green coffee.** ISO 6673 determines loss in mass at 105 °C. By convention it serves as a water content method, giving results about 1.0% lower than the ISO 1446 reference method.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> These details are from the 2003 edition. ISO has since withdrawn it and published a third edition in 2025, whose full text was not consulted for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Roasted coffee.** ISO 11294 is a routine method for roasted ground coffee, by loss in mass at 103 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> ISO describes it as most suited to degassed coffee, because carbon dioxide and other volatiles also leave on heating.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Research methods vary: the 2005 study dried finely ground roasted coffee at 103 °C for 5 h, and the 2021 study oven-dried at 105 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**During the roast.** The 2005 study measured water vapour in the exhaust air with an infrared gas analyser.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors expect the method could be adapted to larger roasters.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Varying green moisture.** It makes the roast harder to control and roast loss a weaker guide to roast degree.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Steep gradients in very fast roasts.** According to the textbook chapter, the surface may over-roast while the core stays under-roasted.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Over-quenching.** Excess quench water is absorbed and raises the final moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Misleading readings.** Oven methods on fresh roasted coffee also count escaping volatiles; ISO 11294 is best suited to degassed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Moisture inside the bean.** Models place evaporation inside the bean, but their authors lack physical data to fit them.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Reaction water in production roasters.** The only split into own and reaction water consulted for this entry comes from a 100 g laboratory fluid bed.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Initial moisture and flavour.** The effect is described in a textbook chapter<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>; the underlying study was not consulted for this entry.
- **Humid-air roasting.** Superheated steam was tested on robusta at laboratory scale<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>; no sensory study of humid-air roasting was found.
- **Vapour pressure at first crack.** It is described but was not measured in any source consulted.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Geiger et al., 2005 – Water and carbon dioxide in the exhaust.</strong> Measures water vapour and carbon dioxide leaving a laboratory fluid bed during a fast and a slow roast to the same colour, and roasts pre-dried beans to isolate reaction water. More water evaporated than the beans brought in; reaction water made up 36–41% of it and appeared from a bean core temperature of 180–200 °C.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 70(2), E124–E130 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb07084.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb07084.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Yeretzian et al., 2002 – Online monitoring of roasting.</strong> Follows volatiles and water in the headspace of a small isothermal roaster by proton-transfer-reaction mass spectrometry. The water signal rose first as the beans dried and then fell, while other compounds appeared later.</p>
<p class="meta">European Food Research and Technology 214, 92–104 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00217-001-0424-7">doi:10.1007/s00217-001-0424-7</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Kim et al., 2021 – An Ethiopian arabica at four roast degrees.</strong> Roasts one coffee from light-medium to very dark and measures moisture, colour, weight loss and extract chemistry. Moisture fell with each step of roast degree, from 8.64 g/100 g green to 0.89 g/100 g at very dark.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Science and Biotechnology 30(2), 235–244 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s10068-020-00865-w">doi:10.1007/s10068-020-00865-w</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Chindapan et al., 2019 – Superheated steam versus hot air.</strong> Roasts robusta at 190–250 °C in superheated steam and in hot air. Steam speeded up heating, moisture loss and darkening, and gave beans with a lower pH and more of some sugars.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 84(2), 292–302 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/1750-3841.14422">doi:10.1111/1750-3841.14422</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bustos-Vanegas et al., 2025 – Evaporation and heat generation.</strong> Models the temperature of single beans in a drum roaster, with and without evaporation and reaction heat. Evaporation had the greater effect on the modelled temperature, and the authors recommend a low initial moisture for easier control.</p>
