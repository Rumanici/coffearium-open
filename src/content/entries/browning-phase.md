## Where the browning phase sits

The browning phase is step 3 of a roast. It begins when the beans visibly change colour and ends at first crack, the first popping sound.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Before it comes the drying phase, from charge to colour change; after it comes development, from first crack to the end of the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Studies name the phases by what the beans show, not by a probe temperature. A 2024 drum-roaster study calls this phase "colour change to first crack".<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Colour change is the moment the roaster sees that the bean has clearly changed from its original colour.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> First crack is marked by sight and sound.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Probe readings at these moments depend on the machine (see Roast profiles).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

Previous step: drying phase. Next step: first crack.

## Why it is called the Maillard phase

In roasting practice, the span from yellowing to first crack is called the "Maillard phase".<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Many roasters link the browning they see with Maillard reactions, the reactions between sugars and amino acids.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The name is a simplification, for three reasons:

- **Browning starts earlier.** Browning through Maillard and Strecker reactions is described as starting above 100 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Browning goes on later.** Maillard reactions are known to continue after first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> After first crack, browning moves faster, from the surface towards the centre.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Other reactions run alongside.** Caramelisation, pyrolysis (breakdown by heat) and the breakdown of chlorogenic acids also take place during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

So the name marks a stretch of the roast, not the limits of one reaction. The chemistry itself is explained in the entry on the Maillard reaction.

## What changes in colour

According to a roasting textbook chapter, the bean turns from greenish grey-blue to yellow, orange and brown during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Much of that darkening happens in this phase.

A 2025 study measured the colour of ground samples from 39 roasts on a 5 kg drum roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It used L*, a lightness scale where higher numbers mean lighter. At colour change, L* averaged 62.38; at first crack, 29.74.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The values at these milestones were about the same for every profile and every coffee tested.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Of the total fall in lightness from colour change to the end of the roast, about 72% came before first crack (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The authors attribute the colour change to melanoidins, brown compounds formed by browning reactions such as the Maillard reaction.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The same chapter describes colour as closely tied to flavour and as the best indicator of roast degree.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## What changes inside the bean

**Sugar.** Sucrose is the main free sugar in green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In a 2000 study, added sucrose reacted more slowly at first than added glucose or fructose. The authors attribute this to the bond that must split to give the more reactive sugars.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In a 2019 study, sucrose fell from 70.1 to 14.3 mg/g (dry weight) in the first 9 min of a 14 min roast.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Acids.** Chlorogenic acids, a large group of acids in green coffee, break down. In the same study, the measured chlorogenic acid fell from 22.7 to 12.2 mg/g by minute 9.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> At the same time, sugars form new acids, such as acetic and formic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In a 5 kg drum, titratable acidity (the total acid that can be neutralised) rose through the roast and peaked at first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Aroma.** In exhaust measurements of one drum roast, hardly any typical coffee aroma compounds appeared before 500 s; first crack came at about 730 s.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> From 500 s on, most traces rose strongly until the end of the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Gas and swelling.** Around 150 °C, the bean releases water vapour, carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide, and its volume increases.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> According to a roasting textbook chapter, gas and water vapour together drive the expansion.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The pressure eventually cracks the bean at first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## How roasters steer this phase

Roasters change the length of this phase with the heat they apply. A specialty roast typically starts near maximum burner output, then steps the power down to slow the browning reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Some roasters design profiles to spend more time in the Maillard phase. A practitioner course calls the benefit doubtful, because Maillard reactions are not limited to it.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> No peer-reviewed sensory study consulted for this entry changed this phase on its own and tasted the result. What studies did find is in the Deep dive.

## Timing of the chemistry

The sources agree on the order of events more than on temperatures. According to a roasting chemistry chapter, sucrose and free amino acids react first, because they are immediately available and highly reactive; arabinose from cell-wall polysaccharides and chlorogenic acids are degraded later, because they first need to be released.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A 2000 study found glucose and fructose in roasted robusta beans enriched with sucrose, and read reducing-sugar formation as an early step in sucrose breakdown.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Sucrose.** The 2019 time course shows the loss is not even. In the medium roast, sucrose fell slowly to 50.1 mg/g at 6 min, then fastest between 6 and 9 min, to 14.3 mg/g; by 14 min it was 3.0 mg/g.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In both roasts studied, 96–98% of the sucrose was degraded by the end.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> First crack was not recorded. The authors suggest that two sharp maxima of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural could correspond to first and second crack<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>; in the medium roast these lie at 9 and 13 min in their table.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> If that reading holds, about four-fifths of the sucrose was gone by first crack (our calculation).

**Chlorogenic acids.** They fell more steadily: in the medium roast by 45% in the middle of the roast and by 67% at the end.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to the roasting chemistry chapter, their breakdown gives quinic acid, phenolic acids, lactones and volatile phenols such as guaiacol (see Acids in coffee).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**New acids.** Sucrose is the principal green-coffee precursor of formic, acetic, glycolic and lactic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In the 2019 study, acetic acid tripled from the green value by minute 8 and formic and lactic acid rose from below 0.005 mg/g.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a 5 kg drum, titratable acidity peaked at first crack in every profile and coffee, and the peak was insensitive to how quickly the roast got there.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the extended Maillard profile, acidity rose relatively slowly during this phase.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Aroma.** In real-time exhaust data of a 15.6 kg drum roast, volatile intensities were low until 500 s. One trace appeared before 400 s and another just before 500 s; both rose until about 730 s, when first crack began, and then either peaked or levelled off.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to the textbook chapter on roasting, summarising a 2002 study, most aroma compounds formed fastest at medium roast stages, at bean water contents of 7% to 2% (wet basis).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to the roasting chemistry chapter, diketones and furfurals form early, alongside the use of free sugars and amino acids.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Maillard, caramelisation and pyrolysis together.** The roasting chapter lists the Maillard reaction, Strecker degradation, pyrolysis and caramelisation among the reactions of carbohydrates during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Where caramelisation begins in the bean is disputed (see Maillard reaction). A 2020 sensory paper describes the heat late in the roast as taking the beans into a state of pyrolysis.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> None of the sources consulted assigns a fixed share of the browning to one reaction in this phase.

## Water, gas and pressure

**Water.** The phase starts with the beans still drying. One heat-transfer model predicts a sharp drying front that divides the bean into a dry outer region and a moist core.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The same paper describes a ridge of high-pressure water vapour near the drying front.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Carbon dioxide.** According to the roasting chapter, gas formation in isothermal roasting is low at first and accelerates strongly in the second half of the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In a laboratory fluid bed with a thermocouple in the bean core, carbon dioxide and water from reactions rose significantly only once the core reached 180–200 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> On that evidence, most reaction gas forms towards the end of this phase and after it (our reading). Strecker degradation, decarboxylation of amino acids, the Maillard reaction and the breakdown of chlorogenic acids and sucrose are named as sources.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Pressure and expansion.** High gas pressure makes the cells expand.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> According to the roasting chapter, the thick cell walls stretch rather than break, and they expand while in a soft, rubbery state; how far the bean expands therefore depends on the profile.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Porosity rises gradually as the bean swells.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Estimates of the internal pressure differ, and no peer-reviewed measurement was found (see the roasting overview).

**Endothermic to exothermic.** Early in the roast, much energy goes into evaporating water; later the process becomes progressively more exothermic.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Where in the roast this happens is contested. In the exhaust study, the authors placed the transition at 625 s, before first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A textbook chapter ties it to first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Rate of rise in this phase

The rate of rise (RoR) is how fast the bean-probe reading climbs, here in °C per 30 s (see Roast profiles).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the 2024 drum study, the mean RoR in this phase was lower than before colour change in six of seven profiles; only in the slow-start profile was it higher (5.79 against 5.07 °C per 30 s).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Length and RoR went together. The extended Maillard profile moved quickly through drying and then spent 7.5 min in this phase at a mean RoR of 2.80 °C per 30 s.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The negative-rate-of-rise profile crossed it in 2.5 min at 7.72 °C per 30 s.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2025 colour study by the same group, of 39 roasts of the same three coffees, found about the same colour at first crack in all roasts<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>; so the profile changed how fast the beans got there, not where they arrived.

## What controlling this phase changes in the cup

**What studies found.** A 2020 analysis of seven sensory studies at constant colour split the roast into time to first crack and development time.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Time to first crack, which includes drying and this phase together, significantly affected five of nine attributes; development time affected all nine, mostly at higher levels of significance.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Longer time to first crack went with less acidity and more bitterness, with no significant effect on sweetness.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Body was not affected by either phase, against a common belief that links the Maillard reaction with texture.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In a separate correlation analysis of one study, time to first crack showed no significant correlation with any attribute.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**What studies did not test.** No study consulted for this entry separated the browning phase from drying in a sensory test. The 2024 study that roasted an extended Maillard profile measured titratable acidity, not flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Roast colour was a stronger predictor of flavour than roasting time.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**What roasters say.** According to a practitioner course, some roasters design their profiles to lengthen this phase.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The same course doubts the benefit, because Maillard reactions are not limited to this phase.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The 2024 study notes, citing a roasting reference, that an extended Maillard profile is thought to reduce sweetness and give flat or baked flavours.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> These are practitioner views, not findings.

## Risks and defects

- **Stretching the phase.** The extended Maillard profile of the 2024 study was designed to replicate a "baked" roast.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its sensory effect was not measured there; baked and underdeveloped roasts are covered under roast defects.
- **Reading the probe as the bean.** The probe reads a mix of bean surface and hot air, specific to the machine, so phase temperatures cannot simply be compared between roasters.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Colour change and first crack are judged on the beans themselves, by sight and by the first popping sounds.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Judging colour change by eye.** The start of the phase is the moment the beans visibly change colour<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>, so its measured length depends on who calls it.

## Open questions

- **Sensory effect of this phase alone.** No sensory study was found that varied colour change to first crack while holding drying, development and colour constant.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Exothermic onset.** Where in this phase the roast becomes exothermic is not agreed (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Time courses on production roasters.** The sugar and acid time course here comes from a 25 g home roaster without recorded first crack<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>; matched chemistry and crack times from production drums were not found.
- **Range of coffees.** The colour and acidity data come from three arabica coffees on one drum roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Anokye-Bempah et al., 2024 – Profiles and titratable acidity.</strong> Seven profiles on a 5 kg drum, with beans sampled every minute of 16 min roasts of three coffees. The paper reports the duration and mean rate of rise of the colour-change-to-first-crack phase for each profile; titratable acidity rose through it and always peaked at first crack.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 14, 8237 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-57256-y">doi:10.1038/s41598-024-57256-y</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Anokye-Bempah et al., 2025 – A universal colour curve.</strong> Measures the colour of 663 ground samples from 39 roasts of three arabica coffees. All samples followed one curve in CIELAB space, and colour at colour change, first crack and second crack was about the same whatever the profile or origin.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 15, 24192 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-06601-w">doi:10.1038/s41598-025-06601-w</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Diviš et al., 2019 – Composition minute by minute.</strong> Follows sucrose, chlorogenic acid, organic acids and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural in a Brazilian arabica during a medium and a full city roast in a home roaster. Sucrose fell fastest in the middle of the roast and was almost gone at the end, while acetic, formic and lactic acids rose.</p>
<p class="meta">Potravinarstvo Slovak Journal of Food Sciences 13(1), 344–350 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.5219/1062">doi:10.5219/1062</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Wieland et al., 2012 – Watching the roast in real time.</strong> Follows volatile compounds in the exhaust of a 15–20 kg drum roaster by PTR-ToF-MS. Few aroma compounds appear before 500 s of a 894 s roast; many traces peak or level off at first crack.</p>
<p class="meta">Analytical and Bioanalytical Chemistry 402(8), 2531–2543 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00216-011-5401-9">doi:10.1007/s00216-011-5401-9</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Münchow et al., 2020 – Colour versus timing.</strong> Pools eight sensory studies of washed arabica. At constant colour, development time affected flavour more than time to first crack, the span that contains drying and browning together; body was unaffected by either.</p>
<p class="meta">Beverages 6(2), 29 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/beverages6020029">doi:10.3390/beverages6020029</a></p>
</div>
