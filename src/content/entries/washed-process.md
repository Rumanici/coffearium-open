## How it is done

1. **Selection** – the process needs ripe cherries only, either picked selectively or separated mechanically during processing,<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> for example in flotation tanks.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
2. **Pulping** – a pulping machine removes the skin and pulp in the presence of water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The seeds are left covered in slimy mucilage, which pulping does not remove.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Kenya, the pulped coffee is then graded by density and size into grades 1 and 2 and "lights".<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
3. **Mucilage removal** – the coffee is usually fermented in water at ambient temperature, so that microorganisms break the mucilage down.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Some producers use enzymes instead.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
4. **Washing** – the loosened mucilage is washed off. The result is called parchment or washed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Some mills add a soak in clean water before the final wash (see below).<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
5. **Drying** – the parchment coffee is dried in thin layers of 3–5 cm and turned regularly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It is considered safe from fungal damage below 12.5% moisture (wet basis) and a water activity of 0.67–0.70.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Soaking and grading

**Soaking.** In some processing chains the washed parchment is held under clean water once more, from overnight up to, rarely, 48 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> This is sometimes called secondary fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In Kenya, about 12 hours of soaking after fermentation was found to improve colour and taste; the parchment is then washed again.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Soaking is used to improve the look of the green bean and give a cleaner cup.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In a trial in Yunnan, a 24-hour soak evened out the green coffee: it tempered the gains of longer fermentation, whatever had been done before.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Short soaks do not seem to cause defects unless the water is tainted.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Grading by density.** Density sorting starts before pulping, when floating cherries are separated in water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In Kenya, the pulped coffee is graded again by density and size into grades 1 and 2 and "lights".<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The final wash there is done in concrete channels, where the parchment is pushed with wooden paddles against a stream of water.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In the Yunnan trial, washing was done by passing the beans through a curved water channel.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Effect on the cup

Whether coffee is processed wet or dry creates typical differences in quality and flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The effect is not the same for every coffee: a 2024 analysis of Coffee Quality Institute cupping records found that the effect of processing on flavour scores depended on the variety.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Within the washed process, fermentation time matters too: in a controlled trial, a longer fermentation gave a fruitier and more acidic cup.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Fermentation: microbiology and chemistry

Wet fermentation is dominated by lactic acid bacteria. In a study in Ecuador, *Leuconostoc*, *Lactococcus* and *Lactobacillus* were the most common group, together with enterobacteria and the yeasts *Pichia* and *Starmerella*.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A 2022 review names *Leuconostoc pseudomesenteroides*, *Lactobacillus vaccinostercus*, *Lactobacillus brevis* and *Lactobacillus plantarum* among the lactic acid bacteria that increase during fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

Their activity acidifies the fermentation water: in a trial in Yunnan, China, pH fell from about 6.0–6.5 to about 4.0 in every variant, with the largest drop after 36 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Lactic acid, acetic acid and mannitol formed in the mucilage were later found inside the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## The seed's own metabolism

Processing does not only act on the outside of the seed. The seed is alive, and processing switches on germination processes inside it. In washed coffee, germination activity peaked about two days after processing began – much earlier than in natural coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Why washed coffee has less sugar

Washed green coffee holds significantly less glucose and fructose than natural coffee processed in parallel, in a study of Arabica var. Acaiá.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Two explanations are offered.

**Seed metabolism.** In washed coffee the two sugars fall during processing, while in natural coffee they stay the same or even rise.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> When Arabica that had been mechanically demucilaged and dry-fermented without water was dried under controlled conditions – with no process water present – glucose and fructose fell significantly within the first day.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The authors concluded that the difference is, at least in part, due to metabolic processes and not to sugars leaching into the process water.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A field study in Ecuador agrees for the tank stage: fructose, glucose and sucrose in the endosperm fell significantly during fermentation, and the authors put this down to the lack of oxygen under water, which triggered germination and an anaerobic sugar-consumption response in the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Loss into the water.** The same Ecuadorian study found that fructose and glucose fell further during a 24-hour soak, and proposed that osmotic pressure helped monosaccharides and fermentation products leave the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In Yunnan, fructose was among the most abundant compounds in the soaking water at the end of soaking, and glucose and fructose in the beans dropped after washing.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The two mechanisms are not exclusive. The evidence points to metabolism during fermentation and drying, with leaching adding to the loss where a soak is used.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> No study in this entry has measured how much of the final difference each one explains.

## Mass balance

Processing removes most of the cherry's weight. Published conversion factors are rounded rules of thumb.

| Parameter | Value reported | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh cherry to green coffee (washed) | About 5:1 often stated; usually about 6:1 in Ethiopia | Schuit et al.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> |
| Fresh cherry to green coffee | 5–6 kg cherry per kg green | European Coffee Federation<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> |
| Mucilage | About 13% of the cherry's weight; about 22% of pulped coffee | Cenicafé<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> |
| Parchment husk | About 20% of the weight of dry parchment coffee (1.25:1 parchment to green) | Schuit et al.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> |

The sources consulted for this entry give no primary figure for the share of skin and pulp on a fresh-weight basis, so it is left out.

## Water and wastewater

Conventional washing can use about 40 litres of water per kilogram of dry parchment coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The ecological processing developed by Cenicafé in Colombia pulps without water and removes mucilage mechanically; with its DESLIM demucilager, water use fell to under 1 litre per kilogram.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
