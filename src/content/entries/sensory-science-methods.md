## What sensory science measures

Sensory evaluation uses people as the measuring instrument. According to a 2017 textbook chapter on coffee, its aim is to understand products in order to optimise them and to drive consumer choice.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It borrows from physiology, psychology and statistics.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The method follows from the question. The same chapter names four kinds of question:<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Are two coffees different?** Difference tests, also called discrimination tests.
- **What does a coffee taste like, and how strongly?** Descriptive tests.
- **Does a coffee match a standard?** Grading and quality-control tests.
- **How much do people like it?** Affective, or hedonic, tests.

Each test is a small experiment. The tester decides what to change, what to measure and which conditions can be controlled. The most accurate tests include a control as a baseline.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Standardised tests are usually preferred because they come with set statistical procedures.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How the senses themselves work is covered in *How we taste coffee*.

## Difference tests

**The triangle test.** Each assessor receives three coded samples. Two are the same and one is different, and the assessor must name the odd one.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It is a forced-choice procedure: an answer is required even when the assessor is unsure.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) describes it in ISO 4120. The test works whether the difference lies in one attribute or in several.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Why so many assessors?** Someone who only guesses still picks the odd cup one time in three.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> One correct answer therefore proves little. The test is repeated with many assessors, and a published table gives the number of correct answers needed.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> With 12 assessors, 8 must be right for a difference at the 5% level; with 24 assessors, 13.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**The duo-trio test.** Here the assessor first tastes a known reference, then two coded samples, and picks the one that matches the reference.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> ISO 10399 calls it statistically less efficient than the triangle test, but easier for assessors.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A guess is right one time in two, so more correct answers are needed for the same confidence (our own reasoning from the procedure; see the deep dive).

Difference tests have a cost. Tasting many sets of samples tires the palate, and tired assessors judge less well.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> They are still worth running first: describing two coffees in detail is pointless if a panel cannot tell them apart.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Describing a coffee with a trained panel

Descriptive analysis answers what a coffee tastes like and how strongly. It uses a panel trained with attribute references.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the textbook chapter, quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) is probably the most commonly used form.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The panel first agrees on its words, either from an existing vocabulary such as the World Coffee Research Sensory Lexicon or from its own sessions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It then rates the intensity of each attribute in every sample.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> ISO 13299 gives general guidance for building such a sensory profile.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

In one coffee study, 12 volunteers were trained in five sessions over 2 weeks. They rated 26 attributes on a 15 cm line scale.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Training of this kind takes time, so full descriptive analysis is kept for research, product standards and products where precision matters.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> ISO 8586 sets out how sensory assessors are selected and trained.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

A quicker method is check-all-that-apply (CATA). Tasters tick every word on a list that fits the sample, without rating intensity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> It needs much less training than a descriptive panel, but many more participants.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The words used to describe coffee are covered in *Flavour language*.

## Measuring liking

Affective tests ask how much people like a coffee. They are usually run with consumers who might buy the product.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Hedonic scale.** Consumers rate each sample on a 9-point scale from “dislike extremely” to “like extremely”.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Preference and ranking.** Consumers choose the preferred sample of two or three, or rank several samples from most to least preferred.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Just-about-right (JAR) scales.** Consumers say whether a property, such as strength, is too weak, too strong or just about right.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Liking and difference are separate questions. In a 2025 study, 42 of 60 coffee-interested participants picked the odd sample in a triangle test of freshly roasted against matured coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Of those 42, however, 22 preferred the matured coffee and 20 the fresh one – no significant preference.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Fair testing: codes, order and rinsing

According to the SCA, tasters can be easily biased if they are given extrinsic information about a coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> To limit bias in tasting, samples are labelled with three-digit codes, served in a random or balanced order, repeated, and judged by each assessor alone.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Blind coding.** A code hides the sample’s identity. Three-digit codes are the textbook rule and appear in coffee studies.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **Order.** Serving order can itself bias judgements, so orders are randomised or balanced across assessors.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Carry-over.** A lingering taste from one sample can change the next. Designs balanced for carry-over aim to control this.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>
- **Rinsing.** Water, and sometimes unsalted crackers, are served between samples.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Temperature and timing.** Coffee flavour changes as it cools, so preparation and tasting are timed.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Seeing.** One study lit its tasting booths with red light to hide colour differences between coffees.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## How it differs from cupping

Cupping is the coffee trade’s own tasting method, described in *Cupping*. It relies on industry experts who judge quality on a quality scale. A review names this as what sets it apart from descriptive analysis.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> SCA Standards 103-2024 and 104-2024 leave the selection, training and qualifications of tasters outside their scope.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Both also leave out the statistics needed to interpret their data with confidence.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

Since 2024, the SCA’s Coffee Value Assessment (CVA) borrows from sensory science. The SCA specifies that intensity is rated on 15-point scales and that descriptors are ticked on CATA lists.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> According to the SCA, 15-point scales are the usual choice in traditional descriptive methods.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Its affective part uses a 9-point scale, the format the SCA calls the most common affective measure in food science.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The uniformity box on the affective form is a small difference test.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to the SCA, a cupping group aligning its scores is not doing what sensory science calls calibration. Calibration means training a panel on set references for one specific test.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## The statistics of a triangle test

Under the assumption of “no difference”, every assessor has a one-in-three chance of naming the odd sample.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The number of correct answers among n independent assessors then follows a binomial distribution with p = 1/3. The probability of x or more correct answers by chance alone is

**P(X ≥ x) = Σ from k = x to n of C(n, k) · (1/3)^k · (2/3)^(n − k)**

The minimum in the table is the smallest x for which this probability does not exceed the chosen significance level α. If the observed count reaches the tabled value, the assumption of “no difference” is rejected.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> This is our own statement of the calculation; it reproduces the FAO values.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

</figure>

The lines rise more slowly than the number of assessors. With 12 assessors, 8 correct answers (67%) are needed at α = 0.05; with 60, 27 (45%).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A larger panel can therefore detect a smaller difference. The table also has no value at α = 0.001 for six assessors: even six correct answers out of six are too likely by chance.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Other ways to compute it.** The Society of Sensory Professionals (SSP) gives a chi-square calculation as one way to evaluate a triangle test.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A coffee study judged its triangles from Z-scores and P values.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Minimums stated in published studies can differ from the exact table; see the contested item below.

**How many discriminate?** A significant result does not say that every assessor perceived the difference. One 2025 study reported 42 correct answers out of 60 (70%) and estimated the proportion of true discriminators at 55.0% (95% confidence interval 41.3–68.7%).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The estimate corresponds to correcting 70% for guessing: (0.70 − 1/3) ÷ (2/3) = 0.55 (our arithmetic).

**The duo-trio test.** Here a guess is right half the time, because the assessor chooses between two samples, one of which matches the reference.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> By the same binomial calculation with p = 1/2 (our own), 24 assessors need 17 correct answers at α = 0.05, against 13 in a triangle test. This is the practical meaning of ISO 10399’s statement that the duo-trio test is statistically less efficient.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> ISO 10399:2017 was withdrawn in May 2026 and replaced by ISO 10399:2026 (as of October 2026).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Difference or similarity.** Both ISO 4120 and ISO 10399 cover testing for a perceptible difference and testing for similarity, for example after a change of ingredients, processing, packaging, handling or storage.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Both standards also name the selection, training and monitoring of assessors as a use.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The settings and tables for similarity testing are in the standards’ full texts, which were not read for this entry.

## Descriptive analysis in detail

Variants of quantitative descriptive analysis include QDA and the Spectrum method.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In QDA the panel first fixes a consistent vocabulary – taken from an existing lexicon or developed over several sessions – then rates each attribute’s intensity; the result is a flavour profile of the sample.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Results are often compared with principal component analysis, which places samples and attributes on one map.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> ISO 13299 lists uses of sensory profiling that include developing or changing a product, defining a product or trading standard by its sensory attributes, shelf-life studies, and relating perceived attributes to instrumental data or to consumer acceptability.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Panel selection and training.** ISO 8586:2023 specifies criteria for selecting, and procedures for training, trained and expert sensory assessors. It supplements the general guidance of ISO 6658.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In a 2019 coffee study, the descriptive panel spent its first two training sessions generating attributes and the next three agreeing terms and references.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Panellists then rated eight coffees in triplicate over four sessions.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> According to the SCA, a trained panel in sensory science is calibrated to use a set of sensory references accurately in a specific test environment and protocol.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Rapid methods.** Several faster alternatives to classic descriptive analysis exist; they can be carried out by experts, trained assessors or untrained consumers.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In CATA, products are presented one at a time in a Latin square or randomised design. The counts of how often each word was ticked for each product form a frequency table, analysed by correspondence analysis.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its main drawback is that the list must be fixed in advance.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Coffee studies have tested CATA results with Cochran’s Q test.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Other rapid methods include sorting tasks, which have been applied to milk coffee drinks, and flash profiling.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Affective tests in detail

The 9-point hedonic scale was the liking scale in a 2019 study of 85 consumers, alongside 5-point JAR questions and a CATA question.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> JAR data are commonly read with penalty analysis. It shows how much liking, or here rated quality, drops when a property is judged too low or too high.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In a 2026 study of 56 coffee experts and 12 coffees, roast and colour judged too dark were the most damaging to quality.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

The CVA uses the 9-point format, but its labels speak of impression of quality rather than liking.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to the SCA, its own research showed that cuppers could act as reliable descriptive panellists, but also suggested that personal preferences divide them into distinct clusters.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> On that account, preference can differ between tasters, even among professionals.

## Designs against order and carry-over effects

Each sample can change the perception of the next one, for example through a lingering taste. Sensory experiments therefore often use designs balanced for carry-over effects.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> A 2004 statistical report found that designs only nearly balanced for carry-over still gave conservative variance estimates, even with large carry-over effects.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

In practice coffee studies combine several tools. One descriptive study used a randomised Williams Latin square block design to control carry-over, and randomised the order of its triangle sets by session.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> A 2026 expert study printed a randomised serving order on each ballot.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In a 2025 triangle test, assessors who tasted together were excluded from the analysis.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Exact versus approximate tables.** Coffee studies compute triangle-test significance in different ways, by tables, Z-scores or their own calculation.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Their stated minimums can differ from the exact binomial table.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Cuppers as panellists.** The SCA reports that cuppers can act as consistent descriptive panellists, citing its own research.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> How cupping descriptors compare with a trained panel’s is discussed in *Cupping*.
- **Similarity testing in coffee.** No coffee study consulted for this entry used the similarity mode of ISO 4120 or ISO 10399, for example to show that a cheaper process gives the same cup.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Zimmermann et al., 2025 – Fresh versus matured roasted coffee.</strong> Sixty coffee-interested participants tasted freshly roasted and matured coffee in a triangle test following ISO 4120, then chose the one they preferred. Most (42 of 60) picked the odd sample, but preference split almost evenly – a clear example of difference and liking as separate questions.</p>
<p class="meta">European Food Research and Technology 251(11), 3839–3845 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00217-025-04873-0">doi:10.1007/s00217-025-04873-0</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Frost et al., 2019 – Basket geometry.</strong> Combined three methods in one study: triangle tests with 45 assessors, a trained descriptive panel of 12 with 26 attributes, and 85 consumers rating liking on a 9-point hedonic scale. The paper documents panel training, a Williams Latin square to control carry-over, and red light to hide colour differences.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/1750-3841.14696">doi:10.1111/1750-3841.14696</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Batali et al., 2020 – Brew temperature.</strong> Drip-brewed coffees were matched for strength and extraction yield at 87, 90 and 93 °C and profiled. Strength and extraction changed the sensory profile; brew temperature on its own had no appreciable effect within that range.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 10 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-73341-4">doi:10.1038/s41598-020-73341-4</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Guinard et al., 2026 – Rating plus JAR and CATA.</strong> Fifty-six coffee experts assessed 12 coffees with an overall quality rating, five 5-point JAR scales and a CATA list, served blind under three-digit codes in randomised order. Penalty analysis showed which deviations from “just about right” cost the most quality.</p>
