## What kind of study is behind the headline

Health headlines about coffee rest on studies of very different strength. The GRADE system, used to rate evidence in health research, starts randomised trials as high-quality evidence and observational studies as low-quality evidence.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a randomised trial, researchers assign coffee or no coffee at random. In an observational study, people choose for themselves, and researchers record what happens.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

The figure arranges the main study types in a rough order. It is a guide, not a rule: the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine notes that hierarchies of evidence have been used inflexibly and criticised for decades.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A well-run study lower down can be more informative than a poor one higher up.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Coffee research sits mostly on the lower and middle steps. A 2017 umbrella review of coffee and health covered 201 meta-analyses of observational research and only 17 of interventional studies.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Its authors concluded that most of the evidence “provides only low or very low quality evidence” and that robust randomised trials are needed to tell whether the associations are causal.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> What that review found, outcome by outcome, is covered in *Coffee and long-term health*.

## Relative and absolute risk

Headlines usually report relative risk (RR): how much higher or lower a risk is in one group than in another. An RR of 0.82 means 18% lower. It does not say how common the disease is to begin with. Absolute risk does: how many people out of 1,000 are affected.

One figure from the 2017 umbrella review and one hypothetical headline show why the difference matters. High versus low coffee intake was associated with an 18% lower risk of any cancer (RR 0.82).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> For a rare outcome, take a hypothetical headline reporting a 50% lower risk of liver cancer (RR 0.50; an illustration, not a result quoted from the review). In the United States, about 39.2% of people are diagnosed with some cancer during their lifetime<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>, and about 1.1% with liver cancer (data 2021–2023).<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Our calculation applies each RR to its baseline. For all cancers: 392 per 1,000 × 0.82 ≈ 321 per 1,000, about 71 fewer. For the hypothetical liver-cancer headline: 11 per 1,000 × 0.50 ≈ 5.5 per 1,000, about 5.5 fewer.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The larger relative change gives the smaller absolute change, because liver cancer is rare.

This is an exercise in arithmetic, not a prediction. The relative risks come from observational studies, which cannot show that coffee caused the difference.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The baselines are for a whole population, drinkers included, and in another country.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The point is the method: always look for the starting risk before judging a percentage.

## What counts as a cup

Most coffee studies measure intake in cups a day. A 2014 study of espressos bought in Scotland, Italy and Spain concluded that “one cup of coffee” is not a reproducible measurement.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Outlet averages in Scotland ranged from 72 to 212 mg of caffeine per espresso, and serving volumes varied widely.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> An earlier survey of 20 cafés in Glasgow found 51–322 mg per espresso, in cups of 23–70 mL.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

The 2017 umbrella review notes that there is no universally recognised standard cup size.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> When a headline says “three cups a day”, it helps to know what kind of cup the study counted. How much caffeine a serving holds is covered in *Caffeine*.

## Confounding, reverse causation and missing studies

**Confounding.** A confounder is a third factor linked both to coffee and to the disease. For coffee, smoking is a well-documented example.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In 2016 an IARC working group concluded that positive associations with bladder cancer in some early studies may have been due to other risk factors, such as tobacco smoking.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In the 2017 umbrella review, harmful associations were largely nullified by adequate adjustment for smoking, except in pregnancy.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Reverse causation.** Sometimes the disease changes the coffee habit, not the other way round. The umbrella review gives the example of symptoms leading people to drink less coffee before a diagnosis.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Low coffee intake then looks linked to disease without causing it.

**Missing studies.** Not every study gets published. A Cochrane methodology review found that trials with positive findings are published more often, and more quickly, than trials with negative findings.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> A meta-analysis can only pool what was published.

## Who paid for the study

Funding is associated with results. A Cochrane review found that drug and device studies sponsored by manufacturers more often reported favourable efficacy results (RR 1.27) and favourable conclusions (RR 1.34) than studies sponsored by other sources.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In nutrition, a 2007 analysis of 206 articles on soft drinks, juice and milk found conclusions favourable to the sponsor far more often when all funding came from industry than when none did.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

Member journals of the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) require authors to declare financial relationships on a standard form, and the committee encourages other journals to adopt it.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> Articles should also state what role the funders played in study design, data analysis and interpretation.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> Articles should be published with these disclosure statements.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> Coffearium uses information sites funded by the coffee industry only as pointers to primary papers, never as evidence.

## From paper to headline

Exaggeration often starts before the newsroom. In a 2014 study of 462 press releases from 20 UK universities, 40% contained exaggerated advice and 33% exaggerated causal claims.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> When a press release exaggerated a causal claim, 81% of the news stories based on it did too, against 18% when it did not.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The paper itself can be the source. A 2012 study of randomised trials found “spin”, a misleading presentation of results, in 40% of abstract conclusions and 47% of press releases.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Spin in the abstract was the only factor linked to spin in the press release.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

## Questions to ask of a coffee headline

Use these steps when you read a new coffee-and-health story:

1. **Find the paper.** Look for the journal name or a DOI in the article or press release; read at least the abstract.
2. **Name the study type.** Laboratory, animal, case-control, cohort, Mendelian randomisation, randomised trial or review? Only trials test cause directly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
3. **Name the people.** Who was studied, how many, in which country and for how long?
4. **Find the dose.** Cups a day or milligrams of caffeine? A “cup” varies widely.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
5. **Look for absolute numbers.** A percentage change needs a starting risk.
6. **Check the wording.** “Associated with” in the paper should not become “causes” or “prevents” in the headline.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
7. **Check confounders.** Were smoking and other habits taken into account?<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
8. **Check funding and disclosures.** Read the statements on funding and conflicts of interest.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>
9. **Compare with reviews and authorities.** One new study rarely overturns a body of evidence; see *Coffee and long-term health*.

## How evidence is graded

GRADE rates the quality of evidence, starting from the study design. Randomised trials start high and observational studies start low.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Quality may be downgraded for limitations in design or conduct, imprecise estimates (wide confidence intervals), inconsistent results, indirect evidence or publication bias.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It may be upgraded for a large effect, a dose-response gradient, or when all plausible biases would reduce the apparent effect.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In the Oxford levels, the top level for questions about treatment benefit is “either N-of-1 randomized trials or systematic reviews of randomized trials”.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> For coffee and long-term disease, few such trials exist: the umbrella review notes that coffee has been given as an intervention only for short durations.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 2023 randomised trial shows what is feasible: 100 adults wore continuous monitors while coffee and no-coffee days were assigned at random, and the outcomes were measured over days, not decades.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Mendelian randomisation** fills part of this gap. Genetic variants are fixed at conception, so they are less likely to be affected by confounding or reverse causation.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A variant is a valid instrument only if three assumptions hold: relevance, independence and the exclusion restriction; horizontal pleiotropy, where a variant acts through another pathway, violates them.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The estimates describe effects of a risk factor over a lifetime, not of a change at a specific time.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Results for coffee are discussed in *Coffee and long-term health*.

## Worked example: pancreatic cancer

Early reports linked coffee with pancreatic cancer.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In 1986 a research group published further data from its multi-year case-control study. It compared coffee intake 10 years before diagnosis in patients and controls, and concluded that its findings did not support earlier reports of a link.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Thirty years later, IARC reviewed 20 cohort studies and 22 case-control studies that controlled for smoking.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Cohort studies and population-based case-control studies showed no overall association, and a pooled analysis of cohort studies found none, including in non-smokers.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> IARC concluded that there was no evidence of an association.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It listed pancreatic cancer among cancers that coffee is unlikely to cause.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

The lesson: a first association, from a design open to bias, is a question rather than an answer. For the same review, IARC rated drinking coffee overall as not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity (Group 3). That means the data do not allow a conclusion either way; it is not a statement that coffee is safe.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Worked example: the California warning label

Cancer warnings on coffee in California came out of a court case, *CERT v. Starbucks*. In its first phase, the trial court found that the coffee companies had failed to establish a legal defence to the requirement to post warnings for acrylamide in coffee, because they had not performed a quantitative risk assessment.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> The Phase 2 statement of decision followed in May 2018.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> Acrylamide forms during roasting; see *Roasting contaminants*.

The state agency then changed the rule. On 3 June 2019, California’s Office of Administrative Law approved a regulation adopted by the Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment (OEHHA). It states that chemicals in coffee created by and inherent in roasting or brewing “do not pose a significant risk of cancer”.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> It took effect on 1 October 2019.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> OEHHA wrote that the regulation is strongly supported by the research summarised by IARC, which placed coffee in Group 3, and expressed no view on whether the court had reached the right result.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>

The ruling turned on what the companies had to prove under one state law, not on a new study of coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> News of a “cancer warning” is therefore a legal fact, not a measure of risk.

## Limits of the absolute-risk example

The worked example uses the simplest possible arithmetic. Three things make it rough:

- **Comparison groups.** “High versus low” compares the top and bottom intake groups of each meta-analysis; the categories differ between studies.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Baseline.** The SEER lifetime risks describe the whole US population, coffee drinkers included.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The risk among non-drinkers would differ.
- **Cause.** Both relative risks come from observational data and may reflect confounding or reverse causation.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

A trial or review that reports absolute numbers directly (for example, events per 1,000 people in each group) avoids these steps.

## Open questions

- **Cup size in older cohorts.** Long-running cohorts recorded cups, not milligrams.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry estimated how much this measurement variation changes the published risk estimates for coffee.
- **Trials for long-term outcomes.** Randomised trials of coffee have been short<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>; trials long enough to test cancer or mortality have not been reported in the sources consulted here.
- **Spin in nutrition news.** The press-release studies covered biomedical research and drug trials in general<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>; no study consulted for this entry measured exaggeration in coffee news specifically.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Sumner et al., 2014 – Exaggeration starts in the press release.</strong> Compared 462 health-related press releases from 20 UK universities with the papers they described and 668 resulting news stories. When press releases exaggerated advice, causal claims or animal-to-human inference, news stories were far more likely to exaggerate too, with little sign that exaggeration earned more coverage.</p>
<p class="meta">BMJ 349, g7015 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g7015">doi:10.1136/bmj.g7015</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Yavchitz et al., 2012 – Spin in trial reports and news.</strong> Followed 70 randomised trials from abstract to press release to news. Spin was common in abstract conclusions and in press releases, and spin in the abstract was the only factor linked to spin in the press release.</p>
<p class="meta">PLoS Medicine 9(9), e1001308 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1001308">doi:10.1371/journal.pmed.1001308</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Lesser et al., 2007 – Funding and nutrition conclusions.</strong> Classified 206 articles on soft drinks, juice and milk by funding source and by conclusion. Articles funded entirely by industry were much more likely to reach conclusions favourable to the sponsor than those with no industry funding.</p>
<p class="meta">PLoS Medicine 4(1), e5 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.0040005">doi:10.1371/journal.pmed.0040005</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Ludwig et al., 2014 – What is a cup?</strong> Measured caffeine and chlorogenic acids in espressos bought in Scotland, Italy and Spain. Content and volume varied widely between outlets, and the authors concluded that “one cup of coffee” is not a reproducible unit for epidemiology.</p>
<p class="meta">Food & Function 5, 1718–1726 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1039/c4fo00290c">doi:10.1039/c4fo00290c</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Poole et al., 2017 – Umbrella review of coffee and health.</strong> Gathered 201 observational and 17 interventional meta-analyses. Associations were mostly with lower risk, but the evidence was rated low or “very low” quality, and harmful associations largely disappeared with adequate adjustment for smoking.</p>
<p class="meta">BMJ 359, j5024 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j5024">doi:10.1136/bmj.j5024</a></p>
</div>
