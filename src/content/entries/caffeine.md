brief:
  - Caffeine is a methylxanthine, a purine alkaloid with three methyl groups (1,3,7-trimethylpurine-2,6-dione).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It is toxic to insects and is thought to protect the coffee plant.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
  - Robusta beans hold about twice as much caffeine as arabica beans; in brews, most studies find 1.4–1.8 times as much. Roasting hardly changes it.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
  - What ends up in a cup depends largely on the coffee-to-water ratio and the serving size. Espressos bought in 20 cafés ranged from 51 to 322 mg each.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
  - EFSA considers up to 400 mg a day (and single doses up to 200 mg) of no safety concern for healthy non-pregnant adults, and up to 200 mg a day during pregnancy.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
  - Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors. The liver breaks almost all of it down, mainly with the enzyme CYP1A2 and mainly to paraxanthine; the speed varies widely between people.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a>,<a href="#src-s20">20</a>,<a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup>

  - 'Adult half-life: EFSA gives an average of about 4 h with a range of 2–8 h<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup>; Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) gives 3–7 h.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The ranges overlap; both describe large differences between people.'
  - 'Does dark roast have less caffeine? Per gram of roasted coffee, caffeine stayed the same or rose slightly from green to dark roast in one study (12 to 13 mg/g).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> But a 2024 brewing study found that, under identical brewing conditions, dark roasts gave lower caffeine concentrations in the cup than light and medium roasts – while at identical extraction yields they gave higher ones.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A 2021 review calls the effect of roasting on caffeine in the brew inconclusive.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The answer depends on whether one compares per bean, per gram or per cup, and on how the coffee is brewed.'
  - 'How much more caffeine robusta has: Ludwig et al. state that robusta beans contain about twice as much as arabica<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>; a 2021 review of brewing studies found that robusta brews mostly contained 1.4–1.8 times as much, and notes one study in which an arabica espresso contained more.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Bean and brew comparisons are not the same measure.'
  - 'How fast caffeine leaves the grounds: a textbook chapter places caffeine among the very soluble compounds extracted in the first seconds<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>, but in a flow-controlled espresso study, caffeine concentration in successive fractions fell more slowly than that of trigonelline and total dissolved solids.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Both agree that most caffeine is extracted early.'
  - 'Genetics: FSANZ calls genetic variation in CYP1A2 a major cause of differences in its activity, and notes that variants in *ADORA2A*, the adenosine A2A receptor gene, influence caffeine’s effects on anxiety and sleep<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>; a genome-wide meta-analysis of 47,341 people linked regions near *CYP1A2* and *AHR* to habitual caffeine consumption.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> But a 2022 systematic review of 141 pharmacokinetic publications found the CYP1A2 genotype reported in only 5 of them.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> How much genotype should change personal advice is not settled in the sources consulted for this entry.'

## What caffeine is

Caffeine is a methylxanthine. Its systematic name, 1,3,7-trimethylpurine-2,6-dione, describes it: a purine ring system carrying three methyl groups.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The molecule is rich in nitrogen. Pure caffeine is a white, odourless, bitter crystalline powder, and at high temperature it sublimes – it passes from solid to vapour without melting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its close relatives paraxanthine, theobromine and theophylline are dimethylxanthines: each lacks one of the three methyl groups.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>

In the green bean, caffeine is held in a structure with chlorogenic acids. Water frees it, which is why every industrial decaffeination process starts by wetting the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Why the plant makes it

Caffeine can be toxic to insects and is thought to protect the plant against herbivores.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Yet the most damaging insect pest of coffee, the coffee berry borer, lives inside the seed. It copes with the help of gut bacteria: several *Pseudomonas* species in its gut can grow on caffeine as their only source of carbon and nitrogen, and when the gut bacteria are removed, the beetle no longer breaks caffeine down.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Caffeine also occurs in the nectar of *Coffea* flowers. In an experiment, honeybees rewarded with caffeine were three times as likely to remember a learned floral scent as bees rewarded with sugar alone.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Caffeine concentrations in nectar did not exceed the bees’ bitter-taste threshold. The authors read this as a way for the plant to keep pollinators coming back.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## How much is in the bean

*Coffea canephora* (robusta) holds about twice as much caffeine as *Coffea arabica*.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2021 review gives 0.9–1.5% of dry weight for green arabica and 1.2–2.4% for green robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In brews, the same review found that robusta mostly gave 1.4–1.8 times as much caffeine as arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Roasting barely touches caffeine. In one study of Brazilian arabica, caffeine stayed at 12–13 mg per gram from green beans to a dark roast, while caffeoylquinic acids (the main chlorogenic acids) fell from 41 to 5 mg/g.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Because roasting removes water and other matter while caffeine stays, the amount per gram of roasted coffee stays the same or rises slightly.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> See *Roasting* for what does change.

## How much ends up in the cup

The amount of caffeine in a cup is set by four things: how much ground coffee is used, the share of robusta, how much water is used, and how much of the caffeine is extracted.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2021 review of brewing studies concluded that the coffee-to-water ratio probably has the greatest influence; brew time, it found, was not a decisive factor.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Caffeine dissolves easily, so most of it leaves the grounds early in any brew.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In a laboratory test of double espressos made from 18.1–20.4 g of arabica, about 67% of the caffeine in the grounds reached the cup, giving 152–174 mg per serving whatever the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Spent grounds from a household filter brewer still held 5.6% of the original caffeine; spent grounds from professional high-pressure machines in restaurants held 21.5–30.8%.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Real cups vary far more than laboratory brews. Twenty espressos bought in Glasgow held 51–322 mg each, in cups of 23–70 mL.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In a later survey, mean espresso caffeine per outlet ranged from 72 to 212 mg in Scotland, 73–135 mg in Italy and 97–127 mg in Spain.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Cafés use different doses, from 5 to 18 g, and different cup sizes.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Espresso is the most concentrated brew per litre, but per serving the volume of the cup also counts.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Decaffeinated coffee

Decaf is not caffeine-free. The US Food and Drug Administration says decaf coffee typically has 2–15 mg in an 8-fluid-ounce cup.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In the EU, coffee extracts (instant coffee) may be labelled ‘decaffeinated’ only if anhydrous caffeine does not exceed 0.3% of the coffee-based dry matter.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> For beans, national law applies<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>; Germany, for example, allows ‘entkoffeiniert’ on green and roasted coffee with at most 1 g of caffeine per kilogram of coffee dry matter (0.1%).<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> How caffeine is removed is covered in *Decaffeination*.

## Official intake advice

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) assessed the safety of caffeine from all sources in 2015. Its conclusions:

- **Healthy adults, single dose:** up to 200 mg (about 3 mg per kilogram of body weight) raises no safety concerns, also when taken less than two hours before intense exercise under normal conditions.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>
- **Healthy adults, per day:** habitual intake up to 400 mg a day raises no safety concerns for non-pregnant adults.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **Pregnancy:** habitual intake up to 200 mg a day raises no safety concerns for the fetus.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **Breastfeeding:** single doses and daily intakes up to 200 mg raise no safety concerns for breastfed infants.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>
- **Children and adolescents:** EFSA found the data insufficient to derive a safe intake, and proposed 3 mg/kg body weight per day as a safety level for habitual intake.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

The FDA cites 400 mg a day for most adults as an amount not generally associated with negative effects. It recommends that people who are pregnant, trying to become pregnant or breastfeeding talk to their health care provider, and notes that some medications can make people more sensitive to caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> It estimates that toxic effects, such as seizures, can be observed with rapid consumption of around 1,200 mg.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> These limits count caffeine from all sources, not coffee alone.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The café survey above shows that a single large espresso can exceed 200 mg.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## What it does in the body

Caffeine is absorbed rapidly and practically completely: about 20% from the stomach and the rest from the small intestine, within 45 minutes of drinking it.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a>,<a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> Its effects come from adenosine receptor antagonism: caffeine occupies the receptors and so blocks the action of adenosine. The A1 and A2 receptors are blocked at serum concentrations of 0.2–2 mg/L.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> On alertness, EFSA’s panel concluded that a product should contain at least 75 mg of caffeine per serving to bear an alertness claim, and did not accept a proposed lower amount of 40 mg.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

Regular users who stop can get withdrawal symptoms. A review of 57 experimental and 9 survey studies, selected by a literature search with set inclusion criteria, confirmed headache, fatigue and irritability among them; headache occurred in 50% of participants in the experimental studies.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Symptoms typically began 12–24 h after the last dose, peaked at 20–51 h and lasted 2–9 days. Stopping doses as low as 100 mg a day was enough to produce them.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

## How it is broken down

Caffeine is almost entirely broken down in the liver.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> An estimated 95% of its clearance from the body is due to the liver enzyme CYP1A2<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>; FSANZ puts the enzyme’s share at more than 90%.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The main reaction gives paraxanthine and accounts for around 80–90% of caffeine demethylation; the remainder goes to theobromine (around 11%) and theophylline (around 4%).<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>

In healthy adults the half-life – the time the body takes to eliminate half of the caffeine – is about 4 h on average, with a range of 2–8 h, according to EFSA<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup>; FSANZ gives 3–7 h.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> EFSA notes that it varies widely with age, body weight, pregnancy, medication and liver health.<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup> In more detail:

- **Smoking:** increases caffeine clearance and shortens the half-life.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>
- **Oral contraceptives:** slow the elimination of caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>
- **Pregnancy:** in the first trimester the half-life is similar to that of non-pregnant women; it rises to about 10 h by week 17 and up to 18 h by the end of pregnancy.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>
- **Newborns:** up to 100 h, because their CYP1A2 enzyme system is immature; clearance reaches or exceeds adult levels by 5–6 months of age.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>
- **Liver disease:** various liver diseases strongly reduce caffeine clearance, cirrhosis most of all.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>
- **Some drugs:** fluvoxamine, pipemidic acid and norfloxacin inhibit caffeine clearance.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>
- **Genes:** twin studies show that CYP1A2 activity is largely heritable<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>, and variation in the CYP1A2 gene is a major cause of differences between people.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

## Caffeine during brewing

Caffeine is among the very soluble compounds in roasted coffee. A textbook chapter groups it with N-methylpyridinium, sugars and organic acids: they are extracted very efficiently in the first seconds of the brew.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Its solubility rises about fourfold between 80 and 100 °C, so small temperature changes in that range matter<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>: 1 g of caffeine dissolves in 5.5 mL of water at 80 °C and in 1.5 mL at 100 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup>

The brew method sets how far extraction goes. Spent grounds from a household filter held only 5.6% of the original caffeine, a moka pot 16.4%, and professional high-pressure machines in restaurants 21.5–30.8%.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In a lab study of espresso, about 67% of the caffeine reached the cup with a regular extraction, and about 85% when the shot was over-extracted with more water.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In filter coffee, caffeine is extracted relatively more efficiently than total solids, most probably because the paper filter holds back solids but not caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

So caffeine does not track the extraction yield one to one. A flow-controlled espresso study kept the dose at 20 g of arabica and changed the beverage mass: caffeine rose from 130 mg in a 20 g ristretto to 182.6 mg in a 40 g espresso and 204 mg in a 60 g lungo (means of 15 runs at different flow rates, grind settings and temperatures).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Most of each compound came out at the start of the shot, but the caffeine concentration in successive fractions fell more slowly than that of trigonelline and total dissolved solids, and flow rate and grind size changed caffeine only slightly.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A longer shot therefore adds caffeine, but less and less. How dissolved solids behave overall is covered in *Extraction yield and TDS*; cold brew kinetics in *Pour-over and immersion*.

## Per bean, per gram, per cup

The “dark roast has less caffeine” question has three answers, depending on what is compared.

- **Per gram of roasted coffee:** about the same or slightly more after a dark roast, because caffeine is stable and other matter is lost (12 → 13 mg/g in one arabica).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Per bean:** caffeine is stable through roasting, so the amount in each bean stays roughly unchanged while the bean gets lighter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Per cup:** it depends on the brew. In a 2024 study of 30 combinations of variety, roast and brew time, brewed in an AeroPress at a 15:1 water-to-coffee ratio, dark roasts gave lower caffeine concentrations than light and medium roasts under identical brewing conditions – but higher ones at identical extraction yields.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Extraction yields fell for roasts with mass losses above about 12–14%.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The authors propose that roasting both removes soluble compounds and opens the bean’s pores, and that these effects compete.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

The breakdown starts by removing one methyl group. Removing the methyl group at position 3 (N-3 demethylation) gives paraxanthine (1,7-dimethylxanthine), the main reaction; removing position 1 gives theobromine and position 7 theophylline.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> The main reaction is mediated exclusively by CYP1A2, and caffeine is used to measure CYP1A2 activity and to test liver function.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>

Because one enzyme does most of the work, the factors that change CYP1A2 activity change how long caffeine stays in the body. Smoking speeds clearance and shortens the half-life; oral contraceptives have the opposite effect.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> During pregnancy the half-life rises to about 10 h by week 17 and up to 18 h by the end of pregnancy.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Newborns clear caffeine very slowly because their CYP1A2 enzyme system is immature.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Twin studies show that CYP1A2 activity is largely heritable.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> Variants in *ADORA2A*, the gene for the adenosine A2A receptor, influence caffeine’s effects on anxiety and sleep.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> A genome-wide meta-analysis of 47,341 people of European descent linked regions near *CYP1A2* and near *AHR*, a gene that regulates CYP1A2, to how much caffeine people habitually drink.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup>

## Measuring caffeine

Studies measure caffeine by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), usually with UV detection: Ludwig et al. quantified it at 280 nm<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>, Schmieder et al. at 272 nm<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> and Vandeponseele et al. at 273 nm.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A coffee refractometer reads total dissolved solids, not caffeine; a 2024 study used refractometry for extraction yield and HPLC for caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> To find the caffeine left in spent grounds, one study first extracted them with water in a Soxhlet apparatus for 4 h, then ran HPLC.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Per-cup variability.** Published surveys find up to six-fold differences between cafés.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Dose, cup size and blend explain much of it<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>, but no source consulted here splits the variation into these causes.
- **Converted values.** Much of the per-serving data for filter, French press, cold brew and moka comes from concentrations converted to a standard serving by review authors, not from cups as served.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Genotype and advice.** Variants in CYP1A2 and the adenosine A2A receptor gene are linked to caffeine response and intake<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a>,<a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup>, but genotype is rarely reported in pharmacokinetic studies.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> EFSA’s intake advice is set by population group, not by genotype.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
