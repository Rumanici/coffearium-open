## What melanoidins are

Melanoidins are the brown compounds that form when coffee is roasted. A 2012 review defines them as high-molecular-weight, nitrogen-containing brown compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> They are large molecules, far larger than the aroma compounds that form alongside them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> They dissolve into the brew and make up a large part of what is dissolved.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

They are the end products of browning. According to a roasting chemistry chapter, the Maillard reaction and caramelisation are the main routes to these brown polymers.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The Maillard reaction joins sugars with amino compounds; caramelisation breaks down sugars alone.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Both are covered in their own entries.

Melanoidins are not one substance. They are a mixture of many molecules of different size and make-up.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> No one has yet drawn their full structure. A thesis on coffee brew melanoidins states that their chemical structures remain unknown.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## What they are made of

In coffee, melanoidins form from three groups of building blocks: polysaccharides, proteins and chlorogenic acids.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Polysaccharides.** These are long sugar chains from the bean’s cell walls. Arabinogalactans appear relatively more involved than galactomannans, according to a 2008 roasting study.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Proteins.** In the same study, the melanoidin level in every fraction rose with its nitrogen and protein content.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Chlorogenic acids.** These are phenolic acids of green coffee. A 2006 study found them built into the large brew molecules.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The roasting chemistry chapter sums this up as a polymerisation of polysaccharides, proteins and polyphenols.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Taken together, the studies consulted for this entry picture a coffee melanoidin as a sugar backbone with protein and brown, phenolic pieces attached; this is a working picture, not an established structure.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## How much there is

Melanoidins are a large part of what dissolves into a brew. A 2002 study reported that they make up to 25% of the dry matter of coffee beverages.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Measured amounts vary widely between studies, which isolated them in different ways. A 2011 survey collected values from eight studies of filter coffee. They ranged from 36 to 115 g of melanoidins per kg of roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Across these studies, using medium-roast values where available, the survey obtained a mean of 7.2 g per 100 g of roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## How roast degree changes them

Darker roasts give more melanoidins. A 2011 survey describes this as known: the darker the roast, the higher the amount.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2002 study roasted one coffee to light, medium and dark. The amount of melanoidins in the brews rose with the intensity of the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

A 2024 study of cold brew found the same direction. It roasted one Ethiopian arabica coffee to three degrees, from Agtron 73 to 53.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Melanoidins, estimated from the brew’s absorbance, rose from light to dark in both brewing methods tested.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

Their make-up changes too. A 2008 study found that light roasting mainly formed melanoidins of intermediate size. Prolonged roasting mainly formed large ones.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2002 study found the opposite trend in molecular weight (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## What they do in the cup

- **Colour.** Melanoidins are the brown pigments of roasted coffee. A 2025 colour study attributes the darkening of beans during roasting to their formation.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The roasting chemistry chapter says the same.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Roast colour measurement is covered in Roast degree and colour.
- **Aroma.** Melanoidins bind some aroma compounds. In a 2002 study, adding coffee melanoidins lowered 2-furfurylthiol, a key roasty odorant, by a factor of 16.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The authors link this to the loss of roasty, sulphury aroma soon after brewing.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Bitterness.** A 2020 sensory paper names melanoidins among the main sources of bitterness in coffee, citing earlier work.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Mouthfeel.** In one 2025 study, tasters could detect a phenolic-rich melanoidin fraction added to coffee, as a subtle drying or astringent sensation.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A link between melanoidins and body is often assumed, but a 2020 analysis of eight roasting studies found no evidence supporting it.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Acidity.** Melanoidins in brewed coffee carry a negative charge.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured how much they add to measured or perceived acidity. Acids are covered in Acids in coffee.

## Structure and formation

**Definition.** A thesis on coffee brew melanoidins defines them as macromolecular, nitrogenous, brown Maillard reaction end-products.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A 2012 review keeps the same three features: high molecular weight, nitrogen and brown colour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In practice, each study measures the brown material that its membrane or column retains.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Polysaccharide backbone.** According to the roasting chemistry chapter, the green bean’s arabinogalactans are highly branched and covalently linked to proteins as arabinogalactan proteins (AGPs).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Roasting breaks their main and side chains and releases free arabinose. The chapter states that most of this arabinose is involved in melanoidin formation.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2007 study isolated an AGP-containing fraction from brew that was brown, where the same material is white in green beans; the authors concluded that the AGPs had undergone the Maillard reaction.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 2008 study found that arabinogalactans seem to be relatively more involved than galactomannans.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Protein and nitrogen.** In a 2006 study of brew, the high-molecular-weight fraction showed a strong correlation between melanoidin content, protein and non-protein nitrogen.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The 2008 roasting study found that the melanoidin level in all fractions correlated with nitrogen and protein, and suggested that proteins and chlorogenic acids are primarily involved in melanoidin formation.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Chlorogenic acids.** In the 2006 study, the low-molecular-weight chlorogenic acid was incorporated into the high-molecular-weight material.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The thesis summary states that intact chlorogenic acids are incorporated through their phenolic acid part by non-ester bonds.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> For light roasts, the 2008 study proposed incorporation reactions between chlorogenic acids, sucrose and amino acids or protein fragments.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A separate entry on chlorogenic acids covers their breakdown in roasting.

**Charge.** Ion-exchange experiments showed brew melanoidins to be negatively charged, with no positive charge at the pH of a coffee brew.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> For the AGP part, the authors suggest the charge probably comes from uronic acids; for melanoidins in general, they hypothesise that acidic groups form or are taken up during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**In-bean studies.** According to the roasting chemistry chapter, the bean itself has been used as the reaction vessel to study how coffee melanoidins form, by changing its content of precursors before roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The chapter notes that free sugars and free amino acids are almost used up in roasting, while crude protein changes only slightly.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The polysaccharides are partly broken down, more in dark than in light roasts: mainly the arabinogalactan side chains, while cellulose and mannans stay almost intact.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How much is degraded is covered in Carbohydrates and body.

## Isolation and measurement

There is no single standard method. Melanoidins are separated from the rest of the brew by size, then weighed or measured by colour.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

- **Ultrafiltration and diafiltration.** A membrane holds back the large molecules. In the 2011 survey’s own measurements, the content was determined by weighing the freeze-dried product after ultrafiltration.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The 2006 study used diafiltration with a 3 kDa cut-off.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Dialysis.** A dialysis membrane also separates by size. In the 2006 study, dialysis of the whole brew retained 7.4% of the dry, defatted beans, against 3.6% for the diafiltration retentate; the authors attribute the difference to the separation technique.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Gel filtration.** A column separates molecules by size. The 2002 study used it, followed by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The 2001 aroma study used a Sephadex G-25 column and collected four fractions from about 1,500 to 60,000 Da.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Colour.** Brown colour is read as absorbance, for example as a specific extinction coefficient at 405 nm<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> or as absorbance at 420 nm of the diluted brew.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The 2024 cold brew study read its values from absorbance against a standard curve, not by weighing.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

The choice of method changes the result. In the 2011 survey, values per kg of roasted coffee differed about threefold between studies (see the chart above).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Binding of aroma compounds

**Thiols.** In a 2001 model study, coffee melanoidins added to a mixture of odorants reduced the headspace of three thiols: 2-furfurylthiol, 3-methyl-2-butene-1-thiol and 3-mercapto-3-methylbutyl formate.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Half of the 2-furfurylthiol was lost from the headspace after 20 min, and almost all after 30 min.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The fraction of 1,500–3,000 Da was the most effective against 2-furfurylthiol.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The authors proposed that thiols add to double bonds or carbonyl groups in the melanoidins, but did not prove it.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

A 2002 follow-up found a drastic decrease of odorous thiols when melanoidins were added. Spectroscopic evidence pointed to covalent binding through pyrazinium intermediates from the Maillard reaction.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> According to the roasting chemistry chapter, chlorogenic acids and their breakdown products also form low-molecular-weight thiol-binding sites.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Losses of aroma in the cup are covered in The aroma of coffee and Freshness and storage.

## Metal chelation, antioxidant activity and health

**Metals.** The thesis on brew melanoidins lists metal chelation among their reported properties.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> No study of metal binding by coffee melanoidins was consulted in full for this entry, so no values are given.

**Antioxidant activity in vitro.** In the 2002 study, the radical-scavenging activity of brew melanoidins fell as roasting intensified. Their ability to prevent linoleic acid peroxidation was higher in dark-roasted samples.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> These are laboratory assays, not measurements in people.

**Health.** A 2012 review lists antioxidant, antimicrobial, anticariogenic, anti-inflammatory, antihypertensive and antiglycative activities that have been attributed to coffee melanoidins, and names coffee brew as one of the main sources of melanoidins in the diet.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2011 survey estimated melanoidin intake from coffee at an order of magnitude of 1 g per day.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> No health authority consulted for this entry has assessed coffee melanoidins. This entry makes no health claim.

## Melanoidins and sensory attributes

**Bitterness and aftertaste.** A 2020 analysis of eight roasting studies names melanoidins as one of two main sources of bitterness, citing earlier work. It also describes most of the lingering aftertaste as due to heavy, poorly water-soluble bitter melanoidins.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Both are background statements in the paper, not its own measurements.

**Body.** The same analysis discussed the belief that more melanoidins mean higher molecular weight, more viscosity and more body. Body was not affected by roasting time in either phase, and the authors found no evidence supporting the link.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> A cupping chapter lists melanoidins, with fibres, lipids and fine particles, among factors that may play a role in body, and states that exact correlations are not known.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> More is in Body and mouthfeel.

**Astringency.** A 2025 study split a high-body drip coffee into fractions and tasted each. Added to water, or to a low-body coffee, a phenolic-rich melanoidin fraction was perceptibly different, described as drying or astringent; the authors call the effect subtle. They present this as the first evidence of melanoidins in coffee’s tactile profile.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Structure.** No full structure of a coffee melanoidin has been established. A 2012 review described exact structures and formation mechanisms as far from elucidated.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Method dependence.** Amounts differ about threefold between the studies collected in one survey.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> No agreed reference method was found among the sources consulted.
- **Molecular weight and roast.** Studies disagree on whether molecular weight rises or falls with roast degree (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Acidity.** Brew melanoidins are negatively charged<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>, but no study consulted for this entry measured their contribution to titratable acidity or to perceived acidity.
- **Body.** A role in body is assumed in a cupping chapter<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> but was not supported in a multi-study sensory analysis.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Roast profile.** No study consulted for this entry compared melanoidin content between fast and slow roasts to the same colour.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Borrelli et al., 2002 – Melanoidins at three roast degrees.</strong> One coffee was roasted light, medium and dark, and the brew melanoidins were isolated by gel filtration. Darker roasting gave more melanoidins of lower molecular weight; their radical-scavenging activity fell, while protection against lipid peroxidation was higher in the dark roast.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 50(22), 6527–6533 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf025686o">doi:10.1021/jf025686o</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Hofmann & Schieberle, 2002 – Thiols bound by melanoidins.</strong> Adding coffee melanoidins sharply lowered odour-active thiols, with 2-furfurylthiol down by a factor of 16. Spectroscopy pointed to covalent binding through Maillard-derived pyrazinium intermediates, offered as a cause of aroma staling after brewing.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 50(2), 319–326 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf010823n">doi:10.1021/jf010823n</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bekedam et al., 2008 – Roast degree and formation routes.</strong> Molecular-weight fractions from green, light, medium and dark roasts were analysed for nitrogen, protein, phenolics and sugars. Melanoidin levels followed nitrogen and protein; light roasting formed mainly intermediate-weight melanoidins, prolonged roasting mainly high-weight ones, with arabinogalactans more involved than galactomannans.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 56(16), 7138–7145 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf800999a">doi:10.1021/jf800999a</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Fogliano & Morales, 2011 – How much melanoidin people drink.</strong> Collects melanoidin values from coffee studies that used gel filtration, dialysis or ultrafiltration. Filter brews ranged from 36 to 115 g per kg of roasted coffee, and daily intake from coffee was estimated at around 1 g.</p>
<p class="meta">Food & Function 2(2), 117–123 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1039/c0fo00156b">doi:10.1039/c0fo00156b</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Moreira et al., 2012 – Review of coffee melanoidins.</strong> Reviews what is known about their structure, formation and possible health effects. Concludes that polysaccharides, proteins and chlorogenic acids take part, but that exact structures and mechanisms remain unresolved.</p>
<p class="meta">Food & Function 3(9), 903–915 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1039/c2fo30048f">doi:10.1039/c2fo30048f</a></p>
</div>
