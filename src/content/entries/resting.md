## What resting is

Resting – *reposo* in Spanish-speaking producing countries – is the time dried coffee is held before it goes to the dry mill. The coffee rests as parchment (washed and honey coffees) or as dried cherry (naturals). Guatemala's national coffee association recommends holding coffee in the store for at least three to four weeks to stabilise the moisture of the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The same body also recommends a short rest on the patio: once a batch reaches its drying end point, it is covered with sacks for 8 to 10 hours before bagging, again to stabilise moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

In Kenya, dry parchment is held in co-operative stores for a short while before it is delivered for hulling, for a maximum of six months.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> On farms in Lao PDR, parchment or dry cherry is stored in jute bags, woven polyethylene sacks, special polyethylene bags or silos.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**What resting is not.** Resting of roasted coffee – letting beans degas after roasting – is a different subject and is not covered here. Storage of green coffee after hulling and export is also a separate topic; it is touched on below only where it helps explain why parchment keeps better.

**A word of caution on terms.** In Colombian quality language, *reposado* is the name of a defect, not of the rest period: a stale, aged flavour with bleached, whitish beans that comes from poor storage.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A well-managed *reposo* is meant to prevent exactly that.

## Why producers rest coffee

**Even moisture.** Drying never ends with a completely uniform lot. Moisture gradients form along the airflow path in dryers, and casehardening leaves beans with a dry outer shell that traps moisture inside.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Uneven drying, casehardening and rewetting all lead to inconsistent cups and poorer storage stability.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Resting gives the water time to move until the lot settles at one level; that is the stated purpose of the rest in Guatemalan guidance.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Protection in parchment.** Coffee keeps better in parchment than hulled. Cenicafé reports that hulled coffee is more exposed to deterioration in storage than parchment coffee, and attributes this to the parchment and silverskin acting as barriers to water.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In a laboratory storage study, hulled washed coffee lost viability within six months, while seeds kept in parchment stayed alive for more than a year.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Logistics.** Resting also bridges the time between drying and milling or sale. In Kenya this holding is limited to six months.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## How coffee is rested

**Moisture going in.** Coffee should go into storage at 10–12% moisture; the sources do not state the basis, and Anacafé gives the range for the hulled bean (*grano oro*).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The Codex code of practice considers parchment coffee protected from fungi at below 12.5% moisture (wet basis) and a water activity of at most 0.67–0.70.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Cenicafé warns against storing coffee drier than 10%, because very dry coffee also oxidises more readily.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**The store.** Kenyan co-operatives stack sacks on wooden pallets 0.5 m above the ground and 0.5 m away from the walls, in stores built for good air circulation.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Anacafé gives the same 50 cm distance from the walls, asks for ventilation between stacks and for no light inside the store.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Stored coffee takes up strong odours, so fuel, fertilisers and agricultural chemicals must be kept away from it.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Air.** Anacafé recommends about 20 °C and 65% relative humidity.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Cenicafé reports that sound parchment at 10–12% moisture keeps its quality for up to 10 months at 65–70% relative humidity, at 8–15 °C in one bulletin and 15–18 °C in a later one.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Colombian coffee zones average 18–22 °C with humidity above 75%, which is why Cenicafé insists on ventilated stores.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Bags.** Jute bags are the usual choice;<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> uncovered jute in a moist climate lets parchment or green coffee take up water and go mouldy.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Hermetic systems are also recommended.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The evidence on impermeable bags is mixed. In a Brazilian trial, parchment and dried cherry stored for 150 days at ambient conditions (about 27.6 °C and 71.2% relative humidity) gave better cups in jute, and the silo bag added no protection.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A master's dissertation from the same group found the opposite under high-humidity stress: there the silo bag prevented rewetting and limited the loss of cup quality better than jute.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## What the evidence shows

The evidence base for *reposo* as such is thin. No controlled study found for this entry compares resting times – say one, four and eight weeks – against cup quality. The recommended three to four weeks comes from producer guidance, not from a published trial.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> What exists is research on neighbouring questions: pauses during drying, and the storage of parchment and green coffee over months.

**Pauses during drying.** In a Brazilian trial, drying of parchment was stopped at 16, 20 or 24% moisture (wet basis) and the coffee held in wooden boxes for 2, 6 or 12 days before drying was finished to 11%.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> After 12 months of storage, the 2- and 6-day pauses showed less membrane damage than continuous drying and the 12-day pause; sugars, acidity and cup quality showed no significant differences.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Storage over months.** Quality does decline with time. In coffee dried to 11% (wet basis) and stored for 12 months in jute bags in an uncontrolled store, electrical conductivity, potassium leaching and fat acidity rose with storage time, and pulped (washed) coffee kept a better cup and changed less chemically than natural coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In another study at 23 °C and 60% relative humidity for 90 and 180 days, washed coffee showed better quality than natural coffee, and coffee dried at 60 °C was worst after 90 days.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Colour changes too: after eight months, the green and blue colour and the lightness of beans depended on processing, hulling and storage conditions (10 °C and 50% RH versus uncontrolled 25 °C).<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Moisture redistribution

Coffee is hygroscopic: its moisture changes with the temperature and humidity of the surrounding air.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The physics of sorption and equilibrium moisture is covered in the entry on moisture and water activity. For resting, two gradients matter.

**Between beans.** Dryers leave moisture gradients along the airflow path, which is why static beds are stirred or partly unloaded.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Bagging and holding the lot together lets the wetter and drier beans converge.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Within the bean.** In casehardening, the outer layers dry faster than the interior and form a hard shell that traps residual moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Resting is meant to let this trapped water even out, but no source consulted here measures it happening. The intermittent-drying trial shows the same principle in a controlled way: holding partly dried parchment for a few days before finishing the drying did not harm the coffee, and short pauses reduced membrane damage.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

The sources consulted for this entry give no measured moisture profile across a single coffee bean before and after resting, and no published curve of moisture spread in a lot over resting time. The figure at the top is therefore a schematic only.

## The seed during rest

The coffee seed is still alive when it comes off the patio. The germination and drought-stress responses that processing sets off belong to the drying stage and are covered in the drying entry. By the time coffee rests, water is scarce: Selmar and colleagues note the general assumption that in cells with a residual water content below about 20%, water is so restricted that the cell's metabolism is shut down.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

What remains is slow death of the seed. At 22 °C and 63% relative humidity with 11.5% moisture, more than half of the hulled wet-processed seeds lost viability within three months, fewer than 10% were alive after six months and all were dead after a year.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> More than half of the seeds stored in parchment were still alive after more than a year.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Over the same storage, glucose and fructose fell slightly and glutamine declined significantly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The authors link loss of viability to the "flattening" of cup quality in stored coffee, and propose Maillard reactions during storage, not metabolism, as the cause of the loss of aroma precursors.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Rewetting and mould.** Coffee that is not dry enough, or that takes up water from humid air, can go mouldy in store.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Cenicafé asks for protection from rewetting through drying, transport and storage.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Uneven lots.** Unevenly dried or rewetted coffee gives inconsistent cups and keeps less well.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **The *reposado* defect.** Aged, bitter, low-acid, heavy and "stored" flavours with discoloured, very light green, whitish beans.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Cenicafé links it to store temperatures above 20 °C, grain moisture above 12.5% and exposure to oxygen, heat and moisture outside the safe range, and notes that deterioration accelerates with time.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Over-dry coffee.** Coffee stored below 10% moisture is also prone to oxidation.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Taint.** Stored coffee picks up odours from fuel and agricultural chemicals, and these carry into the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Resting too long.** Storage time itself degrades quality: membrane-damage markers and fat acidity rose over 12 months in stored coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Parchment slows this but does not stop it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Measurement

The moisture reference for rested coffee is the 10–12% storage range,<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> with the Codex limit of below 12.5% (wet basis) and a water activity of at most 0.67–0.70 for protecting parchment coffee from fungi.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Storage studies track quality with physiological tests – electrical conductivity and potassium leaching of soaked beans, which rise as cell membranes degrade – together with acidity, sugars and cupping.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Viability is tested with the tetrazolium test.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Bean colour can be measured instrumentally as colour intensity and lightness.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

The spread of moisture within a lot – the thing resting is meant to reduce – is not reported as a measured variable in any source consulted for this entry. Water activity, discussed in the moisture entry, is the natural companion measurement, but no study of resting time using it was found.

## Open questions

- **How long is long enough?** The three-to-four-week recommendation is guidance.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A trial measuring moisture spread and cup quality after different resting times, under defined store conditions, was not found.
- **Does resting improve the cup, or only protect it?** Producer guidance speaks of stabilising moisture;<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> no source consulted here shows a cup score that rises during a rest.
- **Why does parchment keep the seed alive?** The storage study that found the effect could not explain it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Where to rest.** Store climate clearly matters,<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> but a direct comparison of resting at a cool highland farm and at a warm, humid port or mill was not found.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Selmar, Bytof & Knopp, 2008 – Viability and aroma precursors in storage.</strong> Wet-processed Arabica was stored for up to two years at controlled temperature and humidity, either hulled or in parchment. Hulled seeds died within months, while parchment kept most seeds alive beyond a year; the authors connect the loss of viability with the flattening of stored coffee and point to Maillard reactions as the cause of falling aroma precursors.</p>
<p class="meta">Annals of Botany 101(1), 31–38 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcm277">doi:10.1093/aob/mcm277</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Borém et al., 2014 – Pauses during drying.</strong> Parchment was dried to 16, 20 or 24% moisture, held for 2, 6 or 12 days, then dried to 11% and stored for a year. Short holds of 2 and 6 days gave less membrane damage than continuous drying or a 12-day hold; other chemical and sensory measures did not differ.</p>
<p class="meta">Bioscience Journal 30(Suppl. 2), 609–616 · <a href="https://seer.ufu.br/index.php/biosciencejournal/article/view/18209">seer.ufu.br</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Coradi et al., 2007 – Drying and storage of natural and washed coffee.</strong> Coffee dried on a yard or at 40 and 60 °C was stored at 23 °C and 60% relative humidity for 90 and 180 days. Washed coffee showed better quality than natural coffee, and coffee dried at 60 °C had the poorest quality after 90 days.</p>
<p class="meta">Coffee Science 2(1), 38–47 · <a href="https://coffeescience.ufla.br/index.php/Coffeescience/article/view/37">coffeescience.ufla.br</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Sperotto et al., 2015 – Jute versus silo bag.</strong> Parchment and dried cherry were stored for 150 days at ambient store conditions in jute bags or in an impermeable silo bag. All treatments lost cup quality; jute gave the better cups and the silo bag added no protection. An earlier master's dissertation by the first author, under high-humidity stress, had found the silo bag better.</p>
<p class="meta">Energia na Agricultura 30(2), 210–216 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.17224/EnergAgric.2015v30n2p210-216">doi:10.17224/EnergAgric.2015v30n2p210-216</a></p>
</div>
