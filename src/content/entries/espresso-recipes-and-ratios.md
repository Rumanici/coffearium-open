## What an espresso recipe contains

An espresso recipe is a short list of settings. Two of them define it. The **dose** is the mass of dry ground coffee in the basket.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The **beverage mass** is the weight of espresso in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In café use the beverage mass is also called the yield. This entry avoids that word, because “extraction yield” means something else (see below).

The **beverage ratio** links the two: beverage mass ÷ dose, written as dose to beverage.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 20 g dose that gives 40 g of espresso is therefore 1:2.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2023 study calls this ratio the “brew ratio”.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> On this site, brew ratio means water ÷ coffee, the usual way to describe filter coffee (see the extraction yield entry). A coffee science textbook also describes espresso by the ratio of dry coffee to beverage mass.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

The other settings complete the recipe:

- **Time.** Shot time. In one aroma study it was counted from the start of the pump.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Water temperature** at the group.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Pump pressure**, or the flow rate on machines that control flow.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Pre-infusion**, a first phase that fills the basket and wets the bed. In one flow-controlled study it ran at 7 mL/s until the pressure rose above 2.5 bar.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Grind.** The grinder setting that makes the bed slow the water enough.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Basket.** It holds the dose. According to a textbook, too much coffee leaves the bed no room to swell.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How the coffee is spread and tamped in it is covered in the puck preparation entry.

Some definitions give the cup in millilitres. The Certified Italian Espresso, for example, is defined by volume, with the foam included.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to a grinding chapter of a coffee science textbook, espresso machines with scales stop the shot at a set beverage weight.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Grinders that dose by weight then lock the coffee-to-beverage ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Named formats

**Certified Italian Espresso.** The Italian Espresso National Institute (Istituto Nazionale Espresso Italiano, INEI) specifies a single espresso by a set of values. As reported in a 2013 study, they are 7 ± 0.5 g of coffee and water leaving the machine at 90 ± 2 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The pressure is 9 ± 1 bar, the time 25 ± 2.5 s and the cup 25 ± 2.5 mL, including foam.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A textbook chapter reports 88 ± 2 °C, 25 ± 5 s and a 25–40 mL cup for the same definition (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2013 authors note that meeting these values is not enough on its own for quality.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Ristretto, espresso and lungo.** These names describe how much beverage is taken from the same dose. In a 2023 study, ristretto, espresso and espresso lungo were about 1:1, 1:2 and 1:3, from a 20 g dose.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> That means 20 g, 40 g and 60 g in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2013 comparison used “lungo” more broadly, for a less intense coffee in a larger cup of 100–250 mL.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Its machine lunghi were 120 mL per cup.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> No standard consulted for this entry defines ristretto or lungo.

**Single and double.** The Italian definition uses about 7 g for one cup.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Research recipes use larger doses of 16 g, 18 g or 20 g.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In two of these studies, “double” named the basket or the shot, not always two cups.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In one study, 16 g gave two espressos of 30 mL.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In another, 18 g in a double basket gave one shot of about 30 g.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Cup size in Italy.** According to a 2011 review, the shot runs until the cup meets the drinker’s preference or the regional tradition.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In Italy that is 15–50 mL, with 25–30 mL as the optimal outcome for a regular espresso.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## What each setting changes

**Beverage mass and ratio.** Taking more beverage from the same dose dilutes the cup but extracts more. In a 2023 study, the beverage mass collected changed the amount of each compound in the cup more than flow rate, grind or temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In a 2013 comparison, lungo shots extracted more efficiently than espressos, because more water washed through the coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Per cup, the lunghi generally held as much of the measured compounds as the espressos, or more.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Dose.** In a 2007 sensory and chemical study, bitterness, astringency and several flavour attributes pointed to 6.5 g or 7.5 g of coffee per 40 mL for conventionally roasted coffees.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> A 2023 study summarises earlier work: more coffee for a similar cup volume put more caffeine, trigonelline and 5-CQA in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A textbook warns that too much coffee leaves no room to swell during wetting. In its account, the bed then compacts too much, percolation is disturbed and solids end up in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Grind.** According to a coffee science textbook, grind is one of the main settings that decide how easily water passes through the bed.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It is a key setting for the barista to adjust.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A finer grind usually extracts more efficiently and also lengthens the flow time, so the two effects multiply.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Too fine a grind can block even flow (see Deep dive).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Time.** Shot time is mostly a result, not a setting. A textbook describes brewing time as the time needed to produce a given beverage volume, set by the pump and the bed together.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a 2024 study, shots of under 10 s and of 15 s already reached 17–18% extraction yield.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> For the one coffee tested, one panellist judged the flavour best at about 30 s and 19–20%.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Temperature.** According to a textbook, less polar compounds, among them most bitter ones, need higher temperatures to dissolve.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In its account, too hot water gives bitter and astringent notes, and too cool water a sweet–acidic, under-extracted cup.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Measured effects in espresso differ between studies (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Pressure and flow.** About 9 bar is the conventional pump pressure.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to a textbook, the pressure on the bed is not set directly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It results from the push of the pump and the resistance of the bed.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2011 review states that the flow into the cup should ideally be close to 1 mL/s.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A survey cited in the review found 0.40–2.73 mL/s in Italian cafés.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The espresso machine entry explains how pumps and the bed set pressure and flow.

## Calculating ratio and extraction yield

Three numbers describe a shot: dose, beverage mass and strength (TDS, total dissolved solids, read with a refractometer). From them come the ratio and the extraction yield, the share of the dry coffee that dissolved into the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Beverage ratio = beverage mass ÷ dose**, written as 1 : (beverage ÷ dose).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Extraction yield (%) = TDS (%) × beverage mass (g) ÷ dose (g)**<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

A worked example, with our own arithmetic:

1. Weigh the dry dose: 20.0 g.
2. Weigh the espresso in the cup: 40.0 g. The ratio is 40.0 ÷ 20.0 = 2, written 1:2.
3. Read the TDS of a filtered sample with a refractometer, as research labs do.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Say it reads 9.7%, close to the 9.68% average a 2023 study reported at 1:2.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
4. Calculate the extraction yield: 9.7% × 40.0 ÷ 20.0 = 19.4%.

The same coffee taken to 1:3 gives a weaker cup with a higher yield; at 1:1 the reverse. The figure shows this for the 2023 study.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Because the cup is small, a few grams of beverage move the yield. In the example, 2 g more at the same TDS adds about one percentage point (our own arithmetic). The extraction yield entry explains TDS, refractometers and the brewing control chart. Its control-chart tool includes an espresso calculator.

## Dialling in

“Dialling in” is the routine of adjusting a recipe for a new coffee. According to the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA), baristas adjust dose, grind and extraction level methodically until the best flavour appears.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> A coffee science textbook names small adjustments, frequent tasting and objective measurement where possible as best practice.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The steps below follow from these sources; the sequence is ours.

1. **Fix the dose** by weight, leaving the bed room to swell.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
2. **Fix the ratio** by stopping the shot at a set beverage weight.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
3. **Change the grind** to change the flow and the time. Finer usually slows the shot and raises extraction; coarser does the reverse.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
4. **Taste** after each small change.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
5. **Measure** TDS where you can, and calculate the extraction yield.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
6. **Re-check during the day.** According to the textbook, temperature and humidity in a café affect extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The barista may have to adjust over a serving day.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Baristas also use rules of thumb for target times and ratios. No study consulted for this entry tested such rules, so none are given here.

## The science of the ratio

Espresso is a percolation: fresh water keeps passing through the bed, so the extract changes over the shot. In a 2023 flow-controlled study, the concentration of every measured compound was highest in the first drops and fell exponentially as more espresso was collected.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Trigonelline and total dissolved solids fell fastest, then 5-CQA (5-caffeoylquinic acid, the main chlorogenic acid), then caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

This is why the ratio drives both strength and yield. Each extra gram in the cup is weaker than the last, so TDS falls; it still adds dissolved coffee, so the yield keeps rising.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In that study, going from 1:2 to 1:3 added far less dissolved coffee than going from 1:1 to 1:2: 0.31 g against 0.96 g (our own arithmetic from its table).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

A coffee science textbook explains the flavour side. Very soluble compounds – caffeine, sugars, organic acids – come out in the first seconds.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Less soluble bitter compounds, such as phenylindanes, keep coming out over time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In its account, this is why long cups tend to be both stronger and more bitter, and why the profile moves from sweet–acidic to bitter–harsh as extraction goes on.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Aroma follows a similar pattern by polarity: in a 2016 on-line study, the most polar compounds came out within the first seconds, while part of the least polar ones was still coming out at 25 s.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Flow and pressure.** In the 2023 study, a slower flow gave higher concentrations early in the shot, and a faster flow lowered the mass of every compound in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Flow rate had the strongest effect of the three settings tested; a finer grind and a higher temperature made it stronger.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The authors relate faster flow to higher pressure through Darcy’s law, and note that earlier pressure studies also found less in the cup at higher pressure.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Their flow-controlled shots ran at measured pressures of 2.6–8.4 bar, below the conventional 9 bar.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to a textbook, a higher pressure was linked to a more viscous espresso with more body in an earlier study.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Pressure and flow profiles.** According to a textbook, newer machines let the barista control flow or vary pressure during the shot, to reproduce chosen extraction profiles.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2023 study used one such machine for flow control.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry compared the flavour of different pressure or flow profiles.

**Grind and the bed.** In a 2020 study combining a model with experiments, the model assumed even flow and predicted that yield falls steadily as the grind gets coarser.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Measured yields instead peaked, with lower yields at very fine settings too.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The authors attribute this to uneven flow at fine settings, which wastes coffee and makes shots less reproducible, and they outline a procedure to avoid it.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In a 2024 study, adding fines lowered the bed’s permeability, slowed the flow and lengthened the shot.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In a 2020 study, smaller particles extracted more caffeine per cup: more at 200–300 µm than at 500–1,000 µm, in every basket tested.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Grinders differ.** According to the grinding chapter of a coffee science textbook, different grinders need different settings to give the same flow time at the same ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In its account, a grinder with a wide particle size distribution over-extracts the fines and under-extracts the coarse particles. The cup is then both more bitter and more acidic, at a lower overall yield.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A recipe moved to another grinder therefore needs dialling in again.

## Signs of under- and over-extraction

A coffee science textbook describes brews below a long-used yield range as under-extracted, too sweet and acidic, and brews above it as over-extracted, bitter and astringent.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It notes that optimal yields outside this range have been reported for several methods.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The extraction yield entry gives the range and its origin. A 2020 sensory study that spanned the classic brewing control chart used drip coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No espresso-specific sensory map was found among the sources consulted for this entry.

A 2011 review lists visual signs in the crema:<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

- **Pale crema:** under-extracted, probably from too coarse a grind, too cool water or too short a time.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Very dark crema with a white spot in the middle:** grind too fine or too much coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **White foam with large bubbles:** over-extracted with water that was too hot.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Only a white spot in the centre:** over-extracted because the shot ran too long.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

The same review notes that such readings of crema rest on decades of trial and error more than on objective measurement.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Tasting and, where possible, measuring remain the checks the brewing textbook recommends.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Uneven extraction** is a defect of its own. In the 2020 grind study, uneven flow at fine settings lowered the average yield and made shots less reproducible.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Channelling, where water takes easy paths through the bed, is one form of uneven flow. The puck preparation entry covers its causes.

## Open questions

- **Espresso control chart.** A 2020 sensory study across the range of the classic control chart used drip coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry mapped espresso flavour or consumer liking across ratio, TDS and extraction yield.
- **Temperature.** Studies disagree on how much brew temperature changes an espresso (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry fixed strength and yield while varying espresso temperature.
- **Profiles.** Some machines vary pressure or flow during the shot, but no controlled sensory study of profiles was consulted for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Less coffee per cup.** Two studies point to using less coffee: one by avoiding uneven flow, one by finer particles and basket design.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Whether a smaller dose keeps the flavour was not tested in the abstracts consulted for this entry.
- **Café rules of thumb.** No study consulted for this entry tested the target times and ratios used in café practice.

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Schmieder et al., 2023 – Flow, grind and temperature.</strong> Collected ten fractions from each flow-controlled shot of a 20 g dose and calculated the cup for ristretto, espresso and lungo. Flow rate had the strongest effect of the three settings, but all were minor next to the amount of beverage collected.</p>
<p class="meta">Foods 12(15), 2871 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/foods12152871">doi:10.3390/foods12152871</a></p>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Smrke et al., 2024 – Fines in espresso.</strong> Followed extraction over shots of 20 g to 40 g at different grind settings, with sieved fines added. Short shots already reached most of the yield, and fines acted mainly by slowing the flow through the bed.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 14, 5612 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-55831-x">doi:10.1038/s41598-024-55831-x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Cameron et al., 2020 – Uneven flow and grind.</strong> Compared a model of espresso extraction with measured shots. Yield peaked at an intermediate grind instead of rising steadily with fineness, which the authors trace to uneven flow; they propose a more reproducible procedure.</p>
<p class="meta">Matter 2(3), 631–648 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matt.2019.12.019">doi:10.1016/j.matt.2019.12.019</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Andueza et al., 2007 – Coffee to water ratio.</strong> Brewed espressos from arabica and robusta, conventionally roasted and torrefacto, at different amounts of coffee per cup. <p class="meta">Journal of the Science of Food and Agriculture 87(4), 586–592 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/jsfa.2720">doi:10.1002/jsfa.2720</a></p>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Gloess et al., 2013 – Espresso and lungo.</strong> Compared espressos and lunghi from semi-automatic and fully automatic machines with other brew methods. Lunghi extracted more efficiently and held as much per cup as espressos, while espressos were more concentrated.</p>
