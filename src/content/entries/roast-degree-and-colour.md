## What roast degree means

Roast degree describes how far a roast has gone. One study, for example, records it as the final bean colour, so that different time–temperature paths can be compared.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> It can be defined through bean weight loss, colour or selected chemical indicators.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Sensory character is also used.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Researchers note that a concise, universally accepted protocol for evaluating it does not exist,<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> and a chemistry chapter says the same of its definition.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

In practice, roast degree is mostly judged by colour. Beans change from greenish grey-blue to yellow, orange, brown, dark brown and almost black.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Colour development is closely tied to flavour development, which makes bean colour the best single indicator of roast degree.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Colour is also the most widely used parameter in day-to-day practice, measured as the light reflected by ground beans or, still, by eye.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Two coffees roasted to the same colour can still differ. Colour fixes where the roast ended, but not the time–temperature path that led there.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> One study therefore recommends reading colour together with development time (see Colour as a sufficient indicator).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## How colour is measured

Roast-colour instruments shine light onto the coffee and measure the light reflected back.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Two families of measurement are in use.

- **Colorimetry in CIE L\*a\*b\*.** This international colour space describes a colour by lightness (L\*) and two colour coordinates, a\* and b\*, from which chroma and hue are derived.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> For roasted coffee, L\* falls steadily as the roast gets darker, and, with hue, it is the most useful of the parameters for following roast colour.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The SCA names CIELAB colorimetry as one way to control roast level.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Roast meters with their own scales.** The SCA also names infrared spectrometry, with the Agtron device as its example.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In one study, a dedicated meter (Neuhaus Neotec Colortest II) measured the light reflected by 25–30 g of ground coffee at 900 nm, in the near infrared.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Dedicated roast meters, many of them infrared, usually measure more accurately than other methods, but their scales are arbitrary.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

On the scales used in the studies cited here, a lower number means a darker roast: lower L\*,<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> lower Agtron value,<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> lower Colortest II reading.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Colour tiles.** The SCA also refers to the Agtron/SCA Roast Color Classification System, a set of numbered reference discs (tiles).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A Colombian study describes the Agtron/SCA chart as a roast-development pattern based on the caramelisation of sugar, and used it to compare samples by eye during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Scales and why they do not convert

The SCA’s cupping protocol gives one target roast and states it in several ways. It defines “cupping level roast” as ground-coffee L\* 26–29, approximately tile #65 on the Agtron/SCA classification, or a reading of 63 on the Agtron “Gourmet” scale or its equivalent on other meters.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> For the same target, the SCA lists 48 on the Agtron “Commercial” scale, 96 on the Probat Colorette 3b and 62 on the Colortrack.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Many other meters have adopted the Agtron “Gourmet” scale and should use the same target.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The L\* value is defined on ground coffee; for the meter targets the SCA does not state the sample form, but asks for grind level and coffee temperature to be controlled.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

These numbers are targets for one roast, not a conversion formula. The SCA notes that roast-meter scales are arbitrary, and that even meters using the same scale do not always correlate completely across brands.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The SCA itself is researching how its roast-level parameters correlate, and expects more accurate guidelines.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

L\* does not map onto meter numbers in a fixed way either. The SCA pairs L\* 26–29 with tile #65;<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> a Colombian study, roasting to 85, 55 and 35 on the Agtron/SCA scale, states that these correspond to L\* 22.0, 18.5 and below 18.5, citing a Colombian roast-degree standard (ICONTEC 2442) and without saying whether the L\* refers to whole beans or ground coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> L\* itself depends on the sample: in one study, the same Arabica roasts read up to 4.6 L\* units apart as whole beans and as ground coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Relations between colour and other indices also depend on the coffee. In one study, chemical aroma indices tracked colour with r² of 0.88–0.89 in Arabica but 0.96–0.99 in Robusta and a 50:50 blend.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Light, medium, dark: names are not standards

Baristas often use names – “city roast”, “espresso roast”, “French roast” – for degrees of roast, while industrial operators and scientists prefer to measure.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The examples below show how loosely the names are tied to numbers.

<figcaption>What published studies and SCA documents called dark, medium and light, against Agtron-scale numbers; one value (Agtron 76) carries all three names. Tile numbers and meter readings are close but not identical scales, so the axis is approximate and is not a conversion.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup></figcaption>

- **One number, three names.** A development-time study roasted to Agtron 76 ± 1 and called it a medium roast. The authors add that this degree counts as a light roast in commodity roasting and as a dark roast in specialty roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Specialty runs lighter.** A multi-study paper covered Agtron “Gourmet” 46–99 (mean 79) and notes that very light roasts, often up to Agtron 120, are commonplace in specialty coffee; its range would be among the lightest in the commodity industry and among the darkest in specialty.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Same name, different numbers.** In a defect study, the “light” roast measured 116.6 and the “dark” 45.7, around a normal roast of 74.4.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A Colombian study, matching samples by eye to the Agtron/SCA chart, called 85 light, 55 medium and 35 dark.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The SCA uses dark-roasted coffee of about Agtron #35 as its reference for bitter taste.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Whole beans and ground coffee

A roast does not colour a bean evenly: a temperature gradient across the bean leaves the outside and the inside at different colours.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Very fast roasting can over-roast the zone near the surface while the core stays under-roasted.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Extremely fast roasts are traditionally treated as defects for this reason – a more pronounced gradient of roast degree from the surface to the centre.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The SCA describes the same sign in sample roasts that are too fast: the surface looks much darker than the inside.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

A meter on whole beans therefore sees mostly the outside, while ground coffee mixes outside and inside. In a study of Arabica and Robusta at three roast intensities, whole beans had lower L\* (were darker) than the same beans ground. The gap shrank as the roast got darker, which the authors suggest may come from the temperature gradient across the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

A coffee-review publication that reports a whole-bean and a ground reading for each coffee makes the same observation: the difference narrows in darker roasts and widens in lighter ones.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Standards measure ground coffee: the SCA cupping roast is defined by the lightness of ground coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Measuring well

A colour reading depends on how the sample is prepared. The SCA asks for grind level and coffee temperature to be controlled when roast meters are used, and warns that samples with a lot of chaff show a larger deviation.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> For the most reliable results, beans are ground and prepared in a standard way before measuring.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Grind.** Research protocols state the grind, for example “finely ground” coffee measured in triplicate.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Replicates.** Studies average several readings per sample, often three.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Instrument and settings.** One study measured under two illuminants (D65 and C) and found that L\* and hue followed similar patterns under both.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Sources using different instruments and preparations give different L\* values for roasts described the same way (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Visual checks.** Judging colour by eye is still used.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> During roasting, one research group used a colour reference tool stated to be accurate to ±1 Agtron value, and a Colombian study compared samples with the Agtron/SCA chart.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

No study consulted for this entry measured how readings change with the time since roasting, so no rule is given here.

## The colour coordinates in roasting

During roasting, L\* falls with roast intensity, in whole beans and in ground coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The other coordinates behave less simply. In whole beans, a\* (red) rose in the first roast stage and then fell, and b\* (yellow) fell throughout. After grinding, only L\* and hue fell steadily; a\* in the two darker roasts stayed well above its value in green coffee, unlike in whole beans.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Species can matter. At the same roast intensity, whole-bean colour parameters of Arabica and Robusta did not differ significantly in one study; ground green coffees of the two species did differ in several parameters.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Colour, roast loss and chemistry

Colour is not the only index of roast degree.

- **Roast loss.** It rises steadily during roasting and is higher in dark roasts. As long as green-coffee quality and moisture stay constant, it can indicate roast degree, but batches roasted to the same colour can differ in roast loss when the green moisture varies.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Organic roast loss.** Calculated on dry matter, it takes the varying water loss into account. It correlates well with the lightness from colour measurement.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Dry-matter loss is regarded as a reliable complement to colour, including in the plant.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The SCA allows dry-matter loss or bean volume increase for cupping roasts once their correlation with colour or spectrometry has been established.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Chemical markers.** Roast degree can be followed through changes in chemical composition.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Chlorogenic acids fall as roasting intensifies: in one study, to nearly half at 230 °C for 12 min and to almost trace levels at 250 °C for 21 min.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Sucrose and free amino acids react early; arabinose side chains and chlorogenic acids are degraded later.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Earlier studies, summarised in a development-time paper, found sucrose degraded by up to 99%, depending on the roast profile.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Aroma fingerprints.** A headspace mass-spectrometry method separated roast degrees in 80 samples of different varieties, origins and blends; ratios of diagnostic ions tracked colour, and the authors propose them for on-line monitoring.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Colour as a sufficient indicator

Colour is a strong predictor of flavour. A 2020 paper pooled eight descriptive sensory studies of washed Arabica. In the two that varied both, colour predicted flavour better than roasting time.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors note that colour measurement is often left out of quality control in specialty roasteries, which could leave consumer-relevant batch-to-batch variation unchecked.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Colour is not the whole story. At one fixed colour (Agtron 76 ± 1), changing only the development time shifted both the chemistry and the sensory profile; the authors conclude that colour alone is not a sufficient indicator, and recommend logging colour and development time together.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A chemistry chapter likewise holds that roast loss and colour cannot describe the aroma profile on their own.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The SCA adds that roasting problems such as too-fast or baked roasts have no simple measurement across roasting technologies; cuppers learn to detect them by bean structure, colour gradient and flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Decaffeinated coffee

Decaffeination changes the beans’ structure and composition, so roasting must be adapted. A suitable target colour can differ from that of regular beans, and mass loss is sometimes the better control parameter.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Decaffeinated green beans often look dull, yellowish or even brownish before roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Conversions.** No published, general conversion links L\*, Agtron and other meter scales; the SCA is researching the correlations.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Natural and other processes.** No source consulted for this entry tested whether natural or other processed coffees read differently at the same roast degree. The main sensory evidence linking colour and flavour comes from washed Arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Time after roasting.** How a reading changes in the hours and days after roasting was not addressed in the sources consulted.
- **Light roasts.** The multi-study evidence stops near Agtron 99, while specialty roasts often go lighter.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
