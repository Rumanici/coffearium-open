brief:
  - Each treatment moves water in its own direction – a softener lowers hardness only, a decarboniser lowers hardness and alkalinity equally, reverse osmosis removes almost everything.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
  - Activated carbon removes chlorine, chloramines and organic off-flavours; chlorine left in brewing water can react with coffee phenols to form medicinal-tasting chlorophenols.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
  - A softener swaps calcium and magnesium for sodium, so alkalinity stays and the sodium content rises.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
  - For espresso machines, the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) advises a decarboniser bypass giving 40–70 ppm alkalinity, or, above 250 ppm input, removing at most 200 ppm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
  - Carbon filters and other point-of-use devices can host bacteria, so replacement and sanitising are part of any treatment.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Why water is treated

Tap water is made safe to drink, not to brew coffee. Treatment for coffee has three aims: to remove off-flavours, to protect machines and to adjust the minerals for flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Off-flavours.** Waterworks disinfect water with chlorine or chloramines, and both leave a strongly unpleasant flavour in the brew.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Chlorine can also react with phenols from the coffee to form chlorophenols, which taste medicinal.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In drinking water, chlorophenols are noticed at extremely low levels.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Iron, earthy and mouldy notes are other taints that treatment may have to remove.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Scale.** When hard water is heated, calcium carbonate settles out as scale.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Scale slows heat transfer and clogs valves and jets, especially where water is hottest.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A review of espresso water calls treatment necessary for both reasons, taints and scale.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Corrosion.** Water that is too soft or poorly buffered attacks metal instead.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The SCA links corrosion to alkalinity below 40 ppm as CaCO₃, pH below 6 or above 8, and much chloride, sulfate or nitrate.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Treatment must therefore not go too far. The water entry sets out the corrosion rules in detail.

**Flavour.** Alkalinity, the water’s capacity to neutralise acid, decides most of how water changes the cup; hardness matters less.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the SCA handbook, most tap waters in central Europe and the US carry more hardness and alkalinity than suits coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Hardness and alkalinity, and how they shape flavour, have their own entries.

## What each treatment does

A treatment either removes something, swaps one ion for another, or adds minerals. Each one moves the water in its own direction on a chart of alkalinity against total hardness.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

| Method | How it works | Total hardness | Alkalinity | Also changes | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Particle (sediment) filter | Traps sand, rust and other particles; filters can run in series, coarse to fine | – | – | Very fine filters clog sooner | SCAA<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> |
| Activated carbon | Adsorbs chlorine, chloramines and organic matter | – | – | Removes the chlorine that kept bacteria in check | SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>, SCAA<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> |
| Softener | Swaps Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ for Na⁺ or K⁺ | Down | Unchanged | Adds sodium or potassium | SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>, WHO<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> |
| Decarboniser (weak-acid cation exchanger) | Swaps Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ for H⁺; H⁺ turns bicarbonate into carbonic acid | Down | Down by the same amount | Dissolved CO₂ up, pH down | SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>, patent<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> |
| Buffered decarboniser | Mostly decarboniser resin with a little softener resin | Down | Down, but not to zero | As above, less strongly | SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Reverse osmosis | A membrane passes water and holds back almost all dissolved substances | Towards zero | Towards zero | Waste water; aggressive if not remineralised | SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>, SCAA<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> |
| Deioniser | Cation plus anion exchanger removes all ions | Towards zero | Towards zero | Low capacity | SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Dealkaliser | Anion exchanger or a strong acid | Unchanged | Down | Chloride up (hydrochloric acid or chloride-form resin) | SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>, textbook<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> |
| Hardener | Adds calcium or magnesium as sulfate or chloride | Up | Unchanged | Sulfate or chloride up | SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Alkaliser | Dissolves sodium or potassium hydrogen carbonate | Unchanged | Up | Sodium or potassium up | SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Carboniser | Dissolves magnesium carbonate | Up | Up by the same amount | – | SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |

## Choosing a treatment

The starting water decides the method. The SCA handbook gives these rules:<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Limestone water** – alkalinity equal to hardness or up to about 70 ppm below it: a decarboniser suits most uses.
- **Gypsum water** (alkalinity more than about 70 ppm below hardness) and **salt water**: reverse osmosis is the only reliable method. The same applies to water with iron, manganese or much sodium.
- **Small volumes at home**: dilute tap water with almost pure water, such as reverse-osmosis water or water below 20 ppm hardness and alkalinity.
- **No single method reaches the target**: combine a decarboniser with a hardener, or demineralise fully and then add minerals back.

Every water that passes a decarboniser or dealkaliser should keep at least 40 ppm alkalinity, to avoid corrosion.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Espresso tolerates more alkalinity than filter coffee, up to about 150 ppm, because it uses far less water per gram of coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The water entry explains why.

**Espresso machines and kettles differ.** In a closed line to an espresso machine, the carbon dioxide formed by a decarboniser cannot escape.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The SCA therefore advises setting the bypass so that output alkalinity is 40–70 ppm for input waters up to 250 ppm alkalinity. Above 250 ppm, alkalinity should fall by no more than 200 ppm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In an open kettle the carbon dioxide escapes on heating, and decarbonisation can be used without these limits.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Taints come first.** Activated carbon removes chlorine compounds and organic matter efficiently.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Low levels of iron can be removed by a softener or decarboniser; higher levels need aeration and filtration of the iron particles that form.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Of the methods in the SCA handbook, only reverse osmosis and deionisation fully remove iron and lead.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**In-line or batch.** In-line systems connect the tap to the machine and run without attention. Batch systems treat water in a tank; an espresso machine may then need an extra pump.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> As of 2018, dosing acid or sodium hydrogen carbonate was not sold as an in-line cartridge.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Keeping treatment working

Every cartridge and membrane has a limited capacity. The SCA notes that makers of treatment products often publish tables of capacity by starting water and volume.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> An estimate of monthly or yearly water use then tells when the next change is due.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Checking the output.** Tap water can vary by day and season, especially from several sources, shallow groundwater, rivers or lakes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A conductivity meter can track a decarboniser or an RO system with bypass, because their output conductivity changes with treatment.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A softener barely changes conductivity, so the meter cannot track it reliably.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The measuring entry covers the methods. For RO, the SCAA handbook recommends monitoring the water pressure to make sure the membranes are not clogged.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Hygiene.** Activated carbon removes the chlorine that kept bacteria in check, so bacteria can build up inside the filter.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2004 study found more bacteria in water from household tap filters than in unfiltered tap water.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A 1995 study found raised bacterial counts after water stood overnight in carbon filters, but lower counts than the incoming water after a short flush.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Bacteria have also been found in reverse-osmosis permeate, and they can multiply in the lines downstream.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to the SCAA handbook, carbon filters must be maintained or replaced regularly.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> After a boil-water alert, the handbook advises sanitising the lines behind the filter, because the filter removes the extra chlorine the waterworks adds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Ion exchange chemistry

Ion exchangers are resin beads that hold one kind of ion and release another. Softeners and decarbonisers both take up calcium and magnesium; they differ in what they give back.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Softener.** The resin exchanges calcium and magnesium for sodium or potassium.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Calcium and magnesium carry two charges and sodium one, so two sodium (or potassium) ions are released for each calcium or magnesium ion taken up (charge balance; our own addition):

2 R–Na + Ca²⁺ → R₂–Ca + 2 Na⁺

Bicarbonate is untouched, so alkalinity does not change.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Water from limestone keeps its full buffer, now paired with sodium. Sodium rises in step: one Swiss federal guidance names ion exchange specifically as a cause of significantly higher sodium,<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> and the World Health Organization (WHO) notes that some softeners add significantly to it.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Our own calculation: each 1 ppm of hardness removed (as CaCO₃) adds 0.46 mg/L of sodium, so softening 200 ppm adds about 92 mg/L. The WHO sets no health-based value for sodium but notes that more than 200 mg/L may give an unacceptable taste.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Decarboniser.** A weak-acid cation resin in hydrogen form releases hydrogen ions instead:<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

2 R–H + Ca²⁺ → R₂–Ca + 2 H⁺  
H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ ⇌ CO₂ + H₂O

The resin removes hardness only to the extent that it is matched by alkalinity; in one patented process a second, sodium-form column then softens the rest.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Hardness and alkalinity therefore fall by equal amounts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The carbonic acid breaks down into carbon dioxide and water.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In the patented process, exhausted resin is regenerated with a mineral acid, preferably hydrochloric acid.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A buffered decarboniser mixes in a small share of softener resin, so that some alkalinity remains.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**The bypass as a mass balance.** If the resin removes all the alkalinity it treats, output alkalinity equals the bypassed share times input alkalinity (our calculation). Hardness that is not matched by alkalinity passes the resin unchanged.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> For the SCA example of 150 ppm alkalinity and 200 ppm hardness, a 40% bypass gives 0.4 × 150 = 60 ppm alkalinity. Total hardness becomes 0.4 × 200 + 0.6 × 50 = 110 ppm (our calculation). Real cartridges, especially buffered ones, leave some alkalinity in the treated stream, so the setting differs in practice.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Carbon dioxide from decarbonisation.** Each hydrogen carbonate ion neutralised becomes one molecule of carbonic acid, which is in exchange with dissolved CO₂.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Our own calculation: 1 ppm of alkalinity removed (as CaCO₃) is 0.020 mmol/L of hydrogen carbonate and forms 0.88 mg/L of CO₂. Removing 90 ppm, as in the example above, forms about 79 mg/L; removing 200 ppm forms 176 mg/L; removing 250 ppm forms 220 mg/L. In a closed line this CO₂ lowers the pH and raises the corrosion risk.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the SCA handbook and a textbook chapter, it also adds to the CO₂ from fresh coffee and can give espresso a foamy crema with large, short-lived bubbles.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The chapter names degassing, or a demineraliser that forms no extra CO₂, as remedies.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Dealkalisers.** An anion exchanger can swap hydrogen carbonate for chloride; the textbook chapter described it in 2017 as not yet commercially available for coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Adding a strong acid works the same way on alkalinity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Both raise chloride, which the SCA links to corrosion in espresso machines.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Membranes, blending and remineralisation

**Reverse osmosis.** Pressure drives water through a semi-permeable membrane; dissolved minerals pass far less readily and are flushed to waste with part of the water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the SCAA handbook, this wastes considerable water and makes RO the most expensive treatment it considers.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A deioniser reaches a similar result by ion exchange, but the SCA finds its capacity too low for high-volume commercial use.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Why pure water is not the end point.** Desalinated water is low in minerals, poorly buffered and usually aggressive to metals and cement.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Water supplies therefore remineralise it, often with calcium carbonate, to reduce its corrosive potential.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The SCAA handbook warns that RO water can corrode metal pipes unless the system is engineered and installed properly, and rules it out with galvanised pipes or lead-soldered copper.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It adds that water with almost no minerals may not be detected by the level probes in many brewers.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Blending.** Mixing RO water with tap water gives any composition on the line between the tap water and zero.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The share of water 1 for a target alkalinity is (alkalinity of water 2 − target) ÷ (alkalinity of water 2 − alkalinity of water 1); the same formula works for hardness.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The water entry corrects a slip in the handbook’s worked example.

**Using the concentrate.** Some RO systems can deliver the concentrate instead of the permeate, raising hardness and alkalinity without changing their ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Our own calculation, assuming the membrane holds back all minerals: the concentration factor is the feed volume divided by the concentrate volume, so a system that sends half the feed to concentrate doubles the minerals. The SCA restricts this use to low-mineral source water that is otherwise of high quality, free of metals and chlorine.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Making water from salts

Recipe waters start from almost pure water and add salts. The SCA handbook gives stock concentrates; we checked each with molar masses.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

| Concentrate | Salt per 1 L of RO water | Strength stated | Dose stated | Our check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alkalising | 3 g sodium hydrogen carbonate | 100 °dH alkalinity | 10 g per L adds 1 °dH | 35.7 mmol/L HCO₃⁻ = 100 °dH |
| Magnesium hardening | 7.3 g MgCl₂·6H₂O | 200 °dH hardness | 10 g per L adds 2 °dH | 35.9 mmol/L Mg²⁺ ≈ 201 °dH |
| Calcium hardening | 4.0 g CaCl₂ | 200 °dH hardness | not stated | 36.0 mmol/L Ca²⁺ ≈ 202 °dH (anhydrous salt) |
| Dealkalising (not advised without training) | 3 g hydrochloric acid, 37% | −100 °dH alkalinity | 10 g per L removes 1 °dH | 30.4 mmol/L H⁺ ≈ −85 °dH |

Sources: SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> for the recipes; checks are our own calculation (1 °dH = 0.178 mmol/L Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺, or 0.357 mmol/L HCO₃⁻; molar masses NaHCO₃ 84.01, MgCl₂·6H₂O 203.30, CaCl₂ 110.98, HCl 36.46 g/mol). Three of the four recipes agree with their stated strength. By our arithmetic the acid recipe is about −85 °dH rather than −100 °dH, so 10 g of it removes about 0.85 °dH per litre, not 1 °dH. The SCA advises against handling concentrated acid without training and protective equipment.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Sulfate or chloride.** For espresso machines the SCA advises calcium or magnesium sulfate rather than chlorides, because chlorides increase the chance of corrosion.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Baking soda sold for cooking often contains other ingredients, so the SCA asks for pure sodium hydrogen carbonate.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Three waters.** Mixing three waters lets hardness and alkalinity be set independently.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the SCA example, an almost pure water (10|10), a hard mineral water (300|320) and an Epsom salt concentrate (10|450) are mixed.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For a target of 40 ppm alkalinity and 55 ppm hardness, the handbook gives 867.2 g, 103.4 g and 29.4 g per litre. For 60|80 it gives 790.0 g, 172.4 g and 37.6 g.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Our check of the second: 0.790 × 10 + 0.172 × 300 + 0.038 × 10 = 60 ppm alkalinity, and 0.790 × 10 + 0.172 × 320 + 0.038 × 450 = 80 ppm hardness.

How individual minerals change flavour is covered in the entry on water minerals and flavour.

## Scale chemistry and scale inhibitors

**Why heat makes scale.** Calcite, the common form of calcium carbonate, dissolves only slightly; its solubility product at 25 °C has a logarithm of −8.480.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Tap water holds calcium and hydrogen carbonate in solution because dissolved CO₂ keeps the equilibrium on the dissolved side.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Heating drives CO₂ out, because gases dissolve less in hot water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Our own rough estimate from Henry’s law (constant 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ mol/(m³·Pa) at 25 °C, temperature coefficient 2300 K):<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> at 95 °C, CO₂ is only about one fifth as soluble as at 20 °C. As CO₂ leaves, calcium carbonate precipitates.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> At most the lower of hardness and alkalinity can leave as scale, which is why both treatments that lower either one reduce scale.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Scale inhibitors.** Polyphosphates such as hexametaphosphate bind a limited amount of hardness and keep it from forming scale.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the SCAA handbook, they help little in hard water, work less well at high pH and do nothing against silica.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> They do not lower hardness or alkalinity, so they do not change the water for flavour (our inference from the mechanism described).

**Magnetic devices.** According to the SCAA handbook, a review by a water-treatment trade association found too little study to certify claims for magnetic scale prevention. The handbook argues that any altered crystal form would not survive the rapid heating in a brewer.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Rules for treatment devices

Treatment devices touch drinking water, so drinking-water law applies to them. These are rules, cited as rules; they say nothing about coffee quality.

- **European Union.** Directive (EU) 2020/2184 sets requirements for materials in contact with drinking water and, separately, for water treatment chemicals and filter media. A 2024 implementing decision states that membranes and ion-exchange resins fall under the article on treatment chemicals and filter media.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Switzerland.** A 2010 guidance from the Federal Office of Public Health lists recognised treatment processes for drinking water and notes that ion exchange can raise sodium significantly.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The SVGW, the Swiss water industry association, recommends keeping a minimum residual hardness when drinking water is softened.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>
- **Taste limits.** Australian drinking-water guidelines set aesthetic limits for chlorophenols at their taste thresholds, which lie below their health-based values.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

No European standard for softeners or point-of-use filters was consulted for this entry.

## Open questions

- **Calcium-for-magnesium exchange.** Cartridges that swap calcium for magnesium change neither hardness nor alkalinity. Calcium and magnesium may affect extraction differently, but the SCA handbook calls this an area of recent research.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No sensory study of such cartridges was found for this entry.
- **Hygiene of café cartridges.** The microbiology studies consulted here tested household devices on drinking water.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured bacteria in coffee-machine filter cartridges.
- **Decarbonised water and machine life.** The SCA limits rest on corrosion indices and calculation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No long-term field study of corrosion in espresso machines run on decarbonised water was found.
- **Salt choice and flavour.** Sulfate is advised over chloride for machine safety.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Whether the anion changes cup flavour at recipe-water levels was not tested in any study consulted for this entry.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Snyder et al., 1995 – Carbon filters on rural groundwater.</strong> Monitored point-of-use powdered activated carbon filters on 24 rural groundwater supplies, sampling raw, filtered and overnight-standing water. Heterotrophic bacteria rose after stagnation in the filters but fell below incoming levels after a brief flush, and coliforms were reduced; the authors judged that, apart from periods after stagnation, the filters did not compromise water quality.</p>
<p class="meta">Applied and Environmental Microbiology 61(12), 4291–4295 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1128/aem.61.12.4291-4295.1995">doi:10.1128/aem.61.12.4291-4295.1995</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Chaidez & Gerba, 2004 – Tap filters and bacteria.</strong> Compared water from household carbon filters with tap water from the same and from unfiltered taps. Filtered water carried the highest bacterial counts, which the authors suggest may come from biofilm growth in the devices; they estimated a low risk of colonisation by the opportunistic bacteria found.</p>
<p class="meta">International Journal of Environmental Health Research 14(4), 253–260 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/09603120410001725595">doi:10.1080/09603120410001725595</a></p>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Navarini & Rivetti, 2010 – Water quality for espresso.</strong> Reviews how water and coffee components interact in espresso brewing. It starts from the two accepted reasons for treatment, disinfection taints and machine scale, and argues that the direct effect of water composition on the cup deserves more attention.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry 122(2), 424–428 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2009.04.019">doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2009.04.019</a></p>
