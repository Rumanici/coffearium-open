## A wild coffee from East Africa

*Coffea eugenioides* is one of the wild species of the coffee genus, *Coffea*. It is a shrub or tree of the wet tropics.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Kew’s Plants of the World Online gives its native range as South Sudan to east-central and eastern tropical Africa.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The countries listed are the Democratic Republic of Congo, Kenya, Rwanda, South Sudan, Tanzania and Uganda.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

It is a plant of the highlands. The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) describes it as indigenous to the highlands of East Africa.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 1984 paper on wild coffees of Kenya lists it, in western Kenya, among the species of altitudes above 1,000 m.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> A coffee science textbook chapter counts it among the species with a wide altitudinal distribution and places it in humid habitats.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Its range is smaller than that of *C. canephora*, the species grown as robusta. A 2024 genome study calls eugenioides more range-limited, while *C. canephora* has one of the widest ranges in the genus.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The IUCN Red List (International Union for Conservation of Nature) lists *C. eugenioides* as Least Concern.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> How it compares with the other wild species is shown in the species index in [The coffee genus](/entries/coffea-genus/).

## Arabica’s maternal parent

*C. arabica* arose when *C. eugenioides* and *C. canephora* crossed in the wild.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Each parent is diploid: it carries two sets of 11 chromosomes, 22 in all.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Arabica kept the sets of both parents and has 44 chromosomes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The two parents did not play the same part. Besides the nucleus, a plant cell holds chloroplasts, a kind of plastid with DNA of its own (chloroplast DNA).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the coffee crosses studied, chloroplast DNA passed only through the mother.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Arabica’s plastid genome derives from *C. eugenioides*.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Put together, the two findings make eugenioides the maternal parent of arabica and canephora the paternal one; the 2024 study itself states only where the plastid comes from.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The parents were not today’s plants but their ancestors.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A textbook chapter describes them as two ecotypes related to *C. eugenioides* and *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The 2024 study compares arabica with modern representatives of these diploid progenitors.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How old the hybrid is and how its genome works is the subject of [Polyploidy and the arabica genome](/entries/polyploidy-and-the-arabica-genome/).

## Low in caffeine

The seeds of *C. eugenioides* hold little caffeine. The SCA states that it has less caffeine than arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 2005 study measured four accessions from Kenya and found 0.44–0.60% caffeine in the dry matter of green seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Four accessions of *C. canephora* in the same study held 1.51–3.33%.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> By mean, eugenioides held about a fifth of the caffeine of canephora (0.51% against 2.64%, our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Evidence for the parentage

A 1999 study compared the DNA of arabica with that of possible diploid parents, using molecular markers (restriction fragment length polymorphism, RFLP). It concluded that *C. arabica* is an amphidiploid formed by hybridisation between *C. eugenioides* and *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Genomic in situ hybridisation (GISH), which labels each parent’s chromosomes, confirmed the genome organisation.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The same study found low divergence between the two constituent genomes and the parent species, and read this as a sign of a relatively recent speciation.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The maternal side rests on the plastid. A 1996 study of chloroplast DNA in the genus found exclusively maternal inheritance in interspecific hybrids between arabica and canephora and in a progeny within canephora.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The 2024 genome study states that the arabica plastid genome is derived from eugenioides.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Taken together, the two findings imply that eugenioides was the seed parent of the founding cross; neither study states this in those words.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Where the cross happened is open. The 1999 study held that a precise site in Central Africa is hard to fix, because the extent of tropical forest has varied considerably.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The 2024 study does not name a site either. Citing earlier work, it notes that the canephora-derived subgenome is most closely related to *C. canephora* accessions from northern Uganda.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## The eugenioides genome

The 2024 study assembled the genome of a wild eugenioides accession, Bu-A, alongside those of canephora and arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The assemblies span 645 Mb for eugenioides, 672 Mb for canephora and 1,088 Mb for arabica, as given in the text; the study’s Table 1 lists a total length of 661 Mb and a projected genome size of 682 Mb for eugenioides.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Transposable elements make up 59.7% of the eugenioides genome against 67.5% in canephora, and canephora holds more recent LTR (long terminal repeat) insertions.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The two diploid genomes show high structural conservation.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Inside arabica the two subgenomes are named subEE and subCC.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The study found no significant global expression dominance of either subgenome.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It did find a bias in homoeologous exchanges, where one subgenome’s segment replaces the other’s, in favour of subCC.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors suggest this may be adaptive, since canephora has a far wider range than eugenioides.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> At one end of chromosome 7, rich in genes with chloroplast-associated functions, all accessions shared a fixed bias towards subEE. The authors link it to compatibility between nuclear and plastid genes, because the plastid comes from eugenioides.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Later breeding left subEE largely untouched. Introgression from the Timor Hybrid, the natural arabica × robusta cross used for rust resistance, occurred almost exclusively within subCC; subEE has received no substantial introgression.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> That story is told in [The Timor Hybrid](/entries/the-timor-hybrid/), and the narrow diversity of arabica in [The arabica bottleneck](/entries/the-arabica-bottleneck/).

## Seed chemistry

The 2005 caffeine study sorted wild *Coffea* species into four groups, separated by discontinuities in caffeine content.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Group means rose about twofold from one group to the next: 0.55%, 1.1% and 2.3% on a dry matter basis (dmb), with a fourth group holding only traces.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A companion study placed *C. eugenioides* in the second group, together with one undescribed taxon, *C.* sp. Moloundou.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The same companion study measured chlorogenic acids (CGA). Eugenioides held 5.17% (dmb), against 11.34% in canephora.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Across 21 taxa, caffeine and CGA contents were qualitatively related: species low in one tended to be low in the other.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Both compounds are treated in [Caffeine](/entries/caffeine/) and [Chlorogenic acids](/entries/chlorogenic-acids/).

## Open questions

**Place of the founding cross.** Neither the 1999 nor the 2024 study could locate where eugenioides and canephora met.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Caffeine across the whole range.** The caffeine figures consulted for this entry rest on four accessions from Kenya.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured eugenioides from the other countries of its range.

**Eugenioides as a crop.** No study consulted for this entry measured yield, agronomy or cup quality of cultivated *C. eugenioides*.
