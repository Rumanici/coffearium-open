## What decaffeination is

Decaffeination removes most of the caffeine from coffee. It is done on green coffee, before roasting. According to a textbook chapter, aroma forms mainly during roasting, which comes afterwards.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A decaffeination plant therefore sits between the green coffee trade and the roaster. Several plants also steam green coffee or give it other treatments that do not aim to remove caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Decaf is not caffeine-free. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) says decaf coffee typically has 2–15 mg in an 8-fluid-ounce cup (about 240 mL).<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> How much caffeine regular coffee holds, and what it does in the body, is covered in *Caffeine*.

## Why the beans are moistened first

Early attempts to extract caffeine from dry beans with organic solvents failed, according to a textbook chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The working method came when the beans were first wetted and swollen with water and steam. The same chapter states that the added water frees caffeine from a structure it forms with chlorogenic acids.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Wetting also opens the bean, so the solvent can reach the caffeine more easily.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In one industrial carbon dioxide plant, steam raised the moisture of the beans to 30% by weight (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> After extraction, the water has to be dried out again – a costly step.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> So every industrial process follows the same three steps: moisten, extract, dry.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Three families of methods

According to a 2017 textbook chapter, four extraction solvents are in industrial use: dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, water and carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Carbon dioxide is used either as a supercritical fluid or as a liquid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In each case a solvent loop carries the caffeine away from the beans and is cleaned for reuse.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Organic solvents.** Dichloromethane (also called methylene chloride) and ethyl acetate are the two in common use.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A textbook chapter notes that dichloromethane is flame resistant but falls under strict emission and health-protection laws. Ethyl acetate is not chlorinated but is flammable.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Water.** Hot water dissolves caffeine well, but it also takes sugars and other aroma precursors.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The process either gives these back to the beans or prevents their loss by using water already saturated with them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Carbon dioxide.** Above about 31 °C and 7.4 MPa (74 bar), carbon dioxide becomes a supercritical fluid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It is readily available and non-flammable, but the plant needs costly high-pressure equipment.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## What the law calls decaf

There is no single worldwide definition of “decaffeinated”.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the EU, only coffee extracts (instant coffee) have one common rule; beans fall under national law.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

| Jurisdiction | Product | Limit | Legal basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| European Union | Coffee extracts labelled “decaffeinated” | Anhydrous caffeine ≤ 0.3% of coffee-based dry matter | Directive 1999/4/EC<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> |
| Germany | Green and roasted coffee labelled “entkoffeiniert” | ≤ 1 g caffeine per kg coffee dry matter (0.1%) | KaffeeV<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> |

| United States | Labelling of decaf coffee | No caffeine limit found in FDA regulations consulted for this entry; FDA gives 2–15 mg per 8 fl oz cup as typical | FDA<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> |
| European Union | Dichloromethane residue | ≤ 2 mg/kg in roasted coffee | Directive 2009/32/EC<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> |

The US specification is a purchasing description for coffee bought by federal, state and local governments and other interested parties.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No caffeine limit for labelling decaf was found in the FDA regulations consulted for this entry. A percentage of caffeine “removed” is quoted for US decaf; according to a textbook chapter, it is a threshold derived from average caffeine contents, not a formal regulation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Roasting decaffeinated coffee

Decaffeinated green coffee often looks dull, yellowish or even brownish rather than green, according to a textbook chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Some treated beans come close to the look of roasted beans, which makes control by eye harder.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same chapter says roasting must be adapted. The target colour may differ, and roast loss can be the better end-point.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In a 2023 study, commercial decaffeinated beans from Cerrado, Minas Gerais, Brazil, were charged at 170 °C and roasted for 8 min; the regular beans were roasted for 10 min.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The decaf beans still ended darker: Agtron 49.20 (water process) and 47.67 (CO₂), against 55.38 for the regular coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Decaffeinated beans are also more brittle. They need more care in fluid-bed roasters, and industrial roller mills need adapted gap settings.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Organic solvents in detail

According to a textbook chapter, the solvent must hardly mix with water, so that it takes caffeine but leaves the other water-soluble components in the moist bean.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Diffusion of caffeine inside the bean is slow and controls the rate, so intense agitation is not needed. Its limited capacity calls for repeated extractions or countercurrent vessel sequences.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Steam then strips the remaining solvent, and the beans are dried with air, often in a sequence of dryers; low air temperatures and the exclusion of oxygen help to limit aroma loss.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The recovered caffeine is refined and sold to the pharmaceutical and beverage industries; the waxy remainder is used as a binder or fuel.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Residue limits.** In the EU, dichloromethane may be used to decaffeinate coffee and tea, with a maximum residue of 2 mg/kg in roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Ethyl acetate is on the EU list of extraction solvents allowed for all uses under good manufacturing practice, with carbon dioxide, ethanol and acetone among others.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In the US, methylene chloride residues may not exceed 10 ppm (0.001%) in decaffeinated roasted coffee and decaffeinated instant coffee<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>; ethyl acetate may be used under current good manufacturing practice.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Status as of October 2026.** The latest consolidated version of the EU directive dates from 16 February 2023; the amendment behind it concerns another solvent, 2-methyloxolane.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> No change to the dichloromethane entry for coffee was found in the EU sources consulted for this entry. In the US, the FDA announced in January 2024 that it had filed a food additive petition from the Environmental Defense Fund and others. The petition asks the FDA to remove four solvents, methylene chloride among them, from its food additive regulations.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In May 2026 the FDA reopened the comment period until 29 June 2026 (docket FDA-2023-F-5684).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No FDA decision was found as of 14 October 2026; the eCFR text, up to date as of 8 October 2026, still sets the 10 ppm limit for decaffeinated coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> How caffeine and decaf relate to health is covered in *Caffeine*.

## Water processes in detail

Caffeine dissolves much better in hot water than in cold, so extraction runs with hot water at atmospheric pressure.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The difficulty is selectivity: sugars and other aroma precursors are extracted too.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A textbook chapter describes two answers.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Fresh water and add-back.** The extract passes a bed of activated carbon chosen to hold caffeine. The nearly caffeine-free extract is concentrated and added back to the beans, which must first be partly dried to absorb it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Preloaded water.** Water from earlier batches is free of caffeine but saturated with the other green-coffee solubles, so it takes little else from new beans. The caffeine is removed from it either by an organic solvent in liquid–liquid extraction or by an adsorbent.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Carbons made more selective, or preloaded with sucrose or glucose to block sites that would otherwise take up similar coffee compounds, are used.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Carbon dioxide in detail

Carbon dioxide reaches its critical point at 304.1 K (about 31 °C) and 7.38 MPa.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A textbook chapter states that supercritical decaffeination runs at about 25 MPa and 100 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2017 life cycle assessment of one industrial plant describes these steps:<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

1. Steaming at 90 °C for 5 h, until the beans hold 30% moisture by weight.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
2. Extraction at 90 °C and 250 bar: 11.5 h for arabica and 22 h for robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
3. Countercurrent washing of the CO₂ with water, which removes 99.5% of the caffeine from the gas; the purified CO₂ returns to storage.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

According to the same chapter, wet CO₂ dissolves more caffeine than dry CO₂, and its solubility falls sharply when the caffeine must leave a water phase full of coffee solubles.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Liquid CO₂ works at lower temperature, which reduces thermal stress, but needs longer processing because caffeine is much less soluble in it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The use of supercritical carbon dioxide to decaffeinate green coffee goes back to a patent by Zosel (see History).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## What else leaves the bean

No decaffeination process removes caffeine alone. A 2006 study analysed arabica and robusta before and after industrial decaffeination with dichloromethane, by direct extraction of the green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Caffeine fell from 0.98 to 0.02 g/100 g (dry basis) in arabica and from 2.15 to 0.07 g/100 g in robusta<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> – by our calculation, 98% and 96.7% of the caffeine removed. At the same time the green coffee lost about 60% of its sucrose (arabica) and 20% (robusta), and 16% and 11% of its chlorogenic acids.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Trigonelline fell by 13% in arabica but rose by 27% in robusta; lipids and total carbohydrates changed less.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

According to a textbook chapter, sugars are aroma precursors.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a 2022 study of eight commercial pairs of regular and decaffeinated coffees, pyrazines were more abundant in the regular coffees; the authors attribute this to the loss of sucrose in decaffeination.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> According to a textbook chapter, mass is lost too, but reliable mass-loss data are rarely recorded and, as of 2017, no comparison between processes had been published.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same chapter states that treated beans generally keep for a shorter time than regular beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Aroma and flavour

The two aroma studies consulted for this entry agree that decaffeination shifts the volatile profile.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

- **Eight commercial pairs (2022).** Headspace analysis of the ground coffees by two-dimensional gas chromatography separated regular from decaf coffees, using 20 discriminating features. Pyrazines were more abundant in regular coffee, and furan-derived compounds marked decaf.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>
- **Brazilian coffees from one region, two processes (2023).** Regular beans were compared with commercial beans from the same region decaffeinated by a water process and by supercritical CO₂. An electronic nose separated the CO₂ decaf from the regular coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The water-processed decaf had the lowest total aroma intensity, which the authors link to the loss of other soluble and aroma components.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The roasts differed in time and colour, so the comparison does not separate decaffeination from roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

No study consulted for this entry ran a trained sensory panel on decaf made by different processes from one green coffee and roasted to the same colour.

## History

Friedlieb Ferdinand Runge discovered caffeine in coffee beans after Johann Wolfgang von Goethe suggested he examine them; a German national biography dates this to 1819<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>, a textbook chapter to 1820.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Both dates are listed under Contested.

Ludwig Roselius, founder of the German company Kaffee HAG, added the step that made decaffeination work: wetting and swelling the beans first, according to a textbook chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Johann Friedrich Meyer Jr., Roselius and Karl Wimmer of Bremen filed the US patent “Treatment of coffee” on 4 May 1906; it was granted on 1 September 1908 as US 897,763.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In the patent, unbroken green beans are extracted under vacuum (with water in its worked example). The caffeine is washed out of the extract with chloroform or benzene, the rest of the extract is returned to the beans, and the beans are dried under vacuum.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The patent text does not describe a separate steaming step.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

Zosel’s patent for decaffeinating green coffee with supercritical carbon dioxide goes back to a German application filed on 5 February 1970 (DE 2005293) by Studiengesellschaft Kohle.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> A textbook chapter dates the patent to 1971, the year of the foreign filings.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> According to a textbook chapter, patenting in decaffeination was intense in the 1980s and early 1990s and had nearly stopped by 2017; process details were then still often kept in-house.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Process comparison on one coffee.** According to a textbook chapter, different green coffees normally go through different industrial processes, so fair flavour comparisons are rare.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry compared all three families on the same green coffee roasted to the same colour.
- **Mass loss.** Reliable data on how much mass each process removes besides caffeine are rarely recorded.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Roasting decaf.** The statement that roast loss is sometimes the better control parameter than colour comes from a textbook chapter<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>; no study consulted for this entry tested it.
- **Dichloromethane in the US.** The reopened comment period on the food additive petition closed on 29 June 2026; no decision was found as of 14 October 2026.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

<p><strong>Toci, Farah & Trugo, 2006 – Dichloromethane decaffeination and green-coffee composition.</strong> Analysed arabica and robusta before and after industrial decaffeination with dichloromethane, green and roasted. Sucrose, protein and trigonelline contents changed markedly, chlorogenic acids changed too, and lipids and total carbohydrates changed less.</p>

Pyrazines marked regular coffee and furan-derived compounds marked decaf; the authors link the pyrazine gap to sucrose lost in decaffeination.</p>
