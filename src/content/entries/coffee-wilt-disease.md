  - It was first observed in 1927 on Coffea excelsa near Bangui, Central African Republic, and destroyed plantations across West and Central Africa by the 1950s.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
  - It re-emerged on robusta in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) in the 1970s and was confirmed in Uganda in 1993.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s21">21</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
  - Two host-specific populations cause the current epidemics, one on robusta in Uganda, Tanzania and the DRC and one on arabica in Ethiopia.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
  - Control rests on uprooting and burning diseased trees and on resistant robusta varieties; Uganda’s national research organisation has released seven.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

## What coffee wilt disease is

Coffee wilt disease (CWD) is a fungal disease that kills whole coffee trees. It is also called tracheomycosis, and it is a vascular wilt.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The fungus, *Fusarium xylarioides*, colonises the xylem, the vessels that carry water from the roots to the leaves.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The fungus enters mainly through the roots and the lower stem, helped by wounds.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Inside, it blocks the uptake of water and breaks down cell walls for food.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The tree wilts and dies.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

As of the EPPO record of March 2024, the disease is present only in Africa.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> It has attacked excelsa and liberica coffee, robusta (*Coffea canephora*) and, in Ethiopia, arabica (*C. arabica*).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> It attacks the whole plant through its water supply.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Signs on the tree

A wilting tree shows signs from the leaves down to the wood and roots.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

- **Leaves.** Wilt and turn inwards, then fall; the branch dies back.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Berries.** Ripen prematurely.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **One branch.** The disease may attack a single branch while the others stay healthy for a while.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Symptoms then spread to the whole plant.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Bark.** The trunk can swell, with vertical or spiralling cracks in the bark.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Perithecia.** Small blackish-brown fruiting bodies of the sexual stage form in the cracks; dead stems can bear them in large numbers.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Wood.** Under the bark the wood shows a blue-black stain, a diagnostic sign according to a 2021 chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Roots and lower stem.** The main entry points, often through wounds.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Young plants can die within weeks of the first signs; mature trees take months.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> In 2003, Ugandan researchers called a therapeutic cure “still unknown”.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## How the fungus spreads

The fungus spreads between trees close together. Neighbouring trees often fall ill, which suggests contact between roots or spread over short distances.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Spread did not appear to be linked to wind direction.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

People move it too. In a trial reported by CABI, trees cut with a machete used on diseased trees developed the disease. Trees cut with a disinfected machete did not.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Infected wood stays infective for a time, and so does soil after diseased trees are removed (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

The fungus has been isolated from banana roots in Uganda.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Leaves are not an entry point, at least in greenhouse tests.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**First wave.** The disease was first seen in 1927 on excelsa coffee in the Central African Republic.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A 2009 CABI synthesis traces its spread to Cameroon, the DRC, Côte d’Ivoire and Guinea in the following decades.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> By the 1950s, coffee in West and Central Africa had been devastated, on excelsa, liberica and robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Producers uprooted and destroyed affected trees over large areas and replanted with resistant *C. canephora*. A 2009 CABI synthesis calls this highly successful in Côte d’Ivoire and the DRC.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> By the 1960s the disease was under control.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Second wave.** From the 1970s the disease re-emerged on robusta in Central Africa.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It was confirmed in Uganda in 1993, and by the end of 1998 it had spread to 18 districts.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> By the 1990s it was causing wide damage to arabica in Ethiopia, according to a 2021 genome study.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2024 study describes its severity on arabica as low at first, with signs of an increase.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> As of 2021, two populations of the fungus were active: one on robusta in Uganda, Tanzania and the DRC, one on arabica in Ethiopia.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Control: uprooting and resistant clones

No therapeutic cure was known in 2003, and chemical control is difficult.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Control therefore removes the sources of the fungus and replaces susceptible trees.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

- **Uprooting and burning.** Infected trees are uprooted and burned on the spot, stumps included.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Clean tools.** Machetes used on diseased trees should be cleaned, for example with a disinfectant or in a fire, according to the CABI report.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Moving material.** In Uganda, rules restricted the movement of unhulled coffee and banned coffee husks as mulch.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Replanting.** Uganda advised replanting on new land; when old sites can safely be replanted is not settled.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Resistant varieties.** Uganda screened its robusta for resistance, and its national research organisation released seven resistant varieties.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

The Regional Coffee Wilt Programme, whose synthesis CABI published in 2009, combined research, surveys and farmer training in several African countries.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Integrated pest management, which combines such measures, has its own entry.

## The pathogen and its names

**Names.** The fungus was isolated from excelsa in the DRC by Steyaert and named *Fusarium xylarioides* from that work.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The sexual stage, *Gibberella xylarioides*, was described by Heim and Saccas.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> EPPO uses *Fusarium xylarioides* as the preferred name.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Some recent papers, such as a 2023 review of Ugandan coffee resources, still write *Gibberella xylarioides*.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

**Species complex.** A 2005 DNA study sequenced 36 isolates previously identified as *F. xylarioides* or related fusaria from coffee and other woody hosts, and 12 isolates from the Ugandan outbreak; they fell into two distinct groups.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The first belongs to the *Gibberella fujikuroi* species complex; the second contained four lineages related to *Fusarium lateritium*.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The authors concluded that unrelated species are associated with coffee wilt, which matters for its epidemiology, causation and control.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**One species or four.** According to a 2021 chapter, a 2005–2006 proposal split the pathogen into four species: one for the Ethiopian arabica outbreak, one for the robusta epidemic in the DRC, Uganda and Tanzania, an asexual form for Guinea and *G. xylarioides* for the original excelsa disease.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The genome studies of 2021 and 2024 treat these as populations of one species (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**How the tree dies.** The fungus colonises the xylem. Wilting follows because the pathogen blocks water uptake, while it also degrades cell walls for nutrients.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the 2021 chapter, colonisation of the vascular system triggers host responses that disrupt water conduction.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Two host-specific populations

The current epidemics come from two populations that differ in host and place.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2006 review describes two genetically and biologically distinct forms.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2006 CABI report names them: one specific to robusta, the other to arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

| Population | Host | Where (as of 2021) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Robusta | *C. canephora* (robusta) | Uganda, Tanzania, DRC | Peck et al. 2021<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> |
| Arabica | *C. arabica* | Ethiopia | Peck et al. 2021<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> |
| Historic (two lineages) | Various *Coffea* species | Earlier outbreaks | Peck et al. 2024<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |

**Cross-inoculation.** In a 2006 cross-inoculation study, an Ethiopian arabica strain killed seedlings of all arabica cultivars tested and caused no wilt on robusta lines.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> Strains from robusta were pathogenic to all robusta lines tested.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> A historical excelsa strain killed *C. liberica* lines and also affected arabica and robusta, to a lesser degree.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> Ugandan studies found host specialisation that was not clear cut (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Temperature.** A 2023 laboratory study grew strains at 10–40 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Robusta strains grew significantly faster than arabica strains at 20 and 25 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Arabica strains produced more spores at 15 °C and below than at warmer temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The authors propose that temperature contributes to host specialisation, since arabica is grown higher and cooler than robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Genomes and borrowed genes

**Historical strains.** Culture collections kept strains from each outbreak. Most strains in the 2024 study came from the CABI collection in Egham, United Kingdom.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2021 study compared six strains collected over 52 years; a 2024 study compared 13 strains spanning six decades.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Ancestry.** In the 2021 study, the robusta population arose from the initial outbreak, while the arabica population is a divergent sister clade.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The 2024 study found at least four lineages: one specific to arabica, one to robusta and two historic lineages.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Horizontal gene transfer.** Both studies found genes that *F. xylarioides* appears to have acquired from *Fusarium oxysporum*, a related wilt fungus with many host-specific forms.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

- **2021.** Fifteen putative effector genes show evidence of horizontal acquisition. A cluster of four sits on a 20 kb scaffold resembling a mobile pathogenicity chromosome of *F. oxysporum*.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> DNA transposons nearby suggest transposition as the route, which the authors offer as a working hypothesis.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **2024.** The populations differ in large genomic regions that closely match several *F. oxysporum* forms; these putative transferred regions range from 20 kb to 200 kb.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> All populations share transposable elements with *F. oxysporum*, including a Starship, a large mobile element that could have mediated the transfer.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **In the plant.** Transferred effector genes were highly expressed during infection of arabica, together with enzymes that degrade plant cell walls.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The authors conclude that horizontal transfer likely contributed to the repeated emergence of coffee wilt.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In 2021 they suggested that this could inform intercropping, to limit effector transfer between closely related *Fusarium* taxa.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Spread and survival

**Entry.** Initial entry is considered to be mainly through roots and the lower stem, through wounds from slashing or livestock.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In greenhouse tests in Uganda, the fungus did not enter through leaves. After stem inoculation, wilt appeared only above the inoculation point.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Spores.** Ascospores form in the perithecia on the bark, and all spore types can carry the disease.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Dry diseased stems produce perithecia in large numbers, especially in the wet season.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Short-distance spread.** Infection of adjacent trees suggests root contact or short-distance dispersal, and transmission was not linked to wind direction.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2003 Ugandan study names infected coffee stalks as a possible route.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Tools, wood and soil.** In a CABI trial, trees wounded with a machete used on diseased trees developed the disease, and none wounded with a disinfected machete did.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> According to the same report, pieces of infected wood left on soil can also act as a source of infection for months.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Survival in soil is reported differently (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Other hosts.** In Uganda, *F. xylarioides* was recovered from banana roots and pseudostems along with other *Fusarium* species.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> According to the 2021 chapter, weeds of the mallow and nightshade families in coffee fields could act as alternative hosts, which needs confirmation.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Insects collected on affected farms did not carry the fungus.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Surveys and losses

**Regional survey.** A regional survey carried out within the programme covered coffee areas in Uganda, the DRC, Rwanda, Tanzania, Ethiopia, Cameroon and Côte d’Ivoire.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Uganda had the highest incidence, with most of the surveyed farms affected.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The disease was not found in Rwanda, Cameroon or Côte d’Ivoire, and in Tanzania only in the Kagera region bordering Uganda.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The survey percentages appear only in reports of the CABI-coordinated programme itself, a conference paper, a project report and a book chapter, so no independent or peer-reviewed source confirms them; Coffearium does not give them.

| Report | Loss or incidence stated | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Laboratory study, 2023 (introduction) | Up to 80% loss in production of plantations without treatment | Zhang et al.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> |
| Uganda, 2003 | 14.5 million trees destroyed since 1993; 15 of 24 main robusta districts affected | Kangire et al.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> |
| Uganda, early 1990s (stated 2022) | About 60% of robusta trees wiped out; estimated loss US$259 million | Mulindwa et al.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> |
| West and Central Africa, by the 1950s | Coffee crops decimated | Peck et al. 2024<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |

These figures use different measures: counts of trees, share of trees or share of production. They refer to different places and decades and are not directly comparable.

## Resistance screening and breeding

**Wild robusta.** According to a 2023 review, wild robusta collected in the Kalangala and Itwara forests of Uganda has a high level of resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

**Screening.** Uganda screened seedlings in a screen house by dipping their roots in spore suspensions. In a 2003 study of 119 robusta cultivars from the national germplasm collection, plus survivors from wilt hot spots, 15.4% of the seedlings survived the first round of inoculation.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Survivors were inoculated again, and those that survived were to be propagated vegetatively for field trials.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Selection and release.** According to the programme synthesis, further screening and agronomic trials reduced the candidates to a short list for release to farmers.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2022 peer-reviewed study states that the effort resulted in the release of seven resistant varieties, named Kituza Robusta (KR).<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The release year is given only in reports of the CABI-coordinated programme, so it is not stated here.

**Named varieties.** World Coffee Research lists NARO-Kituza Robusta 6 (KR6), bred by Uganda’s National Coffee Research Institute (NaCORI), as resistant to coffee wilt disease.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Its yield potential is given as 2,650 kg green coffee per hectare.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> KR8, KR9 and KR10 are listed as resistant too.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

**Ethiopia.** Ethiopian breeders select wilt-tolerant arabica from local landraces, as the entry on Ethiopian landraces describes.

## Chemical control

According to the 2021 chapter, chemical control of this pathogen is difficult.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Copper stem paints worked in the DRC and Uganda but not in Ethiopia; herbicide sprays showed promise but were too expensive for many producers.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The chapter states that wide-scale fungicide use is not advocated, because of the cost to smallholders and environmental concerns.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Coffearium gives no product or dose advice.

## What it means for the cup and the buyer

Coffee wilt disease kills trees, so its effect on supply runs through lost trees and replanting.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

Resistant clones raised a question of cup quality. A 2022 study cupped the five most commonly grown resistant robusta varieties from three agro-ecological zones in Uganda.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Most scored 70–79, a premium grade; KR4 scored 80 or more, a specialty grade, and its bean size varied little between sites.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Blends scored 76–82.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The entry on *Coffea canephora* covers robusta itself.

## Open questions

- **Soil survival.** How long the fungus stays infective in soil, and so when a site can be replanted, is not settled.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Host range.** How strict the split between robusta and arabica strains is, and whether bananas or weeds act as reservoirs in the field, needs more work.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **Species limits.** Whether the outbreaks are one species or several is answered differently in the sources (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Durability of resistance.** No study consulted for this entry tested whether the Ugandan resistant clones stay resistant over decades or against strains from other countries.
- **Excelsa today.** As of 2023, no coffee wilt disease had been reported on excelsa in Uganda, but its level of resistance needs research, as the entry on liberica and excelsa notes.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
