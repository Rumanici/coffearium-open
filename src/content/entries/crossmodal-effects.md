## One sense changes another

Flavour is built from several senses at once. Taste, smell and touch act together and interact to form one perception.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2015 review calls flavour perception perhaps the most multisensory of everyday experiences.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> How the senses combine is explained in the entry on how we taste coffee.

A crossmodal effect goes one step further. A cue in one sense changes what a person reports in another. The cue may belong to the coffee itself, such as its smell. It may also come from outside the cup: the colour of a mug, a word on a label or the music in a room.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

The SCA calls attributes such as origin, processing information or certifications extrinsic. They cannot be discovered from the coffee itself.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the SCA, extensive research has shown that extrinsic attributes affect both sensory perception and the impression of quality.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Smell and taste in the cup

The closest crossmodal effects happen inside the mouth. In a 1999 model study, odours added to sugar solutions changed how sweet they tasted. Some odours raised the sweetness; others lowered it.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The more sweet an odour smelled, the more it raised the tasted sweetness.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> That study used solutions, not coffee.

In espresso, the effect has been shown in the other direction. Sugar or a sweetener raised the caramel ratings of the aroma and lowered roasty ones.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The sweeteners are not volatile, so a taste changed how an odour was perceived.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> As of June 2024, the SCA was researching whether coffee’s own sweetness is a taste or a crossmodal effect of sweet-smelling compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Temperature works partly through physics. In a 2017 study, one coffee released more volatile compounds when served hotter.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Serving temperature has its own entry.

## The cup: colour, shape and texture

The cup is the cue studied most often in coffee. Three kinds of change have been tested.

- **Colour.** In a 2014 study, a café latte was rated less sweet from a white mug than from a blue or a transparent one.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In a 2019 study with 457 participants, cup colour shifted expected and rated sweetness and acidity of specialty coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Shape.** In a 2018 study at a coffee event in Brazil, the same coffee smelled stronger from a tulip-shaped cup. It tasted sweeter and more acidic from an angular “split” cup.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Texture.** In a 2020 study, coffee drunk from a rough cup was rated drier in aftertaste than from a smooth cup. Both Q graders and amateurs reported this.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

In these studies, the coffee in every cup was the same. Only the vessel changed.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Professionals were not immune. In the shape study, both experts and amateurs found the aroma strongest in the tulip cup.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The design and history of cups are covered in the entry on cups and vessels.

## Words, labels and the look of the drink

What people are told also changes what they taste. In a 2013 study, participants tasted two cups and were told that one held “eco-friendly” coffee. Both cups held identical coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Participants preferred the taste of the labelled cup and would pay more for it – at least those with strong attitudes towards sustainable consumption.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

Specialty coffee shows the same pattern. Information about a coffee changed which flavour notes consumers ticked, and the word “fermentation” on a label made one coffee seem more acidic.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> These label studies are described in the entry on flavour language.

The look of the drink counts too. In a 2015 study, latte art raised the value people expected from a milk coffee. They were willing to pay more for it than for the same drink without art.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Room, sound and light

For the setting around the cup, a 2019 review gathers the coffee evidence. It finds that visual, auditory, olfactory and tactile aspects of the environment all influence the coffee experience.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Two of the studies it reports, on lighting and on music, were never published.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

Two examples show the state of the evidence. In an unpublished laboratory study, people who liked weaker coffee drank more of it under dim light.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In an unpublished study with seven tasters, the results suggested that coffee flavour, bitterness and astringency seemed less intense with jazz.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Such results are leads, not established facts.

## Why cuppers taste blind

Cupping protocols hold many of these cues constant. SCA Standard 102-2024 requires cupping vessels of tempered glass or ceramic. All cups in a session must have identical volume, dimensions and material.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The cup studies suggest that a different cup could shift ratings.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Information is kept away from the tasters as well. The SCA states that tasters can be easily biased by extrinsic information about a coffee. It therefore keeps the physical, sensory and extrinsic assessments separate.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to a sensory textbook chapter, samples should be coded and served in random or balanced order.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The methods are explained under sensory science methods.

## How solid the evidence is

The coffee studies are few and close together. All six peer-reviewed cup and latte art studies consulted for this entry share one Oxford-based co-author.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> No independent replication of a coffee cup effect was found for this entry. Most effects are reported as significance tests on 0–10 or 0–100 line scales.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The 2014 colour study and the 2018 shape study report no effect sizes.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Several effects are narrow. In the 2014 colour study, each experiment changed only one of six rated attributes.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The intensity effect of experiment 1 did not reach significance in experiment 2, where only a trend was seen.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In the 2020 texture study, the cup changed acidity for Q graders but sweetness for amateurs. Body did not change in either group.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Designs also differ in what they measure. Online studies with pictures of empty mugs measure expectations, not perception.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The 2018 shape study measured perception but not expectations.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The cups in the texture study also differed in shape and in weight, by about 31 g (our calculation from the reported 173.0 g and 142.1 g).<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A 2017 review of receptacle shape notes that weight and similar factors need to be controlled. It adds that the science that explains such results is not there yet.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Proposed mechanisms

**Physical change.** A cup can change what reaches the nose. Glass shape can change the headspace above a drink.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The review notes, however, that such physico-chemical differences do not always lead to perceptible differences. Claims that a shape changes how liquid flows over the tongue are rarely backed by evidence.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Where only the outside of a vessel varied, physical accounts appear implausible.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In the second experiment of the 2014 mug study, only the colour of the grip differed.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Expectations.** Cues set up an expectation before the first sip. Drinks are rated more favourably in vessels judged congruent with their contents.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In the 2019 colour study, liking fell in incongruent pairings of cup colour and coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Information can change expectations and, through them, sensory perception.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

**Crossmodal correspondences.** People match features across senses in consistent ways. According to the shape review, rounder forms are associated with sweetness and fruitiness, angular forms with bitterness and sourness.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A shape may prime the matching taste and draw attention to it.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In the online mug study, short mugs were expected to give more bitter and more intense coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Contrast.** For the 2014 mug study, the authors suggest that a white mug may change how brown the coffee looks. That colour contrast may have changed perceived sweetness and intensity.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> They did not test this directly.

**Odour–taste learning.** In the 1999 model study, the effect of an odour on taste was best predicted by how sweet the odour smelled.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Caramel odour also reduced the sourness of citric acid.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The authors linked the results to previously learned associations between odours and tastes.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A related effect, oral referral, makes odours seem to come from the mouth.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## The cup-texture study in detail

The 2020 study ran three tests.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In a pre-test, 31 coffee experts tasted while rubbing sandpaper with the other hand; aftertaste was rated more intense.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The two conditions were not counterbalanced.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In the main experiments, 81 Q graders and 117 amateurs each drank one specialty coffee from either a smooth or a rough cup. Ratings used 10 cm line scales from 0 to 10.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Implications for cupping and research

**Vessels.** SCA 102-2024 specifies cupping vessels of tempered glass or ceramic, 200–350 mL, with a top diameter of 75–90 mm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> All cups must be identical in volume, dimensions and material.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The standard does not specify colour or surface texture.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Within one session, identical cups keep the vessel constant between samples; they do not remove its effect on the absolute scores.

**Information.** The CVA keeps physical results concealed from sensory assessors. Extrinsic information is compiled only after the sensory assessments are complete.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A sensory textbook chapter adds coding, random or balanced order, repetitions and individual assessment.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Comparing studies.** Cup material, shape and colour are rarely the same between coffee studies. The cup studies above suggest that this can shift ratings.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Serving temperature is a further variable: hotter coffee releases more volatile compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Replication.** No study consulted for this entry independently repeated a coffee cup effect with a new research team.
- **Size in practice.** No study consulted for this entry measured whether cup colour or texture changes scores on the SCA’s Coffee Value Assessment (CVA) forms.
- **Duration.** The shape review notes that how long such effects last has not been studied.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Coffee sweetness.** As of June 2024, the SCA was researching whether coffee sweetness is a taste or a crossmodal effect of sweet-smelling compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Room and sound.** Much of the evidence on lighting and music in coffee is unpublished or small.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Van Doorn, Wuillemin and Spence, 2014 – Mug colour and café latte.</strong> In two small between-participants experiments, the same café latte was served in white, blue or transparent mugs. The white mug raised perceived intensity in one experiment and lowered perceived sweetness in the other; the authors suggest colour contrast as a cause.</p>
<p class="meta">Flavour 3, 10 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1186/2044-7248-3-10">doi:10.1186/2044-7248-3-10</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Carvalho and Spence, 2018 – Cup shape and specialty coffee.</strong> At a coffee event in Brazil, 276 professionals and amateurs tasted one coffee from a tulip, open or split cup. Aroma was strongest from the tulip cup; sweetness and acidity were highest from the split cup, which amateurs liked least.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Quality and Preference 68, 315–321 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.04.003">doi:10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.04.003</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Carvalho, Moksunova and Spence, 2020 – Cup texture.</strong> Q graders and amateurs drank specialty coffee from a smooth or a rough cup. Both groups rated the aftertaste drier from the rough cup; Q graders also rated it more acidic and amateurs less sweet, while body did not change.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Quality and Preference 81, 103841 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2019.103841">doi:10.1016/j.foodqual.2019.103841</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Sörqvist et al., 2013 – An eco-label on identical coffee.</strong> Participants tasted two identical coffees, one described as “eco-friendly”. Those with strong sustainability attitudes preferred its taste and would pay more for it, also when they answered anonymously.</p>
<p class="meta">PLOS ONE 8(12), e80719 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0080719">doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0080719</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Spence and Carvalho, 2019 – The setting of the coffee experience.</strong> A narrative review of how lighting, sound, scent and touch in the environment influence coffee taste and choice. It is open about the evidence base: some studies are unpublished or small.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Quality and Preference 80, 103802 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2019.103802">doi:10.1016/j.foodqual.2019.103802</a></p>
</div>
