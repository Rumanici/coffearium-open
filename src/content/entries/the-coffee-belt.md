## What the coffee belt is

The coffee belt is an informal name for the band around the equator where coffee is grown. A 2006 review of coffee physiology describes the crop as confined to the inter-tropical zone. It runs from 20–25°N in Hawaii down to 24°S in Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the same review, coffee is grown in some 80 countries on four continents.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The inter-tropical zone is the band between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn. A 2015 study that modelled where arabica can grow used a wider belt, from 30°N to 33°S. Under the climate of 1950–2000, arabica grew on 7.2% of the land in that belt.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The belt is a description of where coffee grows, not a fixed line. Inside it, altitude, rainfall and soil decide which land suits coffee. Those limits are covered in Site selection and climate.

## Why coffee stays near the tropics

The 2006 review names temperature and humidity as the main reasons for the crop’s limits.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Frost is the hardest limit. It easily damages the coffee tree and can kill it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the review, this happens from time to time in southern Brazil. Closer to the equator, it happens at altitudes of around 2,000 m and above.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Height and distance from the equator work together. The review states that arabica coffee (*C. arabica*) grows best at higher altitudes, while robusta (*C. canephora*) can be grown from sea level to 800 m.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Further from the equator, coffee can also be grown at lower altitudes, unless frost limits it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Temperature, rainfall and dry-season values for each species are given in Site selection and climate.

The two species also come from different places. Wild arabica is native to tropical forests in Ethiopia at 1,600–2,800 m, according to the 2006 review; other sources give lower altitudes (see Coffea arabica).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> *C. canephora* comes from the lowland forests of the Congo River basin, which reach up to 1,200 m in Uganda.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Who grows the coffee

Production is concentrated in a few countries. The US Department of Agriculture (USDA) puts world production in 2025/26 at 178.8 million bags of 60 kg.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Brazil produced 63.0 million bags and Vietnam 31.7 million. Together that is 53.0% of the world total (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The five largest producers, with Colombia, Indonesia and Ethiopia, made up 73.3% (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## At the edges of the belt

Some coffee regions lie at or beyond the limits the 2006 review gives.

- **Hawaii and southern Brazil.** They mark the review’s northern and southern limits.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In southern Brazil, frost is the risk.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Yunnan, China.** In a 2024 modelling study, arabica land in Yunnan was most suitable at around 1,200 m, within about 680–1,750 m.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The minimum temperature of the coldest month contributed most to the model.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Nepal.** Commercial arabica production began in the 1990s.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A 2026 study worked on farms in the mid-hills, around 28°N, at 525–1,275 m.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> That is further north than the review’s limit.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Australia.** According to Southern Cross University, coffee grows in a few pockets: northern New South Wales, south-eastern Queensland and the Atherton Tablelands in northern Queensland.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Latitude, altitude and the cold limit

The 2006 review ties the latitudinal limits to ecological factors of temperature and humidity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It describes two places where frost limits coffee: southern Brazil, and altitudes around 2,000 m and above closer to the equator.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Even occasional frost may strongly limit the economic success of the crop.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How temperature falls with altitude in one coffee zone is set out in Site selection and climate.

The outlier regions fit the review’s statement that coffee grows lower further from the equator.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The Nepali study plots lay at around 28°N and 525–1,275 m.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Native arabica in Ethiopia grows at 1,600–2,800 m, according to the review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Nepal, suboptimal climate and a lack of cultivation expertise were identified as the main reasons for low yields.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

In the Yunnan study, the minimum temperature of the coldest month was the largest of the top five factors in the model. The others were altitude, mean temperature of the wettest quarter, slope and aluminium saturation of the soil.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The authors note that temperatures below 5 °C in the coldest months can cause leaf damage and stop growth.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In this model, cold rather than heat was the main limit.

## Climate change and the belt

Global modelling studies agree that land suited to coffee will shrink, and disagree on how far it can move away from the equator.

- **Both species, 2015.** Climate change will reduce the global area suitable for coffee by about 50% across emission scenarios.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Impacts are highest at low latitudes and low altitudes; the abstract names Brazil and Vietnam among the regions with large losses.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Arabica zones, 2015.** The share of suitable land in the 30°N–33°S belt halves from 7.2% to 3.6% by the 2050s.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Zones suited to arabica move upwards by about 500 m.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> About half of the new area lies at higher elevations within existing coffee regions.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Substantial areas become suitable south of the Brazilian coffee region, but the authors expect little expansion beyond the present latitudinal limits.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Coffee, cashew and avocado, 2022.** Coffee was the most vulnerable of the three crops. High-latitude and high-altitude areas may gain from rising minimum temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Vapour pressure deficit, 2022.** At 2.9 °C of global warming, countries making up 90% of global supply are more likely than not to exceed the threshold above which arabica yields fall rapidly.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The threshold itself is explained in Site selection and climate.
- **Yunnan, 2024.** Under future scenarios, suitable arabica habitat in Yunnan contracted most under SSP3-7.0; under SSP5-8.5 expansion was highest, peaking in 2040–2060.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

These are projections from models, not forecasts by Coffearium. For the edges of the belt, the studies point in two directions: warmer winters may open land at higher latitudes and altitudes, while temperature variability may still hold the southern limit in Brazil (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Production at the margins.** No source consulted for this entry gives production figures for Nepal, Yunnan or Australia that would show how much coffee comes from outside the 2006 limits.
- **Southern limit in Brazil.** Whether warmer winters will move coffee south of the present Brazilian region is not settled by the sources consulted here (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Robusta at the edges.** The sources consulted here describe the edges of the belt mainly for arabica; no study consulted for this entry mapped the cold limit of robusta by latitude.
- **Comparable statistics.** FAOSTAT, USDA and ICO use different years, units and collection methods; no source consulted here reconciles them country by country.
