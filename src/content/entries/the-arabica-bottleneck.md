## What a genetic bottleneck is

A genetic bottleneck happens when a population shrinks to a few individuals. The descendants carry only the variation those few plants had. Domestication usually involves such bottlenecks, which leave cultivated plants less diverse than their wild relatives.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Arabica went through several of them. A 2020 genome study found in *Coffea arabica* the lowest genetic diversity reported so far in any crop species.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its two parent species, *C. canephora* and *C. eugenioides*, were about 5–11 times more diverse in the same study, by our calculation from its figures.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same holds for wild arabica: the narrow genetic base affects both cultivated and wild plants.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Why arabica started narrow

Arabica is a hybrid of *C. canephora*, the species grown as robusta, and *C. eugenioides*.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The cross most likely happened only once.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2024 genome study dates this founding event to 350,000–610,000 years ago.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A species that begins from one hybrid begins with very little variation. How its two genomes sit side by side is covered in Polyploidy and the arabica genome.

The species then stayed small for a long time. The 2024 study found a bottleneck in the wild that began about 350,000 years ago and ended about 15,000 years ago.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Arabica also mainly fertilises itself. Insects may occasionally carry pollen between plants.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to a 2025 review of studies under Colombian conditions, self-pollination can account for 56.4–95.3% of fruit set.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Self-fertilisation leads to high levels of inbreeding, so each plant holds little variation of its own.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Flowering and pollination has the details of the flower.

## From Ethiopia to Yemen and the world

The ancestors of today’s cultivars split from the wild population about 30,500 years ago.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> People later took a few plants from Ethiopia to Yemen. A 2022 genetic study calls this a first severe bottleneck, in the 15th century.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Isolation of some populations within Yemen then caused a second one, before coffee was smuggled out in the 18th century.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

From Yemen, two genetic bases spread from the early 18th century: Typica and Bourbon. They gave rise to most arabica cultivars grown worldwide.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The Bourbon group descends from a single plant that survived on Réunion by 1720.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The full journey is told in [Arabica varieties and their lines](/entries/arabica-varieties/).

Each base carries very little variation, as historical records of a few founding plants would predict.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Breeding then worked from the same few lines. In Brazil, seven ancestors make up 97.55% of the genetic base of the country’s arabica cultivars.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Why it matters

Low diversity leaves a crop exposed. The 2024 genome study links arabica’s low diversity to its susceptibility to many pests and diseases, such as coffee leaf rust.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> As a result, the classic Bourbon and Typica lines grow successfully in only a few regions.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Breeders feel it too. A 2015 review names limited access to further arabica genetic resources and the breakdown of rust resistance among the newer challenges.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> According to World Coffee Research, most introgressed varieties owe their rust resistance to one shared parent, the Timor Hybrid.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Robusta genes from that hybrid have widened diversity in some cultivated lines.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The hybrid is covered in The Timor Hybrid.

## Wild Ethiopia as a reservoir

Ethiopia holds more of the species’ variation. In a 2019 study, Ethiopian accessions were more diverse than commercial varieties.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Accessions from west of the Great Rift Valley carried 34 alleles, gene variants, found in no other group.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The study names such accessions as a source of new alleles for breeding.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> How they are grown and collected is covered in [Ethiopian landraces](/entries/ethiopian-landraces/).

The reservoir has limits. The 2024 genome study calls the narrow base of both cultivated and wild arabica an obstacle to breeding with wild diversity.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Measured diversity

Studies measure diversity in different ways. Marker studies count variants at a few loci: amplified fragment length polymorphisms (AFLP) or microsatellites (simple-sequence repeats, SSR). Genome studies estimate nucleotide diversity (π), the average share of DNA positions that differ between two copies of the genome.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Marker studies.** A 2002 study compared 11 accessions derived from the Typica and Bourbon bases, four cultivars from Yemen and 11 subspontaneous accessions from the species’ primary centre of diversity, using 107 AFLP markers and six microsatellites.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Except for one AFLP marker, every marker in the cultivated material also occurred in the subspontaneous accessions. Polymorphism was much higher among the subspontaneous accessions and very low within the genetic bases.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The Yemen cultivars grouped with the Typica-derived accessions.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

A 2019 study used 30 microsatellite markers on 33 arabica genotypes, 25 of them from the Ethiopian collection.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Unbiased expected heterozygosity (uHe) was 0.183 in the western Ethiopian group, 0.170 in the eastern group and 0.106 in cultivars and inbred lines.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

A 2022 study genotyped 555 samples, mostly cultivated trees from Ethiopia and Yemen, with 10 microsatellite markers, and found six genetic clusters.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Two “Core Ethiopia” clusters held the most alleles, 65 and 45, of 77 in total.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The Ethiopian Legacy cluster held 39; the Typica/Bourbon, New-Yemen (109 samples) and Harrar (30 samples) clusters held 29–30 each.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Cultivated varieties worldwide come from the “Ethiopian Legacy” and Typica/Bourbon clusters, none from New-Yemen or Harrar.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Genome studies.** A 2020 study estimated diversity in 736 arabica accessions by genotyping by sequencing.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It found π = 2.3 × 10⁻⁴, from 652 SNPs across 193,873 informative nucleotides. The parent species gave π = 2.6 × 10⁻³ (*C. canephora*) and 1.1 × 10⁻³ (*C. eugenioides*), one order of magnitude higher.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The authors found such low levels in crops only in bread wheat, another recent allopolyploid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

A 2024 whole-genome study of 95 arabica accessions detected 1,877,440 SNPs and a median π of 3.12 × 10⁻⁴; the parent species were again one order of magnitude higher.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It found that cultivars, landraces and spontaneous germplasm all have a limited genetic base.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Timing of the bottlenecks

A second 2024 genome study sequenced 41 arabica accessions, among them 17 wild and three wild or cultivated accessions from both sides of the Great Rift Valley.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Modelling population history, it found the oldest bottleneck beginning abruptly about 350,000 years ago and ending about 15,000 years ago. A more recent bottleneck began gradually about 5,000 years ago and lasts to this day.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Cultivated accessions showed the oldest bottleneck but not the recent one.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The same study puts the split between the wild population and the cultivar founding population about 1,450 generations ago, about 30,000 years.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In cultivars, nucleotide diversity was only slightly lower than in wild populations, which the authors read as only minor bottlenecks during domestication.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Bourbon was less diverse than Typica, probably because of its known single-plant bottleneck.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> This reading differs from the marker studies; both are listed under Contested.

## New variation in a narrow species

Diversity is not fixed. The 2024 whole-genome study found that diversity rises in some cultivated genotypes to levels seen in one of the parent species, presumably through introgression from the Timor Hybrid.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It also found cryptic Timor Hybrid introgression in one accession of the Bourbon/Typica group.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The same study found recent chromosomal changes still varying within the germplasm: aneuploidies, deletions, duplications and exchanges between the two subgenomes. Four accessions showed whole-chromosome aneuploidies.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The authors propose these changes as a fundamental source of variation in this low-diversity species.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Consequences for breeding

Breeding has drawn on few parents. A 2013 pedigree study traced the 121 arabica cultivars released in Brazil between 1939 and 2009 to 13 ancestors. Seven of them make up 97.55% of the genetic base, and Bourbon Vermelho alone 52.76%.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

A 2015 review lists limited access to additional arabica genetic resources, breakdown of host resistance to leaf rust, worsening insect pests and climate change as challenges for breeders.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> According to World Coffee Research, cultivars from Typica and Bourbon still make up much of Latin American production, contributing to the bottleneck.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Breeders’ answers, introgression and F1 hybrids with wild Ethiopian parents, are covered in The Timor Hybrid and F1 hybrids.

## Open questions

- **Strength of the domestication bottleneck.** Marker studies describe strong losses on the way out of Ethiopia; a 2024 genome study finds cultivars only slightly less diverse than wild plants.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Chromosomal changes as a source of variation.** How much aneuploidies and exchanges between subgenomes add to usable diversity is proposed, not yet measured in breeding.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Breeding from wild diversity.** Ethiopian accessions carry private alleles, but the narrow base of wild arabica itself limits what they can add.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Anthony et al., 2002 – Where cultivated arabica came from.</strong> Compared accessions from the Typica and Bourbon bases, Yemeni cultivars and subspontaneous coffee from the centre of diversity with AFLP and microsatellite markers. Almost every marker in the cultivated material also occurred in the subspontaneous accessions, and variation within each base was very low.</p>
<p class="meta">Theoretical and Applied Genetics 104, 894–900 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00122-001-0798-8">doi:10.1007/s00122-001-0798-8</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Scalabrin et al., 2020 – One polyploidisation, little variation.</strong> Estimated diversity in 736 arabica accessions and in both parent species. Arabica showed the lowest diversity reported in any crop, one order of magnitude below its parents, which the authors trace to a single polyploidisation event and self-fertilisation.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 10, 4642 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-61216-7">doi:10.1038/s41598-020-61216-7</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Scalabrin et al., 2024 – Chromosome changes as a source of diversity.</strong> Built a chromosome-scale arabica genome and resequenced a large panel. Found low diversity even in the centre of origin, higher diversity in some Timor Hybrid-derived lines, and many recent chromosomal changes that may generate new variation.</p>
<p class="meta">Nature Communications 15, 463 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-44449-8">doi:10.1038/s41467-023-44449-8</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Salojärvi et al., 2024 – The arabica genome.</strong> Assembled genomes of arabica and its parents and modelled population history from wild and cultivated accessions. Dated the founding hybridisation and two wild bottlenecks, and found cultivars only slightly less diverse than wild plants.</p>
<p class="meta">Nature Genetics 56(4), 721–731 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-024-01695-w">doi:10.1038/s41588-024-01695-w</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Montagnon et al., 2022 – Ethiopia and Yemen genotyped.</strong> Genotyped 555 samples, mostly cultivated coffee from Ethiopia and Yemen. Found six clusters, with the most alleles in core Ethiopian groups, and placed a first severe bottleneck at the introduction into Yemen.</p>
<p class="meta">Agronomy 12, 3203 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy12123203">doi:10.3390/agronomy12123203</a></p>
</div>
