## What the ceremony is

In Ethiopia, coffee is usually prepared and served as a ceremony, often called the *buna* ceremony.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2018 ethnography from a rural district in eastern Amhara found that coffee there is never drunk alone.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> There is always an element of ceremony.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The ceremony starts with raw green coffee and ends with three rounds of small cups.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Everything happens in front of the guests: washing, roasting, pounding and boiling.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The same 2018 study describes these actions as showing the coffee maker’s skill.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Ethiopia is the centre of origin of *Coffea arabica*.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Its wild and cultivated coffees are covered in [Ethiopian landraces](/entries/ethiopian-landraces/). Coffee in Ethiopia is also eaten and cooked, for example as *buna qalaa*, a ritual meal of dried coffee berries.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Those uses have their own entry, [Coffee as food in Ethiopia](/entries/coffee-as-food-in-ethiopia/).

## The steps

The room is prepared first. Grass, leaves and, on special occasions, flowers are spread on the floor, and incense is burnt.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Two sources say the incense wards off evil spirits.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Then the coffee is made:

1. **Washing.** The green coffee is rubbed between the hands in water, which removes husks and dirt.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
2. **Roasting.** The coffee is roasted in a flat iron pan, preferably over charcoal.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A museum source describes a long-handled, wok-like pan over hot coals or a small fire.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
3. **Sharing the aroma.** The coffee maker waves the smoke of the roasting beans towards everyone present.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Guests breathe in the scent.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
4. **Pounding.** The roasted beans, with any spices, are pounded to powder with a mortar and pestle.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Amharic these are the *muketcha* (mortar) and *zenezena* (pestle).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
5. **Boiling.** The powder is boiled in the jebena, a clay pot with a round bottom.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
6. **Pouring.** The coffee is poured into small cups called *sini*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A skilled pourer fills each cup equally in one unbroken stream.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

A snack is served with the coffee, however small. Popcorn or roasted grain are traditional; a museum source also names cooked barley.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Sugar is passed round for those who want it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## The three rounds

One pot gives three rounds, and the first is the strongest and most prestigious.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the 2017 thesis, the same grounds are boiled again for the later rounds, so each round is weaker than the one before.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

A Cambridge museum page and a 2017 thesis from Addis Ababa name the rounds *abol*, *tona* and *baraka*.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The thesis spells the third *bereka*.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Both sources say the last name means “to be blessed”.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Serving follows an order. In the Amhara village of the 2018 study, the first round goes to the men in order of age, then to women and children.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## The jebena and the tray

The jebena is made of clay.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to a Cambridge museum page, it has a round bottom, a narrow spout and a handle on the side, and most have a long neck.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A filter, often of horsehair, is placed in the spout to hold back the grounds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The same page says a jebena is usually made by an elderly woman from local materials.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Who makes the coffee

The sources consulted for this entry agree that the coffee is usually made by a woman; the Amhara study says almost always.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A museum page calls her the matriarch of the household and says she considers the role an honour.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the Amhara study, the coffee maker should cover her head.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

According to an archaeologist writing about the Oromo of Wallaga, mothers teach their daughters to make coffee and hold the ceremony.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The article also describes the ceremony as a place where women come together.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2017 thesis describes it as strengthening social belonging.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## What the ceremony does socially

The ceremony is a measure of hospitality, according to the 2018 ethnography.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It is also a place for useful discussion and for hurtful gossip.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A textbook chapter names communication and connection as its central themes.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

It can settle conflict. Among the Oromo of Wallaga, people who have quarrelled are reconciled at the ceremony.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the Amhara study, government workers held coffee ceremonies of their own to share information, and the solidarity built there let them push back together.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Regional and religious variation

The Oromo article from Wallaga uses Afaan Oromo terms. The pot is the *jabanaa*, set over the fire on hearthstones, and the coffee is ground in a small mortar, *muka mooyyee bunaa*.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The article also names a ceramic coffee griddle, the *beddee*, and reports that salt is added while the *jabanaa* is still on the fire.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to the same article, metal kettles boil faster but are believed not to give the desired flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The cups were formerly made of clay; ceramic cups are now imported.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Religion shapes the ceremony in different ways. The Amhara study places coffee both in the religious sphere of Orthodox Christianity and in secular social life.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It describes coffee as a material medium for contact with the spiritual. It reports that *zar* spirits can be appeased by the smell and consumption of coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Some evangelical and reformist Orthodox Christians in the same area refuse to drink it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Among the Oromo, the Wallaga article describes the ceremony as integral to the traditional religion, Waaqeffannaa.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It adds that Christian and Muslim Oromo, too, hold the ceremony in the same manner.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Coffee also has ritual uses as food, such as the Boorana *buna qalaa*, which accompanies Oromo ritual performances.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Beyond that statement about Muslim Oromo, no source consulted for this entry describes how Muslim communities in Ethiopia hold the ceremony.

## The brew as a decoction

In technical terms the jebena makes a decoction: ground coffee is boiled with water, and the grounds stay in the pot.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The museum page describes the coffee being brought to the boil, then poured back into the jebena and boiled again.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The filter in the spout holds back the grounds as the coffee is poured.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Reusing the grounds for the second and third rounds makes each round weaker.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

No study consulted for this entry measured the dose, water volume, boiling time, temperature or extraction of a jebena brew. Nor did any measure the roast degree reached in the pan. Values for other boiled coffees, such as Turkish coffee, are in [Coffee drinks around the world](/entries/coffee-drinks-around-the-world/); they should not be transferred to the jebena.

## The Kaldi legend and the written record

According to a 2017 textbook chapter, the most widely told coffee legend is that of Kaldi, a goatherd in Abyssinia whose goats became lively after eating berries presumed to be coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The chapter takes the story from an older book.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It is a legend, not history.

An early written version is in a Latin treatise on coffee by Antonio Fausto Naironi, printed in Rome in 1671.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In the digitised copy, the herder has no name. He keeps camels “or, as others say, goats” (our translation), near a monastery in the region of Ayaman, “which is Arabia Felix”, that is, Yemen.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The Ethiopian setting and the name Kaldi therefore come from later retellings; no source consulted for this entry dates their first appearance or establishes which written version is the earliest.

A review of a university-press history of coffee in the Near East reports that the book explains why universally told stories such as the one about the goat are apocryphal.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> How coffee drinking reached Yemen and then the coffeehouses of the Ottoman world and Europe is covered in Coffeehouses from Mecca to Europe.

## Heritage status

The coffee ceremony is not on a UNESCO list of intangible cultural heritage. As of October 2026, UNESCO’s page for Ethiopia shows seven inscribed elements, all on the Representative List, and none of them is the coffee ceremony.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> They include Ethiopian epiphany, inscribed in 2019, and the Shuwalid festival of the Harari people, inscribed in 2023; neither decision mentions coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Heritage listings of other coffee traditions are covered in [Coffee drinks around the world](/entries/coffee-drinks-around-the-world/).

No source consulted for this entry confirms whether the ceremony is on Ethiopia’s national inventory of intangible heritage.

## Open questions

- **Typical duration and recipe.** The ceremony is described as long, but no source consulted for this entry measured its length, the coffee dose or the brewing time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Variation across Ethiopia.** The sources cover eastern Amhara, Oromo communities and Addis Ababa; practice in other regions, and in Muslim communities beyond one statement about Muslim Oromo, is not described in them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Heritage registration.** Whether the ceremony is on Ethiopia’s national inventory, or is being prepared for a UNESCO nomination, is not confirmed by an official record consulted for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **Origin of the Kaldi name.** The 1671 text names no herder;<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> when the name and the Ethiopian setting first appeared in print is not established here.
