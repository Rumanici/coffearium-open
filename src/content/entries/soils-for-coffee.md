## What coffee roots need from a soil

Soil holds the tree up and stores the water and nutrients its roots take in. A coffee science textbook chapter states that coffee tolerates many soils, provided they are deep, porous, well drained and well balanced in texture.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Texture means the mix of sand, silt and clay particles. The same chapter describes volcanic soils as particularly well suited.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Most coffee roots stay near the surface. In arabica coffee, the main part of the root system lies in the top 0.30 m of soil.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It spreads over a circle about 1.50 m across around the stem.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Conilon, a group of robusta, seems to follow a similar pattern.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Root depth and length change with soil characteristics, plant age, planting density and variety.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How the stem and branches grow above ground is covered in branching and fruiting wood.

A 2022 global suitability study of coffee, cashew and avocado found climate factors more limiting than land and soil factors for all three crops.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> On the soil side, low pH, poor texture and steep slopes mattered most.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Depth, structure, drainage and water

**Depth.** The FAO Ecocrop database lists a medium soil depth of 50–150 cm as optimal for robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> It lists shallow soils of 20–50 cm as the limit the crop still tolerates.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No comparable database entry for arabica was consulted for this entry.

**Structure and pores.** Structure is the way soil particles clump into crumbs and blocks, leaving pores between them. Pores hold water and air and give roots a path. In an Oxisol under coffee in the Brazilian Cerrado, pores made up 0.61 m³ per m³ of soil. Large pores made up 0.21 m³ per m³.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Wheels and machines compact soil, which can damage its structure and lower coffee production.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Drainage.** Water must drain away so that roots get air. Ecocrop lists well-drained soil as optimal for robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The textbook chapter gives good drainage as a condition for coffee in general.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Water holding.** How much rain coffee needs depends partly on how much water the soil retains.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the Cerrado study, a grass grown between the rows improved the soil properties linked to water supply for coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Irrigation itself is covered in [Irrigation](/entries/irrigation/).

## Typical coffee soils

Soil scientists name soils by their main properties. The international system, the World Reference Base (WRB), groups them into reference soil groups.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> National studies often use other systems, such as the Brazilian classes (Latossolos) or Oxisols, and the sources consulted here do not equate these classes in general. Two WRB groups appear in the coffee sources of this entry:

- **Andosols** are marked by allophanes or aluminium–humus complexes.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Colombian coffee studies speak of andic soils, or Andisols, derived from volcanic ash; they do not give a WRB group.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Nitisols** have low-activity clays, many iron oxides and a strong structure, and they bind phosphorus.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Two Kenyan reference profiles under coffee are Nitisols.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Colombia.** In Colombia, 52% of coffee plantations stand on soils derived from volcanic ash.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> These soils are acid, with a pH below 5.5, and rich in organic matter.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Their dry soil is light: about 0.7 g per cm³, half the 1.4 g per cm³ of a sandy soil in Tolima.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Brazil.** Coffee in the Cerrado, the savanna of central Brazil, expanded mainly on Oxisols.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Oxisols cover more than half of the Cerrado.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The soil of that study is a Latossolo Vermelho in the Brazilian system, which the authors give as a Typic Haplustox.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> One studied plantation in Patrocínio, Minas Gerais, grows on a red Latossolo.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Kenya.** Two Kenyan reference profiles held by ISRIC are Nitisols linked to coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> One, used for coffee, maize, bananas and cassava, is very deep, well drained and derived from basic volcanic breccia.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The other, at about 1,700 m east of the Rift Valley, grew arabica coffee before its present crops.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> No source consulted here gives the share of Kenyan coffee on Nitisols.

**Where the species come from.** According to the textbook chapter, wild robusta grows mainly on red, acidic, well-drained soils of low natural fertility.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Wild arabica in Ethiopia grows on deep, red or brownish, well-drained soils.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Soil pH in brief

Soil pH measures how acid a soil is. Recommended ranges for coffee differ by country and species (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The Colombian research centre Cenicafé recommends liming to raise soil pH to 5.0–5.5 and lower exchangeable aluminium below 1.0 cmol(+)/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> An extension page of Kerala Agricultural University in India recommends pH 6.0–6.5, without naming a region or species.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Why acidity matters, aluminium toxicity and how lime is applied are covered in [Soil acidity and liming](/entries/soil-acidity-and-liming/).

## How soil is sampled and tested

A soil test tells a producer what the soil holds before fertiliser is bought. According to the textbook chapter, training programmes recommend that producers have soil samples analysed by a competent laboratory before buying fertiliser.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Colombian guidance from Cenicafé describes the sampling step by step:<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

1. Take samples under the trees, to a depth of 20 cm.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
2. Walk the lot in a zigzag and take five to six subsamples per hectare.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
3. Mix them into one composite sample of about 1.0 kg.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
4. Sample at least 3–4 months after the last fertiliser application.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The bulletin interprets soil pH, organic matter, exchangeable aluminium and nutrients such as phosphorus, potassium and magnesium.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Cenicafé compares each value with a critical level and sets fertiliser doses from it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> How those doses are worked out belongs to [Nutrition and fertilisation](/entries/nutrition-and-fertilisation/).

## Roots in the profile

Root activity moves through the profile with the seasons. In a Kenyan study cited in a 2007 review, arabica root activity after a long dry period was greatest at 0.45–0.75 m depth, close to the stem.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> After rain, activity was greatest at the soil surface, 0.70 m from the stem.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In dense plantings, roots grow deeper and take up water and nutrients from lower soil horizons.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Root growth is seasonal and often starts before shoot growth.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Heavy crops may seriously impair root function.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Branch die-back is preceded by the death of a large share of the absorbing roots.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> This link is covered in [Overbearing and dieback](/entries/overbearing-and-dieback/).

Where the absorbing roots sit also decides where soil is sampled. In a Brazilian plantation, most absorbing roots were found under the canopy.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Colombian guidance samples the same zone under the trees.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Soil mass and what a test value means

A laboratory reports a concentration, such as milligrams of phosphorus per kilogram of soil. The amount per hectare depends on how much soil the roots explore. In the Colombian coffee zone, the top 20 cm of one hectare weighs between 1,400,000 and 2,800,000 kg.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The range follows from the dry soil density, from about 0.7 g per cm³ in Andisols to 1.4 g per cm³ in a sandy soil.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

As a worked example, take the Cenicafé critical level for phosphorus at one crop stage, 30 mg/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In the lightest soil, that is 42 kg of phosphorus per hectare in the top 20 cm; in the heaviest, 84 kg (our calculation, from the soil masses above). The same test value therefore stands for different stocks in different soils.

## Organic matter

According to the textbook chapter, farming should preserve and if possible increase soil organic matter, to support microbial life and the soil's exchange capacity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Exchange capacity is the soil's ability to hold nutrient cations such as potassium, calcium and magnesium.

Measured values differ widely between coffee soils. In Colombian soils from volcanic ash, organic matter averaged 18% in the soil units CH and TI, 22% in PI and 6% in MN.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> That study examined how this organic matter binds aluminium.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> On 54 farms in Huila, Colombia, the mean organic matter of four soil types ranged from 3.54% to 5.13% in samples to 15 cm.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The paper's table gives P = 0.0054 for this difference, while its abstract reports P < 0.001.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Cenicafé treats organic matter below 6% as very low and then recommends decomposed coffee pulp or other organic amendments.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Compaction, cover and soil carbon

Mechanised plantations compact their soil where wheels run. A 2008 study in Patrocínio, Minas Gerais, sampled a red Latossolo under coffee planted at 4 × 1 m in 1995.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It compared the cart track, the soil under the canopy, the middle of the interrow and native Cerrado vegetation.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The cart track had the highest load-bearing capacity; the interrow, subsoiled about 14 months before sampling, was the most susceptible to compaction.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Soil under the canopy behaved like the native soil, with a structure favourable to root growth.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Ground cover changes the topsoil too. In a 2016 Cerrado trial, irrigated coffee with a brachiaria grass grown between the rows gave higher yields.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Irrigation favoured organic carbon stocks in the upper soil layers, while brachiaria favoured the physical properties linked to water availability.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

According to the textbook chapter, soil erosion rates vary widely between coffee farms, and most farms lose soil faster than is optimal.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured erosion on coffee farms; soil conservation will be covered in a planned entry, Land preparation and soil conservation.

## Soil and cup quality

Only a few studies have tested soil against cup quality directly, and they are correlational. According to the textbook chapter, depleted soils go with lower coffee quality, and the most acidic coffees come from volcanic soils.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Ethiopia, 2016.** A study paired 53 soil samples, taken at 0–50 cm near the trunks, with the coffee from the same places in the 2010/11 harvest.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Quality correlated positively with cation exchange capacity (r = 0.36), available magnesium (r = 0.28), exchangeable acidity (r = 0.35) and soil pH (r = 0.30).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> It correlated negatively with available copper (r = −0.35), zinc (r = −0.40) and total nitrogen (r = −0.40).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Available iron and exchange capacity explained 21.9% and 13% of the variation in overall quality.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Huila, Colombia, 2015.** A study of 54 farms in Suaza and Timaná related soil, crop management and processing to cup quality.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The four soil types differed significantly in pH, calcium, magnesium, base saturation, aluminium, phosphorus and zinc.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The abstract associates the cups with the lowest sensory scores with aluminium and iron in the soil.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The results text is not fully consistent with this: it links the low-scoring group to moderately acid soils high in iron, copper, zinc and sulphur, and very acid soils high in aluminium to the group associated with body, sweetness, balance, flavour and acidity.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The results text also links iron-rich, very acid soils to a third group, so the paper gives no consistent soil signal for the lowest-scoring cups. Fermentation time and shade management also entered the groups, so soil is one factor among several.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

The two studies point in different directions for iron (see Contested). Neither controls for altitude, variety and processing the way a designed trial would. No study consulted for this entry shows that changing a soil property changes cup quality.

## Open questions

- **Arabica soil limits in a database.** No database or institute value for the soil depth arabica needs was consulted for this entry; the depth values above are for robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **One pH target.** Recommended ranges differ between Colombia, India and the Ecocrop database, and no study consulted here compared yields across pH in several countries.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Soil and the cup.** The two cup-quality studies are correlational and disagree on iron.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A designed trial that changes one soil property and scores the cup was not found.
- **Shares of soil groups.** Apart from the Colombian 52% on ash soils, no source consulted here gives the share of coffee land on each soil group by country.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Ortiz-Escobar et al., 2006 – Organic matter in Colombian ash soils.</strong> Studied the organic matter of andic soils in the Colombian coffee zone and its capacity to bind aluminium. Reports that 52% of Colombian coffee stands on soils from volcanic ash, acid and rich in organic matter, with averages of 6–22% by soil unit.</p>
<p class="meta">Cenicafé 57(1), 51–57 · <a href="https://www.cenicafe.org/es/publications/arc057%2801%29051-057.pdf">cenicafe.org</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Rocha et al., 2016 – Oxisol under coffee and brachiaria.</strong> Measured carbon, nitrogen and physical properties of a Cerrado Oxisol under coffee managed with irrigation and with brachiaria between the rows. Irrigation favoured carbon in the upper layers; the grass favoured properties linked to water supply.</p>
<p class="meta">Pesquisa Agropecuária Brasileira 51(9), 1476–1483 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1590/S0100-204X2016000900046">doi:10.1590/S0100-204X2016000900046</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Gontijo et al., 2008 – Compaction in a coffee Latossolo.</strong> Modelled the load a red Latossolo can bear at four positions in a plantation in Minas Gerais. The cart track had the highest load-bearing capacity, the subsoiled interrow the lowest, and soil under the canopy kept a structure favourable to roots.</p>
<p class="meta">Revista Brasileira de Ciência do Solo 32(6), 2227–2234 · <a href="https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/1802/180214064002.pdf">redalyc.org</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Mintesnot et al., 2016 – Soil chemistry and Ethiopian coffee quality.</strong> Correlated soil chemical properties of 53 samples with grade and specialty quality. Exchange capacity, magnesium and pH correlated positively; zinc, copper and total nitrogen negatively; available iron explained 21.9% of the variation in quality.</p>
<p class="meta">East African Journal of Sciences · <a href="https://eajs.haramayajournals.org/index.php/eajs/article/view/277">eajs.haramayajournals.org</a></p>

<p><strong>Suárez Salazar et al., 2015 – Soil, management and the cup in Huila.</strong> Related soil chemistry, crop management and processing on 54 farms to sensory scores with multivariate methods. <p class="meta">Acta Agronómica 64(4), 329–335 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.15446/acag.v64n4.44641">doi:10.15446/acag.v64n4.44641</a></p>
