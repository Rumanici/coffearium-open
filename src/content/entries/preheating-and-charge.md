## What happens at the start of a roast

A roast begins before any coffee goes in. The roaster is first heated while empty. Then the green coffee is dropped in; this moment is called the charge.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A stopwatch measures the roast time from the charge to the drop, when the beans leave the roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

This is step 1 of 6 in the roast. The roasting overview describes the whole process, and the roast profile entry explains how the curve is read. Next step: drying phase.

## Why roasters are preheated

A roaster is a mass of hot metal as well as hot air. Even models of an air roaster include heat passing from the air to the metal wall, and from the metal to the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to a roasting textbook chapter, in a drum a substantial amount of heat may pass to the beans by conduction from the hot wall.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Preheating brings that metal to a known state. In a 2024 study, a 5 kg drum was preheated to 210 ± 5 °C for at least 30 min before each roast. The aim was "to stabilize the drum temperature".<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Laboratory air roasters are preheated too, before the beans go in.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

A roasting textbook chapter names the "cold start behavior of the roaster" as one reason why the beans' temperature can differ from batch to batch. Green-coffee moisture and the weather are others.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Practice between batches.** Practitioners describe a "between-batch protocol": a fixed routine between roasts meant to bring the machine back to the same starting state. One practitioner quoted on a roasting-software company's blog describes its aim as resetting "the thermal energy" of the machine.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Practitioners also say the first roast of the day is always a little different.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> No peer-reviewed study consulted for this entry measured a first-batch effect or tested a between-batch protocol.

## Charge temperature

**What it means.** In two studies from one research group, the charge temperature (also "start temperature") is the air temperature when the beans enter the roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Roasters also log the reading of the bean probe, the temperature probe that sits in the bean mass.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It sits where it touches both beans and hot air, so in the empty drum it reads hot air.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Where the probe sits matters.** An air temperature depends on where it is measured. In an empty laboratory air roaster, the air leaving the chamber was cooler than the air going in. At the lowest air speed tested, it read 228.5 °C against 249 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> According to a roasting textbook chapter, the bean probe of a production roaster reads a mix of bean surface and hot air. Its reading is specific to the machine design.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Machine-specific values.** In eight sensory studies from one research group, most roasts on a 1 kg drum were charged at 200 °C. A 15 kg roaster was charged at 160 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> With washed coffee from Sidamo, Ethiopia, in both studies, first crack came after 8:50–9:52 min on the drum and 8:25–9:25 min on the convection roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A charge temperature is therefore a setting for one machine, not a number to copy to another.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## The drop and the turning point

When the green coffee goes in, the bean-probe reading falls. The probe reads a mix of bean surface and hot air, and it now sits among cool beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The lowest reading is called the turning point. In one drum study, the rate of rise was highest just after it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The beans themselves do not cool down after the charge. They enter at room temperature, and from then on heat flows into them from the hotter surroundings.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The dip belongs to the probe: it moves from sitting in hot air to sitting among cool beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2025 roaster model treats the thermocouple reading and the bean temperature as two different values.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> One 2024 study describes the turning point as the moment when "the coffee beans and the roaster temperature equilibrate".<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

So the turning point is a useful marker for comparing batches on one roaster, with one probe in one place. It is not a bean temperature that can be compared between roasters.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The roast profile entry shows where it sits on the whole curve.

## Batch size and charge temperature

A bigger batch is more cool mass to heat. A roasting textbook chapter describes the air-to-bean ratio: the amount of hot air used per kilogram of green coffee. The same heat can be delivered with less, hotter air or more, cooler air.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In a 2025 study on a 0.5 kg spouted-bed air roaster, batches of 200–500 g were roasted at different airflows.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Batch size and airflow changed the time–temperature profiles more than inlet air temperature did, especially early in the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Practitioners adjust the charge temperature to the batch size, on the reasoning that a larger batch needs more energy.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> No peer-reviewed study consulted for this entry tested a rule for matching charge temperature to batch size in a drum.

## Drum and air roasters at the start

According to a roasting textbook chapter, a substantial share of the heat in a drum may pass by conduction from the hot wall. In a fluid-bed roaster, which lifts the beans on hot air, convection predominates.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured how much heat the preheated drum wall adds in the first minutes. Air roasters also have metal walls that exchange heat with air and beans.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Laboratory air roasters make the start easier to define. In one study, a batch of 100 g went into a preheated fluid bed with hot air at a set temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> How the drum and fluid-bed roasters compare in other ways is covered in their own entries.

## Heat transfer in the first minutes

**The driving force is the temperature gap.** Single-bean models treat the heat flux into the bean as proportional to the difference between the roasting temperature and the bean surface temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> At the charge, with the bean at room temperature, the gap is at its largest.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In a 2024 drum study the rate of rise was highest just after the turning point.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Inside the bean.** A 2017 model started a bean at 20 °C and about 12% moisture (basis not stated) in air at 200 °C. In the model adapted from earlier work (Model 1), the main differences in temperature and moisture inside the bean occur only in the first minute, and after that they are nearly uniform.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The authors' new model (Model 2), whose drying front they consider more realistic, shows a much slower drying front.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> That model includes convection at the surface and conduction inside the bean, but no radiation.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In extreme fast roasting, a textbook chapter warns, a steep gradient can over-roast the surface while the core stays under-roasted.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Three paths, disputed shares.** According to the textbook chapter, in any hot-air roaster convection, conduction and radiation act at the same time, and their shares vary between machines.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The textbook chapter calls radiation usually negligible.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2025 study held single beans in a gas-fired drum, touching neither wall nor beans; the fitted overall coefficient was far above the calculated convective one, and the authors concluded that radiation governs.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In a laboratory fluid bed, the bean core never reached the set air temperature, which the authors attributed to "excessive heat transfer by radiation", typical of small equipment with a large air-to-bean ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Production drums with a full bed and drawn air were not measured in either study.

**Probe and bean.** A 2025 spouted-bed model treats the thermocouple reading and the bean temperature as different values. It fits system parameters that are hard to measure, among them a "thermal response coefficient".<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The depth and timing of the turning point therefore describe the probe in its machine as well as the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## What studies show about charge temperature

**Charge temperature is one lever among several.** In the 2024 titratable-acidity study, every roast started from the same preheat of 210 ± 5 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A "fast start" profile with high initial heat reached first crack at about 8 min; a "slow start" with low initial heat at 12 min.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**The defect study.** Six roasts of one washed Kenyan coffee were charged at 135–275 °C on a 1 kg drum, with roasting time also varied.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The highest charge temperature (275 °C) gave the earliest first crack, at 5:41 min, and the lowest (135 °C) the latest, at 17:58 min.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In between the order broke: the "baked" roast, charged at 230 °C, reached first crack at 10:20 min, later than the normal roast charged at 210 °C (9:03 min).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**A cold charge did not remove the aroma.** The "underdeveloped" roast was charged at 135 °C and roasted 8 min longer than the normal roast. The authors expected the stalled start to suppress typical coffee aromas. Instead, most aroma compounds ended up at levels comparable to the normal roast.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Its colour was almost the same as the normal roast's (Agtron 74.9 against 74.4).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Held constant elsewhere.** In the other seven studies of the same research group, the start temperature was kept the same within each study, so that timing could be studied.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Their finding that colour predicts flavour better than timing therefore says nothing about charge temperature itself.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In a multivariate study on a professional roaster, initial drum temperature was one of five variables tested; time after first crack had the largest influence.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Fast and slow starts in research roasters.** Laboratory fluid-bed studies compare high-temperature short-time (HTST) and low-temperature long-time (LTLT) roasting by setting the hot air. In one, 260 °C air for 170 s and 228 °C air for 720 s gave the same colour.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A doctoral thesis on hot-air roasting called the temperature profile the most crucial parameter in process design.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> These studies set an air temperature for the whole roast; they do not isolate the charge.

## Safety at start-up

Roasting air carries chaff (silverskin), dust and bean fragments out of the drum; a cyclone separates them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In two health hazard evaluations at coffee roasting facilities, the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) described chaff build-up in roaster exhausts. At one site, an afterburner stack was smoking because of chaff residue.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> At another, NIOSH recommended exhaust cleaning "to remove chaff build up to reduce a fire hazard".<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Neither report addresses preheating or start-up itself.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Events late in the roast are covered under second crack.

## Risks and defects

- **Too cold a start.** In one defect study, a roast charged at 135 °C ran 8 min longer than the normal roast; the authors describe a stalling of the curve at the start.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Too hot a start.** A charge at 275 °C gave the shortest roast (7:40 min) with a major change in aroma composition; trained assessors placed it with the dark roast, associated with intensity, bitterness and burnt notes.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Practitioner names for surface marks from too much heat (scorching, tipping) are collected in the roast defects entry.
- **An unsettled machine.** According to a textbook chapter, with air-temperature control the beans' temperature path can vary from batch to batch with a cold roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Reading too much into the turning point.** Its value depends on probe position and machine design.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **First batch and between-batch routines.** Practitioners describe both<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>; no peer-reviewed measurement was found.
- **Charge temperature and flavour in isolation.** In the sensory studies found, charge temperature changed together with other settings or was held constant.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Batch size in a drum.** The batch-size effect on early profiles was shown in a spouted bed<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>; no drum study consulted for this entry measured it with heat.
- **Heat stored in the drum.** No study consulted for this entry measured how much heat a preheated drum wall gives to the first minutes of a roast.
- **Radiation at the start.** Its share in a loaded production drum has not been measured in the sources found.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Anokye-Bempah et al., 2024 – Same preheat, different starts.</strong> Roasted coffees on a 5 kg gas drum preheated to 210 ± 5 °C for at least 30 min before every batch, and sampled every minute. With the same preheat, high or low initial heat moved first crack from about 8 to 12 min.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 14, 8237 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-57256-y">doi:10.1038/s41598-024-57256-y</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Al-Shemmeri et al., 2025 – What the thermocouple sees.</strong> Models heat and mass transfer in a 0.5 kg spouted-bed roaster from logged inlet, outlet and product temperatures. It treats thermocouple reading and bean temperature as different, fits system parameters such as a thermal response coefficient, and shows batch size and airflow shaping early profiles more than inlet temperature.</p>
<p class="meta">Beverages 11(6), 162 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/beverages11060162">doi:10.3390/beverages11060162</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Giacalone et al., 2019 – Defects made at the charge.</strong> Six roasts of one Kenyan coffee on a 1 kg drum, charged at 135–275 °C and roasted for different times, were analysed for aroma and tested with trained assessors and consumers. The cold-charged roast ended up with aroma close to the normal roast.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Quality and Preference 71, 463–474 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.03.009">doi:10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.03.009</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Fadai et al., 2017 – Heat and moisture inside one bean.</strong> Two mathematical models of a single bean starting at 20 °C in 200 °C air. In the model adapted from earlier work, gradients matter mainly in the first minute; the authors' new model, which they consider more realistic, shows a much slower drying front.</p>
<p class="meta">International Journal of Heat and Mass Transfer 104, 787–799 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2016.08.083">doi:10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2016.08.083</a></p>
</div>
