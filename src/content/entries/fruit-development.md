## From flower to cherry

A coffee cherry starts as a fertilised flower and ends as a ripe fruit holding two seeds. How the flower opens and is pollinated is covered in [Flowering and pollination](/entries/flowering-and-pollination/). This entry follows what happens next, inside the fruit.

Growth is not steady. In arabica coffee, a 2007 review describes five stages: a pinhead stage, rapid swelling, a short pause, endosperm filling and ripening.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2025 review uses the same stages with limits in days after flowering.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Each stage builds a different part of the fruit, so a problem at one stage leaves a different mark on the harvest.

The layers of the finished fruit, from skin to seed, are described in [Coffee cherry anatomy](/entries/coffee-cherry-anatomy/). Here they matter only as they form.

## The five phases

**Pinhead.** For the first 6–10 weeks after flowering the young fruit hardly grows.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It looks like a small pinhead and reaches about 10% of its final size.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Rapid expansion.** Then the fruit swells fast, for about 10 weeks, roughly from week 6 to week 17.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The fruit gains size and fresh mass fast in this phase.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Inside each seed, the space is first filled by the perisperm, a tissue that comes from the mother plant.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Slow growth.** A pause of about two weeks follows, during which the fruit reaches its final size.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Endosperm filling.** From about week 17 to week 28, dry matter rises steadily.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The endosperm, the tissue that becomes the green coffee bean, grows into the space the perisperm leaves behind.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It starts as a liquid and hardens as it fills.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The phase ends at physiological maturity: the fruit is olive green and the endosperm is solid.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Ripening.** Ripening spreads over about 10 weeks, from week 24 to week 34 after flowering.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The outer fruit grows again in size and mass, and turns red or yellow.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## What sets the size of the bean

The size of the bean is decided early, before the bean itself has formed. During rapid expansion the seed coat tissues swell and set the size of the cavity, or locule. According to a 2007 review, this expansion sets the maximum size of the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The endosperm later fills that space.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Water is the main limit. Water shortage during rapid expansion often reduces the growth of the fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Fruits that expand in wet weather become larger, with larger locules, than fruits that expand in hot, dry weather.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2025 review links a deficit in this phase to more small beans.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Slow ripening also goes with larger beans. Longer maturation times and larger beans are usually observed at high altitude and under shade, according to a 2006 review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A coffee science textbook chapter explains it the same way: slower ripening allows better bean filling.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> How growers use altitude and shade is covered in [Site selection and climate](/entries/site-selection-and-climate/) and [Shade and agroforestry](/entries/shade-and-agroforestry/). Bean size as a grade is covered in [Bean size and density](/entries/bean-size-and-density/).

## Green to red

The skin of the fruit stays green for most of its development.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At the end it turns briefly yellow and then red.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Two things happen at once: the green chlorophyll breaks down, and red pigments called anthocyanins build up.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Colour is the main sign that a fruit is ripe.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Some cultivars never turn red. In natural yellow-fruited mutants of arabica the skin stays yellow when ripe.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Why is not settled (see Contested). In *C. liberica*, ripe fruits are red, red-streaked yellow or occasionally fully yellow, according to a plant-resources handbook.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> How pickers use the colour stages is covered in [Harvesting and sorting](/entries/harvesting-and-sorting/).

## How long each species takes

The species differ widely. A 2006 review gives 6–8 months from flowering to ripe fruit for *C. arabica* and 9–11 months for *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same review gives more than a year for *C. liberica*, and only 10–12 weeks for *C. racemosa*, a wild species.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Other studies report longer spans for the two crop species. A 2025 review gives 220–243 days on average for arabica, and 180–330 days overall.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Conilon clones of *C. canephora* took 36–55 weeks, depending on the clone.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Warm weather shortens the cycle: above 23 °C, arabica fruits develop and ripen faster, often with a loss of quality.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Perisperm and endosperm

The coffee seed is built twice. The perisperm develops from the nucellus of the ovule soon after fertilisation; until mid-development the fruit consists mainly of pericarp and perisperm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The endosperm is a triploid tissue that develops inside it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In *C. arabica* cv. IAPAR 59, a rapid expansion of the perisperm occurred between 60 and 90 days after flowering (DAF); the endosperm began to develop between 60 and 75 DAF and became detectable at 75 DAF.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> At 118 DAF the two tissues were present in equal proportions.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> By maturity, at 205–234 DAF, the endosperm filled the whole locule.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The perisperm is consumed as the endosperm grows. Its inner part gives way, and in the Acaiá Cerrado measurements cited in the 2006 review the endosperm occupied the whole space by about week 19.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Only the outer layer remains. At maturity it is a pellicle about 70 µm thick: the silverskin.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The perisperm also stores starch for a while, which is degraded as the seed develops.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the endosperm, sucrose accumulated to close to 6% of dry weight.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> How the finished seed is built, and how long it stays alive, is covered in [Seed anatomy and storage](/entries/seed-anatomy-and-storage/).

*C. racemosa* runs the same programme at high speed. In a field study its perisperm disappeared between 12 and 40 DAF, replaced by the endosperm, and the fruits completed their development in 68 DAF.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The authors give 60–110 days for the species, against 180–250 days for *C. arabica*.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Water first, dry matter later

The fruit takes up water before it stores dry matter. The 2011 field study below sampled 300 fruits of arabica cv. Obatã IAC 1669-20 every two weeks at Jaboticabal, São Paulo, Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The authors describe slow growth from 96 to 126 DAF, rapid growth from 127 to 158 DAF and slower, linear growth from 159 to 279 DAF.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Fresh mass grew fastest in the rapid phase, dry mass in the linear phase.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

By our calculation from the same tables, fresh mass had reached 57% of its peak at 174 DAF, dry mass only 30%. Dry matter rose from about 17% of fresh mass during rapid growth to 31% at 279 DAF.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The pattern matches the stage model: volume and water first, dry matter during endosperm filling.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The timing does not. Rapid growth started about 10 weeks later than in the 2025 review, and the authors relate the slow start partly to an 83 mm water deficit in October.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry gives a full growth curve for *C. canephora* or *C. liberica*.

## Bean size and water stress

A 2007 review states that the expansion of the integument sets the maximum size of the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Under limiting water the ovules do not reach their potential full size.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In a study at Ruiru, Kenya, cited in the review, potential bean size was strongly determined by rainfall 10–17 weeks after blossoming.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The 2025 review adds that water scarcity during rapid growth can reduce the fresh mass and volume of the fruits.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Later stress does different damage. The 2025 review cites a study in which a deficit in the second part of rapid growth (about 91–119 DAF) caused black beans in 11.41% of ripe fruits.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A continuous deficit (under 10 mm) from 57 to 80 DAF caused fruits to dry and drop, with losses of 10–40% after 80 DAF.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> These are single field results reported in a review, not general thresholds. Seeds that develop alone in a fruit, and bean shape, are covered in [Peaberries and bean shapes](/entries/peaberries-and-bean-shapes/).

## Pigments of ripening

The colour change has been measured in *C. arabica* var. Colombia at 1,400 m in Chinchiná, Colombia.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Total chlorophyll in the outer fruit tissues was 7.96–16.74 µg/g fresh weight from 182 to 203 DAF; from 210 DAF on, no chlorophyll values were reported.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Anthocyanins rose from 1.52 µg/g at the semi-ripe stage (210 DAF) to 8.26 µg/g when ripe (217 DAF) and 47.15 µg/g when overripe (224 DAF).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The 2006 review cites the same study with the values per gram of dry weight.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Fruit diameter in that study grew until 217 DAF.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

A 2024 study of Catimor peel found cyanidin-3-O-rutinoside, cyanidin-3-O-galactoside, cyanidin-3-O-sambubioside and cyanidin-3-O-glucoside as the main anthocyanins.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Carotenoids, above all lutein, coloured the peel in the green and green-yellow stages; anthocyanins dominated once it turned red.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> For yellow-fruited cultivars the sources differ: the 2025 review names leucoanthocyanin in place of anthocyanin, the 2024 study carotenoids (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Stage timing in other regions.** The stage limits in the two reviews draw on earlier field studies they cite; the one Brazilian growth curve consulted here ran about 10 weeks later.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Fruit development in canephora and liberica.** The sources consulted give total durations for these species but no stage-by-stage account.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Yellow fruit colour.** Whether yellow-fruited arabica lacks anthocyanins because of leucoanthocyanin or because carotenoids dominate is not settled in the sources consulted.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Maternal control of bean traits.** The 2006 review suggests that the maternal genome, through the perisperm, could be in part responsible for physical traits of the bean, though it notes that no such maternal effect had been observed in arabica or robusta breeding programmes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>De Castro & Marraccini, 2006 – Cytology of coffee fruit development.</strong> A review of how the coffee fruit and seed form, from the perisperm that fills the young seed to the endosperm that replaces it and hardens. It also gathers data on colour change, storage compounds and gene expression, and compares development times across species.</p>
<p class="meta">Brazilian Journal of Plant Physiology 18(1), 175–199 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1590/S1677-04202006000100013">doi:10.1590/S1677-04202006000100013</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>DaMatta et al., 2007 – Ecophysiology of coffee growth and production.</strong> A review of how the coffee plant grows and yields under different climates. Its section on fruit growth sets out the five stages with weeks after flowering and explains why water during rapid expansion decides bean size.</p>
<p class="meta">Brazilian Journal of Plant Physiology 19(4), 485–510 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1590/S1677-04202007000400014">doi:10.1590/S1677-04202007000400014</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Geromel et al., 2006 – Sucrose metabolism in the developing arabica fruit.</strong> Followed field-grown IAPAR 59 fruits from pinhead to maturity, separating pericarp, perisperm and endosperm. Dates when the endosperm appears and overtakes the perisperm, and tracks starch, sugars and the enzymes behind sucrose storage.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Experimental Botany 57(12), 3243–3258 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/jxb/erl084">doi:10.1093/jxb/erl084</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Geromel et al., 2008 – Fruit development in <em>C. racemosa</em>.</strong> Studied a wild species whose fruit matures in about two months. The perisperm is replaced within the first 40 days, which makes the species a contrast to arabica for studying seed filling.</p>
<p class="meta">Annals of Applied Biology 152(2), 179–187 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1744-7348.2007.00199.x">doi:10.1111/j.1744-7348.2007.00199.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Marín-López et al., 2003 – Ripening of var. Colombia.</strong> Measured size, weight, pigments and cup quality of fruit picked at eight stages from 182 to 231 days after flowering. Chlorophyll fell and anthocyanins rose sharply in the last two weeks; the best cups came from semi-ripe to overripe fruit.</p>
<p class="meta">Cenicafé 54(3), 208–225 · <a href="https://biblioteca.cenicafe.org/bitstream/10778/265/1/arc054(03)208-225.pdf">PDF</a></p>
</div>
