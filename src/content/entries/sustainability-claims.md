## What counts as an environmental claim

An environmental claim is any voluntary message that states or implies that a product, brand or trader has a positive or zero environmental impact.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It also covers messages that something is less damaging than others, or has improved over time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Text, images, symbols, brand names and spoken words all count.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Information that the law requires is not an environmental claim.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Greenwashing is the everyday word for environmental claims that mislead. The EU and Switzerland do not ban claims as such. They ban claims that are vague, unproven or based on the wrong kind of evidence.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The EU rules also cover social claims, such as fair working conditions or ethical commitments.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The rules matter to the coffee trade because claims are common in it. In a 2020 study of 513 coffee companies, a third reported no commitment to sustainability and another third reported only vague commitment.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The authors found indications of potential greenwashing by some companies.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

This entry describes what the legal texts and official guidance say, as of October 2026. It is not legal advice.

## What the EU rules ban

The EU Directive on empowering consumers for the green transition is Directive (EU) 2024/825.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It amends the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, the EU's general law against misleading business-to-consumer practices.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Member States had to adopt national rules by 27 March 2026 and apply them from 27 September 2026 (Article 4).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the European Commission's questions-and-answers document, the directive provides no transition period beyond that date.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The directive adds practices to the list of practices that are banned in all circumstances (Annex I).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Four of them matter most for coffee:

- **Generic environmental claims without proof of excellence.** A generic claim is a vague claim such as “eco-friendly”, “green” or “climate friendly”, with no specification on the same medium.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It is banned unless the trader can show recognised excellent environmental performance relevant to the claim (point 4a).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Neutral or reduced impact through offsetting.** Claiming that a product has a neutral, reduced or positive greenhouse gas impact because of offsetting is banned (point 4c).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The directive does not define offsetting; recital 12 refers to offsetting emissions outside the product's value chain.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Home-made sustainability labels.** A label that is not based on a certification scheme, and not set by a public authority, is banned (point 2a).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Future claims without a plan.** A claim about future environmental performance needs clear, verifiable commitments in a detailed, realistic implementation plan (Article 6(2)(d)).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> An independent third-party expert must check progress regularly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Two further bans concern the scope of a claim. A claim about the whole product or business is banned if it concerns only one aspect (point 4b).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Presenting a legal requirement for all products in a category as a special feature is also banned (point 10a).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The rules apply to dealings with consumers. Business-to-business practices, such as selling green coffee to a roaster, fall outside the directive.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Member States may extend protection to them in national law.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## What it means for bags, menus and websites

The directive covers any communication directly connected with promoting or selling a product to consumers.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The Commission does not list channels. For a roaster or café, bags, shelf cards, menus, websites and social media posts that promote coffee fit this description. A mandatory sustainability report is usually outside its scope; reusing its content in consumer marketing brings it back in.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The Commission names brand and product names as a risk. Terms such as “green”, “eco”, “natural” or “climate neutral” in a name may themselves be environmental claims.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Artwork can also count. A green leaf or water drop next to sustainability statements may be read as a trust mark.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Products already on the market are covered too. The Commission mentions stickers on old packaging and extra information at the point of sale as practical measures.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Authorities and courts may consider whether a trader made reasonable and proportionate efforts.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Certification labels remain possible. The directive defines a certification scheme by four conditions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It must be open to all traders on fair terms, and developed with experts and stakeholders.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It must also have procedures for non-compliance and independent third-party monitoring.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The directive does not list which schemes meet these conditions. The certifications entry describes what each coffee scheme requires.

Organic terms follow their own law. The Commission states that terms from the EU organic regulation, such as “bio” and “eco”, can be used to show compliance with organic rules.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> This holds even though they could otherwise count as generic environmental claims.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Switzerland

The FOEN guide notes that many Swiss companies also operate in the EU, and summarises EU law for them.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The Commission states that the EU rules apply whether traders or products come from inside or outside the EU.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A Swiss roaster selling to consumers in the EU is therefore subject to the EU rules there.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

In Switzerland, traders follow the Federal Act against Unfair Competition (UWG). Its general rule bans incorrect or misleading statements (Art. 3(1)(b)).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Since 1 January 2025, a separate rule covers the climate (Art. 3(1)(x)). Claims about the climate impact of a company or its products must be backed by objective and verifiable evidence.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> It entered into force together with an amendment to the CO₂ Act (Art. 39(4bis)).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The Federal Office for the Environment (FOEN, German BAFU) presented a guide on climate claims on 2 March 2026 (the document is dated January 2026).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It states that “climate neutral” is in practice not demonstrable and should not be used.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Product claims must not rest on compensation.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> For companies, compensation is possible only under strict conditions, with emission cuts first.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The guide sets out how the authorities read the law; claims under the UWG are decided mainly by the civil courts.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The Swiss rule targets climate claims, while the EU rules also cover other environmental and social claims.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> On product claims based on compensation, the two lead to the same result.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Offsetting, insetting and carbon removal

These three terms describe where an emission cut or a removal happens, and who counts it.

- **Offsetting** means counting emission cuts or removals from outside the value chain against one's own emissions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> These usually come as carbon credits, which are obtained and sold within defined standards.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Insetting** means that a company reduces emissions, or funds removals, within its own supply chain.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In a 2026 study of the UK soil carbon market, agri-food businesses gave their main suppliers incentives to change farming practices.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The study describes insetting as experimental, with little consistency between companies.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Carbon dioxide removal** means taking carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere and storing it durably.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> According to the IPCC, it is not a substitute for deep emission cuts.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

For coffee, insetting could mean shade trees planted on the farms that supply a roaster. The figure shows the difference that the EU rules draw.

## Generic claims and coffee

For coffee, the exception in point 4a is narrow. The EU Ecolabel is one route to recognised excellent environmental performance.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The Commission's EU Ecolabel page (accessed 13 October 2026) states that it is not intending to develop EU Ecolabel criteria for food and feed products.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The other routes are national or regional type I ecolabels under EN ISO 14024, or top performance under other Union law.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study or official source consulted for this entry names such a route for coffee.

The Commission also treats generic carbon-neutrality claims as generic environmental claims.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> They are therefore banned unless recognised excellent environmental performance is shown.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> If they rest on offsetting, point 4c bans them in any case.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

A specified claim is not generic. The Commission gives “100% of energy used to produce this packaging comes from renewable sources” as an example of a specific claim.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Whether such a claim is permitted depends on the circumstances.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Specific claims remain subject to the general rules on misleading actions and omissions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Sector law takes precedence where it conflicts with the directive.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The Commission applies this to organic terms under Regulation (EU) 2018/848.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## The offsetting rule in detail

Recital 12 of the directive explains point 4c. Claims of a neutral, reduced or positive impact should only be allowed when they are based on the actual life-cycle impact of the product.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> They should not be based on offsetting greenhouse gas emissions outside the product's value chain.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same recital states that companies may still advertise their investments in environmental initiatives, including carbon credit projects.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The Commission adds three points.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> First, point 4c does not apply to offsetting claims at company level; those remain subject to the other provisions of the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Second, a product may be described as having a “reduced CO₂ impact” if this is specified clearly and prominently on the same medium.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Third, it cites the examples given in recital 12, such as “climate neutral”, “CO₂ neutral certified” and “carbon positive”.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The Commission's document presents the preliminary views of its services. Only the Court of Justice of the EU can interpret EU law authoritatively.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Future performance claims

A claim such as “net zero by 2035” is a claim about future environmental performance. Article 6(2)(d) treats it as misleading unless four things are in place.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

1. Clear, objective, publicly available and verifiable commitments.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
2. A detailed and realistic implementation plan with measurable, time-bound targets.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
3. Regular verification by an independent third-party expert.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
4. The expert's findings made available to consumers.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The Commission suggests annual or biennial reviews as good practice.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The Swiss guide adds that a future claim must say clearly that it describes a future state.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A net-zero target must also rest on an emission reduction path in line with Swiss climate targets.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Swiss rules in detail

Art. 3(1)(x) UWG makes it unfair to make climate-impact claims that cannot be substantiated by objective and verifiable evidence.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> According to the FOEN guide, this shifts the burden of proof to the company making the claim.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The revised CO₂ Act (Art. 39(4bis)) also lets the FOEN provide the basis and standards for determining the climate impact of companies and products.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The guide's main positions:<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

- “Climate neutral” is, as of 2026, in practice not demonstrable for a company or a product.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> “CO₂-neutral” and “greenhouse-gas-neutral” are in practice excluded for product claims.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- A company may claim CO₂-neutrality only if all its hard-to-avoid emissions are fully balanced by negative emissions.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- A company may state that it funds reductions outside its value chain as a contribution, without relating them to its own emissions. Such a contribution supports no further claim, and its context must be explained.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- Companies give priority to reductions within the company and its value chain.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Certificates must not prevent, delay or replace those reductions.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- A certificate from abroad can support a claim such as “net zero” only if the host country has authorised transfer of the effect. This is the corresponding adjustment under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- “Climate-friendly” and “CO₂-reduced” product claims need a clear explanation of their scope. They must not rest on compensation outside the value chain.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A “CO₂-reduced” claim must state the reference point and the size of the reduction.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- Third-party verification before a claim is not required. A company that skips it carries the risk that the claim is found unfair.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Enforcement runs mainly through the civil courts.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Competitors and consumer organisations can sue.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The Confederation, represented by the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO), can sue where the economic interests of several people are affected.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Intentional unfair conduct under Article 3 can also be prosecuted, on complaint, under Article 23 UWG.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The Swiss Commission for Fairness in Commercial Communication, the industry's self-regulatory body, also judges cases.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Accounting standards for removals

Two voluntary standards set out how companies may count land-based removals. Neither is law, and neither decides what a product label may say.

The Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi) guidance for forest, land and agriculture (FLAG) targets covers emissions and removals.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> It requires removals and emission reductions to be accounted for and reported separately.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Removals count only within the boundaries of the FLAG target; the guidance states that offsets are not acceptable removals.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> It lists coffee among the commodities that drive deforestation.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The Greenhouse Gas Protocol published its Land Sector and Removals Standard on 30 January 2026; it takes effect on 1 January 2027.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Version 1.1, published on 30 June 2026, is the version to use, as of October 2026.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> It covers agriculture and CO₂ removal technologies; forestry is excluded from this version.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Companies must report removals separately from emissions, by accounting category.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> For removals, companies must establish physical traceability to the sourcing region or harvested area.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> They must also monitor continued storage and account for reversals.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Companies that report the impact of projects and interventions must report it separately from the physical inventory.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Storage and permanence

According to the IPCC, carbon stored in vegetation, buildings and soils is held for decades to centuries.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In soils and sediments it can be held for centuries to millennia, and in geological formations for 10,000 years or more.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Storage in vegetation, soils or sediments lasts for less time and can be more vulnerable.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The IPCC's removal definition does not include natural removal, such as the growth of natural forests.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

This matters for coffee wherever insetting relies on trees and soils, as in the shade-tree example above. A 2026 study of the UK soil carbon market notes that insetting schemes have been critiqued for often lacking independent verification.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Citing earlier work, the authors state that issues with additionality and permanence would likely prove persistent.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## The Green Claims proposal

The Commission adopted a proposal for a Green Claims Directive on 22 March 2023.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It would have required explicit environmental claims to be based on an assessment, with verification before use by an accredited independent body.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It also proposed a validation procedure for new private labelling schemes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The European Parliament adopted its first-reading position on 12 March 2024, and the Council its general approach on 17 June 2024.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> On 20 June 2025, the Commission announced that it intended to withdraw the proposal.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The trilogue scheduled for 23 June 2025 was cancelled.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The Commission's 2026 work programme, adopted on 21 October 2025, lists the proposal as pending.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> As of October 2026, no source consulted for this entry records a formal withdrawal. The proposal is not law.

## Company claims in the coffee sector

A 2020 study coded the sustainability efforts of a random sample of 513 companies in the global coffee sector.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A third reported tangible commitments, a third vague commitments and a third none.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In that sample, 9% of the companies claimed to be “sustainable” while adopting only three or fewer sustainability practices and no external standard.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The authors call for a transparent, mandatory reporting framework.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The study predates the EU and Swiss rules described here.

## Open questions

- **Routes to recognised excellent performance for coffee.** The Commission is not intending to develop EU Ecolabel criteria for food, and no source consulted for this entry names another recognised route for coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Case law under the new rules.** As of October 2026, the Commission's guidance states preliminary views, and claims under the Swiss UWG are decided mainly by the civil courts.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> How courts read terms such as “recognised excellent environmental performance” is not yet established.
- **Insetting claims on products.** Within-value-chain removals in coffee, such as shade trees, store carbon in vegetation and soils, where storage is shorter and more vulnerable than in geological formations.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> How such removals can support a product claim under the life-cycle rule is not settled in the sources consulted.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>West et al., 2023 – Forest-conservation offsets.</strong> Compared 26 forest-conservation project sites in six countries on three continents with synthetic control areas. Most projects had not significantly reduced deforestation, and those that had reduced it substantially less than claimed. The authors trace the gap to project baselines that did not match observed deforestation in the control areas.</p>
<p class="meta">Science 381(6660), 873–877 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ade3535">doi:10.1126/science.ade3535</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bager & Lambin, 2020 – Sustainability strategies in coffee.</strong> Coded the sustainability practices of a random sample of 513 companies in the global coffee sector. Commitments split into thirds: none, vague and tangible. The authors found indications of potential greenwashing and called for common indicators and mandatory reporting.</p>
<p class="meta">Business Strategy and the Environment 29, 3555–3570 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/bse.2596">doi:10.1002/bse.2596</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Simmonds et al., 2026 – Offsetting and insetting in soil carbon.</strong> Studied how the UK voluntary soil carbon market is forming, through offsetting and insetting in agri-food supply chains, using 17 interviews and a validation workshop. They describe insetting as experimental and inconsistent between companies. Citing earlier work, they note criticism that insetting often lacks independent verification and that additionality and permanence remain open issues.</p>
<p class="meta">Environmental Policy and Governance (early view) · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/eet.70128">doi:10.1002/eet.70128</a></p>
</div>
