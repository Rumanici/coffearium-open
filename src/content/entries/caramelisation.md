## What caramelisation is

Caramelisation is the browning of sugars by heat alone. A classic 1953 review of browning defines it as sugars heated to relatively high temperatures without amino compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2022 food chemistry paper uses the same definition for aldose and ketose sugars, such as glucose and fructose.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The Maillard reaction is the other main type of non-enzymatic browning.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It differs in one point: it needs amino compounds, such as amino acids, as well as sugars.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Green coffee contains both sugars and amino acids, so the two reactions run side by side in a roast.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

In coffee, both reactions are named as the main sources of flavour and colour.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to a roasting chemistry chapter, caramelisation gives caramel-like and seasoning-like notes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The same chapter says aroma forms more readily by the Maillard route, because amino acids lower the energy the reaction needs.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## The sugar in green coffee

Sucrose is the main free sugar in green coffee. A 2000 study gives 3–8% of the green bean.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Arabica green coffee contains more sucrose than robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Glucose and fructose are present only in small amounts.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Sucrose is made of one glucose and one fructose unit. It must split into these two before it reacts readily.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The amounts of glucose and fructose therefore rise early in the roast, as sucrose breaks down.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## What happens to the sugar during a roast

Almost all of it disappears. In a 2019 study, sucrose fell from 70.1 mg/g (dry weight) in the green beans to 3.0 mg/g at the end of a medium roast.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In both roasts studied, 96–98% of the sucrose was gone.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A roasting chemistry chapter says almost all free sugars are lost through the Maillard reaction and caramelisation.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

In the reported values, the loss was slow up to minute 6 and fastest between minutes 6 and 9.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The chart shows both roasts of that study.

The lost sugar turns into many other things, by both routes:

- **Acids.** Sucrose is the main green-coffee source of formic, acetic, glycolic and lactic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Aroma compounds.** These include furans and the caramel-like enolones.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Water and carbon dioxide.** Both are released as the sugars react.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Brown polymers.** Caramelisation and the Maillard reaction are the main routes to them.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Common misunderstandings

- **“Roasting caramelises the sugars in the bean.”** Caramelisation is only one of two routes. The sugars are broken down into acids, aroma compounds, water, carbon dioxide and brown polymers, by both routes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **“Caramelised sugar makes the coffee sweet.”** A 2020 study found no identifiable simple sugars in its brews. Yet the panel found significant differences in sweetness.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The authors call a sugar-based sweetness unlikely and suggest, as a hypothesis, sweet-smelling compounds instead.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The compounds they name, two buttery, caramel-like diketones, come from sugar fragments.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> So sugar breakdown may still matter for sweetness, through aroma rather than sugar. According to a June 2024 Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) document, research is in progress on whether this sweetness is a taste or a smell effect.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **“There is a caramelisation phase.”** A Specialty Coffee Association handbook speaks of a “caramelization phase”.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> A roasting textbook chapter says caramelisation stops once a roast enters second crack.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured caramelisation on its own in the bean. Sources disagree on when it begins (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **“Caramelisation and the Maillard reaction are the same.”** They are not: in food chemistry, caramelisation is defined by the absence of amino compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## The chemistry

**Reactions.** The 1953 review names 1,2-enolisation, sugar dehydration to furfurals and sugar fission as the known caramelisation reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In acidic systems, sugar dehydration gives furfurals.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2025 systematic review of caramel model studies describes caramelisation as isomerisation, epimerisation, dehydration and oxidation through enolisation.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It names furans, furfural, 5-methylfurfural and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) as furanic products of sugar dehydration.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**HMF.** HMF forms when 3-deoxyglucosone loses two molecules of water; glucose and fructose both reach HMF through such a dicarbonyl intermediate.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In caramel models, low pH promotes sugar breakdown and HMF formation, with sharp rises between pH 1 and 5.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> HMF is not specific to caramelisation: the 2022 paper studied it in a system that also contained an amino acid.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Temperature and time.** Food chemistry gives no single caramelisation temperature. The caramel models in the 2025 review were made at 50–240 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2011 study of crystalline sucrose found that it loses its structure by thermal decomposition, which depends on both temperature and time. It concluded that caramelisation can occur well below the reported melting temperature of sucrose, given enough time.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In the bean, the onset is disputed (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Acids from sugar alone

The clearest coffee evidence for a sugar-only route comes from a 2000 study of the four aliphatic acids. Robusta green beans were soaked in sucrose, glucose or fructose, dried back to about 10% moisture (basis not stated) and roasted for 3 min at 150–280 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Sucrose fell rapidly in every series and was not detectable after roasting at 280 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The pure sugars were then heated with sea sand (1 : 10) at 240 °C for 15 min, with no amino acid present.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> They gave formic, acetic, glycolic and lactic acid, in amounts largely independent of the sugar used.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Arabinose, erythrose and 1,6-anhydroglucose formed as intermediates and gave the same acids when heated themselves.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Under argon, sucrose gave similar acid profiles, so oxygen played no measurable role.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Adding glycine, an amino acid, to sucrose lowered the yield of formic acid markedly.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Carbon-13 labelling traced the acids through endiols and deoxyglucosones.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The authors concluded that the acids form readily from the pure carbohydrates, so reactions with other bean components play no key role.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In the bean, both routes run at once; the roasting chemistry chapter attributes formic and acetic acid to the Maillard reaction and caramelisation together.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Their sensory role is covered under acids in coffee.

## Furans and HMF in the roast

The 2019 time-course study also followed HMF. It stayed below 0.010 mg/g until minute 5 and then rose.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It passed through two maxima in each roast: 0.281 and 0.346 mg/g at 9 and 13 min in the medium roast, and 0.341 and 0.549 mg/g at 9 and 12 min in the full city roast.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The authors suggest the two maxima could correspond to first and second crack, which were not recorded.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> After the second maximum HMF fell again; the authors attribute this to its further breakdown.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Several sugar-derived compounds depend on how much sucrose the green coffee holds. According to the roasting chemistry chapter, roasted arabica has more diketones, furfurals and cyclic enolones such as furaneol than robusta, because its green beans hold more sucrose.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The chapter also names furan among the undesirable products of Maillard-type reactions that roasting should keep low.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Furan and other process contaminants are covered under roasting contaminants.

## Caramel notes and sweetness in the cup

**Sugar in the brew.** A 2020 development-time study found no identifiable simple sugars in its brews by NMR. Any carbohydrate was below 0.5 mM.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The authors note that 1 mmol/L of glucose or fructose would have been detected, and that taste recognition thresholds of sugars are generally above 20 mmol/L.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> They suggest that the buttery, caramel-like diketones 2,3-butanedione and 2,3-pentanedione may partly explain the sweetness the panel perceived.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Caramel-like aroma and sweetness.** In a 2019 roast defect study, maltol, a caramel-like compound, was among the compounds that increased most in the baked roast compared with the normal roast.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In the study's multivariate map, maltol loaded on the same dimension as bitter and burnt, while sweet loaded strongly in the opposite direction.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Roast degree.** In a sensory analysis of eight roasting studies, darker roasts went with more bitterness and less sweetness, acidity and fruitiness.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The authors trace most coffee flavour, including sweet and caramel aromas, to non-enzymatic browning such as the Maillard reaction and caramelisation.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## How hard the two routes are to separate

- **Shared intermediates.** Sugar dehydration and sugar fragmentation are steps of the Maillard scheme as well as caramelisation reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Shared products.** Enolones, aliphatic acids and brown polymers come from both routes in coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Model systems.** Sugar-only experiments show what sugars can do alone.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The roasting chemistry chapter warns that the whole bean acts as a pressurised reactor, so model results cannot simply be applied to it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Amino acids change the yields.** Glycine lowered formic acid yields from sucrose.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In the bean, where amino acids are present, the sugar-only yields of model systems need not apply.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Composition by difference.** A composition table in the roasting chemistry chapter gives a single line, “caramelization/condensation products”, calculated by difference rather than measured.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

No study consulted for this entry measured how much of the sucrose in a bean is lost to caramelisation and how much to the Maillard reaction.

## Measuring it

- **Sugars.** Sucrose, glucose and fructose are followed by liquid or ion chromatography in samples taken through a roast.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In brews, NMR showed no detectable simple sugars in one study.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Furanic markers.** A 2025 systematic review compares the analytical methods used for HMF and related furans in caramel models.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In coffee, HMF was followed each minute from minute 4 through two roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Spiked beans and labelled sugars.** Green beans enriched with a sugar, and sugars labelled with carbon-13, show which products come from sugar.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to the roasting chemistry chapter, the bean itself can serve as the reaction vessel in such experiments.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Share of each route.** How much sucrose is lost to caramelisation and how much to the Maillard reaction in a real bean has not been measured in the sources consulted.
- **Onset temperature in the bean.** The 130 °C and 180–200 °C figures are background statements, not measurements.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Sugar studies show that time matters as well as temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Meaning of the HMF maxima.** The link to first and second crack is the authors’ suggestion; crack times were not recorded.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Sweetness.** Whether coffee sweetness is a taste or a cross-modal effect of sweet-smelling compounds was, according to an SCA document of June 2024, still being researched.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Hodge, 1953 – Chemistry of browning reactions.</strong> Brings browning chemistry into one scheme. Separates browning with amino compounds (the Maillard reaction) from caramelisation, defined as sugars heated without them, and names enolisation, dehydration to furfurals and sugar fission as caramelisation reactions.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 1(15), 928–943 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf60015a004">doi:10.1021/jf60015a004</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Ginz et al., 2000 – Acids from sugars.</strong> Robusta beans enriched with sugars were roasted, and pure sugars were heated with sand, with and without glycine. Sugars alone gave formic, acetic, glycolic and lactic acid, sucrose was confirmed as their main green-bean precursor, and glycine lowered the formic acid yield.</p>
<p class="meta">European Food Research and Technology 211(6), 404–410 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s002170000215">doi:10.1007/s002170000215</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Lee et al., 2011 – Sucrose decomposes rather than melts.</strong> Heated crystalline sucrose was analysed by HPLC at fast and slow heating rates. Glucose and HMF appeared as the structure began to break down, showing that thermal decomposition, governed by time and temperature, drives the change.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 59(2), 702–712 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf104235d">doi:10.1021/jf104235d</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Diviš et al., 2019 – Composition minute by minute.</strong> Followed sucrose, HMF and organic acids in a Brazilian arabica through a medium and a full city roast in a home roaster. Sucrose was 96–98% degraded by the end, and HMF passed through two maxima in each roast.</p>
<p class="meta">Potravinarstvo Slovak Journal of Food Sciences 13(1), 344–350 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.5219/1062">doi:10.5219/1062</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Alstrup et al., 2020 – Sweetness without sugar.</strong> Four roasts of one Colombian coffee to the same colour, with different development times. The panel found significant differences in sweetness, but NMR found no simple sugars in the brews; buttery, caramel-like diketones are proposed as a partial explanation.</p>
<p class="meta">Beverages 6(4), 70 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/beverages6040070">doi:10.3390/beverages6040070</a></p>
</div>
