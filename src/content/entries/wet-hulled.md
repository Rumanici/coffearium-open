## What it is

Wet hulling – *giling basah* in Indonesian – is a way of processing Arabica in which the parchment is taken off while the bean is still wet, and the naked bean is dried afterwards.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In the washed process and in dry hulling, the parchment coffee is dried first, to about 12%, and only then hulled.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In the trade the process is also called "semi-washed"<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>, but the same term is used elsewhere for other processes: the FAO uses it for coffee whose mucilage is removed by a demucilager straight after pulping, without fermentation<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>, and the Codex code of practice for green coffee from pulped beans dried within their mucilaginous parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> This entry uses "wet-hulled" only.

The method is used in parts of Indonesia, above all Sumatra.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Mandheling coffee from the highlands around Lake Toba in North Sumatra is made this way by smallholder farmers.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It is also used in the Gayo highlands of Aceh<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> and in Sulawesi.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> One importer reports that some buyers now ask for wet-hulled coffee from places such as Bali and Flores, which did not traditionally use it.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## How it is done

1. **Pulping and fermentation** – the farmer pulps the cherries and ferments the pulped coffee, then washes it the next day.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Importers describe the fermentation as overnight, in bags, tubs or tanks.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In some areas the pulped seeds are instead held with their mucilage in tanks or sacks until delivery.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
2. **Brief drying in parchment** – the wet parchment is sun-dried only briefly: half a day in an FAO trial, 1–2 days in North Sumatran practice.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
3. **Sale and holding** – the farmer sells the wet parchment to a collector.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In North Sumatra it may be stored for up to a few weeks before hulling; in a 2004/05 trial<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> the stored parchment held 41.74–53.96% moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
4. **Wet hulling** – a huller, usually owned by the mill, takes off the parchment while the bean is still wet: at 40–45% moisture in the Mandheling trial, 35–45% (huller) or 40–50% (practice) in the FAO report.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The bean that comes out is swollen with water and white<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> or whitish-green.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
5. **Drying the naked bean** – the green bean is dried straight after hulling, to limit mould,<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> down to about 12% moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It then goes through the usual sorting before export.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Why it arose

The sources give two reasons. The first is climate. Rain and humidity are high in the growing regions, which makes it hard to dry parchment coffee fully; taking the parchment off early reduces the mass and shortens drying.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The second is money. Smallholders can sell wet parchment soon after harvest, so cash comes in quickly at every link of the chain.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> One importer adds market access and local tradition to the list.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

The structure of the trade follows from this. Farmers process small lots, collectors buy and gather the wet parchment, and the miller owns the wet huller.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In some places collectors also do the pulping and fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Appearance and cup

**Green coffee.** Wet-hulled coffee is dark, opal-green, with little silverskin left on the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Because the bean is soft when it passes through the huller, many beans come out with crushed or split ends.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Cup.** Mandheling coffee is described as low in acidity, with a heavy, complex body and spicy, slightly earthy and fruity flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A trial in Gayo found the same pattern – low acidity and strong body – and tasters preferred wet-hulled over dry-hulled coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> One importer lists earthy, savoury, herbaceous and dark-chocolate notes<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>; another treats herbal, mossy and mushroomy notes as signs of faults in processing, though it notes that a slight green-herb note can be pleasant.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The authors of the North Sumatran trial suggest that the handling itself contributes to this character.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Moisture and mechanics of hulling

In a fully washed chain the parchment comes off at about 12% moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In wet hulling it comes off at about 35–50%, roughly three to four times the moisture value.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> At that point the bean is soft and swollen rather than hard and glassy.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A standard huller does not work on such coffee without changes: in the FAO project the steel huller bar was replaced by a rubber strip, and the machine was given more power and speed.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The price of this is mechanical damage. Soft beans are crushed or split at the ends in the huller.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In a Brazilian study of Arabica hulled after drying, mechanical damage from hulling lowered quality in storage for both natural and washed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In the North Sumatran storage trial, the first visible mould was on naked and broken beans, in one lot on day 3.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Drying without parchment

After wet hulling, the bean dries with no parchment around it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Importers note that this leaves it exposed to swings in relative humidity and to contamination from dirt, debris and animals while it dries on tarpaulins.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Some is dried on patios, on the road or on bare dirt.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The Codex code of practice warns against both: bare soil is not appropriate for drying in rainy areas, and rewetting favours rapid fungal growth and OTA.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Moulds and ochratoxin A

The weakest point of the chain is the wet parchment waiting for the huller. In a 2004/05 trial in North Sumatra, six lots of wet parchment from farmers and collectors were stored for 1, 7 and 14 days in sacks before wet hulling.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> When mould became visible, the parchment was spread on a plastic sheet inside the warehouse.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Mould.** The FAO report states that infection clearly increased with storage time<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>; the journal abstract found no clear trend and points to differences between lots (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Black *Aspergillus* dominated; *Aspergillus ochraceus* was found at a low infection rate of 0–15.3%.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Ochre-group fungi were found in 5 of the 6 lots stored for two weeks, and not at the shorter storage times.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **OTA.** OTA was found only at low levels, below the European Union limit according to the journal report.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Most of the positive samples came from parchment stored wet for 14 days.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The exact counts differ between the two reports of the trial (see Contested).
- **Cup.** Longer wet storage led to more musty flavours in the green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Even so, the cup of many samples stored wet for up to two weeks was better than the authors expected.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Chemistry and sensory studies

Comparative chemistry is thin. No study consulted for this entry measured sugars, acids, lipids or volatile compounds side by side in wet-hulled and fully washed coffee from the same lots. What exists is sensory:

- In the North Sumatran trial, coffee stored wet for one day, hulled fast and dried fast to 12% was most likely to show the Mandheling character, and three of four tasters picked a sample of this kind as having the best Mandheling character.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> All tasters used the term "fruity/fermented", which the report expected to be the easiest and commonest negative character to identify.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- Tasters also found some trial samples too clean in the cup, with less complex body and too green in colour compared with the traditional Mandheling they expected.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- In the Gayo highlands, beans were dried in two stages for wet hulling – partly before hulling, to a moisture within the range given by the North Sumatran reports, and again after it – or dried fully in one stage for dry hulling.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Wet-hulled coffee scored better in the organoleptic test and was preferred.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Where the flavour comes from.** The authors of the Mandheling trial thought the handling probably contributes to the cup character<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>, but no source consulted for this entry separates the effect of wet hulling itself from wet storage, fermentation, drying on the ground, and defects.
- **The seed's own metabolism.** Seed metabolism during processing affects sugars in washed coffee (see the washed process entry). No study consulted for this entry follows the living seed through wet hulling, when the bean loses its parchment at high moisture.
- **Best moisture at hulling.** The FAO and trial reports give about 35–50%<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>, and trade descriptions vary more widely, some well below that range.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The trade-off between damage, drying time and mould has not been measured.
- **History.** The sources consulted for this entry do not give a primary, dated account of when wet hulling spread in Indonesia.

<p><strong>Abreu et al., 2023 – Hulling damage and storage.</strong> Studied how hulling and storage conditions affect green coffee quality. In Brazilian Arabica hulled after drying, mechanical damage from hulling, together with the loss of the fruit layers, lowered quality during storage – relevant to wet hulling, where the bean is hulled soft and then dried and stored naked, though wet hulling itself was not tested.</p>
