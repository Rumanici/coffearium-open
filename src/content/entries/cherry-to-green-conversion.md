## Where the weight goes

A coffee cherry is mostly not green coffee. Throughout processing the crop loses weight, through the loss of water and the removal of mucilage and parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Pulping also takes off the skin and pulp, which make up about 44% of the fresh fruit's weight.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The trade measures this loss as a ratio. A ratio of 6:1 means that 6 kg of fresh cherry give 1 kg of clean (green) coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> For washed coffee, the ratio is often stated as about 5:1, but in Ethiopia it is usually about 6:1.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A Colombian study measured 6.23:1 for washed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

A conversion factor is the same idea used as a multiplier. The International Coffee Organization (ICO) turns parchment coffee into its green-coffee equivalent by multiplying its weight by 0.80, and dried cherry by multiplying by 0.50.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Step by step in the washed process

Cenicafé, the Colombian coffee research centre, weighed coffee at every stage of washed processing in Caldas, Colombia. For selected ripe cherry harvested in 2005–2006, 100 kg of fresh cherry gave:<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

1. **Pulped coffee, still in its mucilage** – about 55 kg. The pulper removed about 43–44 kg of fresh pulp (44.02% of the fruit by weight; conversion factor 0.43).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
2. **Washed coffee** – about 39 kg, after the mucilage was fermented off and washed away.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Mucilage is about 13% of the cherry's weight.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Washed coffee holds 52.7–53.5% moisture (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
3. **Dry parchment coffee** – about 20 kg, dried to 10–12% moisture (wet basis).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> This step removes mostly water.
4. **Green coffee** – about 16 kg, once the parchment is hulled off.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

So the washed chain loses about four-fifths of the cherry's weight before drying ends, and the last fifth of the parchment's weight at hulling.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Specialty chains that sort hard lose more: five Ethiopian cooperatives worked at 8:1 or higher, up to 11:1, when producing specialty coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Natural, honey and pulped natural

**Natural.** The whole cherry is dried, so the skin, pulp and mucilage leave only at hulling. The standard ratios are 3:1 from fresh to sun-dried cherry and 2:1 from dried cherry to clean coffee – 6:1 overall.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The ICO factor for dried cherry, 0.50, says the same as the 2:1 ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Per 100 kg of fresh cherry, the standard ratios give about 33 kg of dried cherry and 17 kg of green coffee (our arithmetic). Freshly harvested coffee holds about 50–60% moisture (wet basis); safe storage needs 10–12%.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Honey and pulped natural.** These start like the washed process: the pulp comes off. The mucilage, or part of it, stays on and dries with the parchment. Mucilage is about 13% of the cherry's fresh weight.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry measured how much of that mass stays on the dry parchment. None measured the cherry-to-green ratio of honey or pulped natural coffee either.

## Arabica and robusta

Measured yields differ between the species, but the figures come from different studies, places and processes (see Open questions).

A 2025 Brazilian study measured robusta cultivars in the Amazon region. On average, 100 kg of fresh fruit gave 24.68 kg of green coffee at 12% moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The best cultivar needed only 3.57 kg of fresh fruit per kg of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> For washed arabica, the Colombian study found 6.23 kg.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Calculating weight loss on drying

Drying removes water but leaves the dry matter. So the mass after drying can be calculated from the moisture before and after.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The moisture must be on a wet basis: water as a share of the whole wet mass.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

- **Mass after drying:** m₂ = m₁ × (100 − M₁) ÷ (100 − M₂)<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Water removed:** m₁ − m₂

Here m₁ and m₂ are the masses before and after, and M₁ and M₂ the moisture contents in per cent (wet basis).

**Example.** Cenicafé assumes 53% moisture (wet basis) for washed coffee entering the dryer and 11% for dry coffee. It states that the coffee is dry when its mass has fallen by 47.3%.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The formula gives the same: 100 kg × (100 − 53) ÷ (100 − 11) = 52.8 kg, a fall of 47.2% (our arithmetic).

## Fresh weight and dry matter

The share of a fruit layer depends on the basis. Pulp is about 43–44% of the fresh fruit's weight,<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> but only about 30% of its dry matter.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Fresh pulp holds about 80% moisture (basis not stated),<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> washed parchment coffee about 53% (wb),<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> so the pulp's share shrinks on a dry basis.

The dry matter is also a check on measured ratios. In the Cenicafé data, 39.1 kg of washed coffee per 100 kg of cherry at 53% moisture holds 18.4 kg of dry matter; 20.2 kg of dry parchment at 11% holds 18.0 kg (our arithmetic from the ratios and moisture values<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>). From washed coffee onwards, almost all the mass lost is water.

## Mass balance arithmetic

All formulas use moisture in per cent; m is mass.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The wet and dry bases are explained under moisture and water activity.

| Quantity | Formula | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture, wet basis | M<sub>wb</sub> = 100 × water ÷ (water + dry matter) | Trade convention |
| Moisture, dry basis | M<sub>db</sub> = 100 × water ÷ dry matter | Used in sorption research |
| Conversion | M<sub>db</sub> = 100 × M<sub>wb</sub> ÷ (100 − M<sub>wb</sub>) | |
| Dry matter | m<sub>dry</sub> = m × (100 − M<sub>wb</sub>) ÷ 100 | Unchanged by drying |
| Mass after drying | m₂ = m₁ × (100 − M₁) ÷ (100 − M₂) | Wet basis only |
| Water removed | m₁ − m₂ | |
| Ratio to factor | factor = 1 ÷ ratio | 1.25:1 → 0.80 |
| Mass at a reference moisture | m<sub>ref</sub> = m × (100 − M) ÷ (100 − M<sub>ref</sub>) | Used to compare lots at 12% |

The FAO chapter illustrates the two bases with grain: if 100 kg of moist grain loses 20 kg of water on drying, it held 20% moisture on a wet basis and 25% on a dry basis.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The Brazilian robusta study used the reference-moisture correction to express every sample at 12%.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Worked examples

Each example uses published inputs; the arithmetic is ours.

1. **Wet to dry basis.** Washed coffee at 53% (wb)<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> holds 53 kg of water and 47 kg of dry matter per 100 kg. On a dry basis that is 100 × 53 ÷ 47 = 112.8% – more water than dry matter.
2. **Drying washed coffee.** 1,000 kg of washed coffee at 53% dried to 11% (wb)<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>: m₂ = 1,000 × 47 ÷ 89 = 528.1 kg of dry parchment. Water removed: 471.9 kg. Cenicafé's own figure from 280 kg of washed coffee is about 148 kg of dry parchment at 11%, the same proportion.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
3. **Over-drying costs weight.** Drying the same 1,000 kg to 10% instead of 11% gives 1,000 × 47 ÷ 90 = 522.2 kg, 5.9 kg less. Cenicafé lists losses per 1,000 kg of washed coffee of 5.9, 8.8, 11.6, 14.4 and 17.5 kg of dry parchment at 10, 9.5, 9, 8.5 and 8% moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The table does not state the reference moisture; the formula, measured against drying to 11%, reproduces the first four and gives 17.2 kg for the last.
4. **Cherry to green with factors.** 2,000 kg of selected ripe cherry × 0.20 = 400 kg of dry parchment; × 0.80 = 320 kg of green coffee. Cenicafé's direct factor from cherry to green, 0.16, gives the same 320 kg.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
5. **Natural coffee by standard ratios.** 1,200 kg of fresh cherry ÷ 3 = 400 kg of dried cherry; × 0.50 = 200 kg of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> By moisture alone, fruit at 60% dried to 12% (both wb) would weigh 1,200 × 40 ÷ 88 = 545 kg (see Open questions).
6. **Colombian factor de rendimiento.** Excelso (kg) = dry parchment (kg) × 70 ÷ factor. At the reference factor of 94,<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> 940 kg of dry parchment gives 700 kg of excelso – ten 70 kg bags. At 104.7, the mean of unclassified lots in the 2025 study, it gives 628.5 kg.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
7. **Correcting to a reference moisture.** A sample of 1.50 kg of green coffee at 14% moisture (wb) equals 1.50 × 86 ÷ 88 = 1.47 kg at 12% (wb), the reference used in the robusta study.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Trade yield measures

**ICO factors.** For its statistics, the ICO converts dried cherry to green coffee by multiplying by 0.50 and parchment coffee by multiplying by 0.80.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The same rules convert roasted coffee with 1.19 and soluble coffee with 2.6.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Colombia: factor de rendimiento.** In Colombia, dry parchment is valued by its milling yield, and a lower factor indicates higher quality and price.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Cenicafé defines the rendimiento en trilla as the quantity of dry parchment coffee needed for a 70 kg bag of excelso.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In 2008 the national base line for trade was 92.8 kg of dry parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2025 study gives the reference value as 94: 94 kg of dry parchment give 70 kg of excelso and 6.3 kg of pasilla.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Pasilla is the defective fraction sorted out of the green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Measured factors in 2008 were 90.75 for selected and 95.99 for unselected coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In 2025, the best value was 92.33 on the farm and 90.01 at Cenicafé's own processing plant.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Excelso and pasilla together make up 76.3 of the 94 kg, about 81% of the dry parchment (our arithmetic<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>). That is close to the factor of 0.80 for parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Volume measures.** Where harvests are counted in volume, bulk density converts volume to mass. At Cenicafé's 622 kg/m³ for fresh fruit, 1,000 L of cherry weigh about 622 kg (our arithmetic<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>). No official Brazilian reference for volume of field coffee per 60 kg bag was found in the sources consulted for this entry, so none is given.

## Bulk density along the chain

Bulk density – mass per volume of a heap, air gaps included – changes at every step. Cenicafé measured it by free fall (Colombian standard NTC 4607) for each state of the coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Pulped coffee in its mucilage is the densest heap, at 827 kg/m³; dry parchment the lightest coffee state, at 391 kg/m³.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Where the numbers vary

- **Sorting.** In the 2025 Colombian study, classifying at three points (fruit by density before pulping, pulped coffee by size, parchment by density in washing) raised the ratio from 4.33 to 5.87 kg of cherry per kg of dry parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The classified lots also needed less parchment per bag: a factor of 97.22 against 104.7.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Ripeness.** The robusta study set the outturn of unripe fruit at 19%, and its field outturn index, which allows for unripe fruit, was 22.57% against 24.68% for the plain index.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Specialty grading.** Rejecting defects, mostly at the later stages, pushed the Ethiopian cooperatives to 8:1–11:1.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Final moisture.** Each point of over-drying costs weight that is sold by the kilogram (see example 3).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Reference basis.** A ratio means little without the moisture of the end product. The robusta study corrected to 12%;<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> the Cenicafé ratios refer to parchment dried to 11–12%.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Open questions

- No source consulted for this entry measured cherry-to-green ratios for honey or pulped natural coffee.
- The standard 3:1 ratio from fresh to dried cherry<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> is larger than a pure water balance predicts. Fruit at 50–60% moisture (wet basis)<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> dried to 10–12% gives about 1.8–2.3:1 (1.76 for 50% → 12%, 2.25 for 60% → 10%; our arithmetic with the drying formula<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>); 3:1 would need fruit at about 70–71% moisture (wb), or losses beyond water. Which losses make up the gap is not shown in the sources consulted.
- How the 18% hulling loss and the 20% parchment share in the same Cenicafé table relate to each other is not explained in the source.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- Arabica and canephora were measured in different studies, places and processes. No study consulted for this entry compared both species under one protocol.
