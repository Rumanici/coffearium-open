## A natural cross on Timor

The Timor Hybrid is a coffee plant that arose without human help. A *C. arabica* plant and a *C. canephora* (robusta) plant crossed on the island of Timor.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Most sources date the plant to 1927.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> One study gives 1917 and says it grew in a plantation of Typica.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In Portuguese it is called Híbrido de Timor; papers shorten it to HDT or HT, and this entry uses HdT.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The plant came from the part of Timor that was then a Portuguese colony, now Timor-Leste.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the 1950s, its offspring were shown to be natural hybrids of the two species.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Most of them resisted all or some of the known races of coffee leaf rust.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A race is a form of the rust fungus that can overcome certain resistance genes.

Arabica itself is an old hybrid, with robusta as one of its two parents. How that happened is covered in Polyploidy and the arabica genome. The Timor Hybrid is a much younger, second meeting of the two species.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Why breeders chose it

Robusta carries ample resistance to leaf rust.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Bringing it into arabica through direct crosses, which give triploid hybrids, is a long route.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The Timor Hybrid has the same number of chromosomes as arabica, and crosses with arabica are fertile.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to a coffee science textbook, this made it the main source of resistance genes for most breeders.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

The resistance comes from robusta genes. Four rust resistance genes from robusta, SH6, SH7, SH8 and SH9, give the Timor Hybrid populations their resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The plant also carries a gene against coffee berry disease.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Some lines derived from it also resist the root-knot nematode *Meloidogyne exigua*.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> How the SH genes work against the fungus is explained in Coffee leaf rust.

The Timor Hybrid is not one uniform plant. Its accessions carry different numbers of resistance genes.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> In a 2009 Brazilian study, two dominant genes controlled the rust resistance of one accession, UFV 443-03.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

## From one plant to many varieties

Breeders cross the Timor Hybrid with a good arabica variety. They then select for several generations. The aim is to keep the robusta genes for resistance and lose the rest of the robusta traits.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The result is called an introgressed variety: arabica that carries some robusta DNA.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Catimor and Sarchimor

The coffee rust research centre in Portugal, the Centro de Investigação das Ferrugens do Cafeeiro (CIFC), built its breeding programme on the Timor Hybrid, starting in 1960.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It crossed two Timor Hybrid plants with dwarf arabica varieties.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Caturra × HdT CIFC 832/1 gave the Catimor population. Villa Sarchi × HdT CIFC 832/2 gave the Sarchimor population.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Colombia crossed Caturra with HdT CIFC 1343, and Brazil crossed Catuaí with HdT CIFC 2570.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Catimor and Sarchimor are not single varieties. Each is a group of distinct varieties with similar parentage.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> National programmes selected their own lines and gave them local names.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> As of 2017, these populations were the source of most rust-resistant varieties grown.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Resistance that wears off

Rust races that overcome the Timor Hybrid genes have appeared. Some commercial varieties derived from it are gradually losing their resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> World Coffee Research records that Costa Rica 95 and Lempira, both Catimors, have been confirmed susceptible to leaf rust in Costa Rica and Honduras.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In Brazil, a rust isolate infected Oeiras, a cultivar from Caturra × HdT CIFC 832/1 released as resistant to race II.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> A 2013 study found the rust in Brazil highly diverse, with no separate populations on different hosts.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The Coffee leaf rust entry follows the races and how resistance is lost.

## What was introgressed

Introgression here means that robusta DNA was moved into the arabica genome by crossing and backcrossing. Arabica has two subgenomes, one from *C. canephora* and one from *C. eugenioides*.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The 2024 arabica genome study sequenced five descendants of the original Timor Hybrid, obtained by backcrossing to arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Their robusta segments lay in large blocks, a sign of recent hybridisation, and covered 7–11% of the genome.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Introgression occurred almost exclusively within the subgenome from robusta. One introgressed region on chromosome 4 was shared by all Timor Hybrid lines.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Earlier work measured the same thing with markers. A 2000 study used AFLP markers (amplified fragment length polymorphism) on 19 introgression lines (BC1F4) and two Timor Hybrid accessions.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It found 178 markers that set the Timor Hybrid material apart from arabica. The introgressed markers in these genotypes were estimated at 9–29% of the *C. canephora* genome.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Introgression involved recombination within chromosomes, not only the replacement of whole chromosomes.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2020 Brazilian study with AFLP and SSR (microsatellite) markers concluded that introgression into the Timor Hybrid does not reach 30% of the *C. canephora* genome.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The figures do not measure the same quantity. The marker studies give a share of the robusta genome detected in the lines. The genome study gives the share of the arabica genome covered by robusta blocks.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Resistance genes and candidate loci

Nine rust resistance factors are known in coffee, SH1 to SH9.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2006 review states that SH6, SH7, SH8 and SH9 were found only in Timor Hybrid derivatives.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2017 review describes them as robusta-derived genes that confer the resistance of the Timor Hybrid populations and places them in the Timor Hybrid and other interspecific hybrids.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Accession HdT CIFC 832/2 carries additional introgressions compared with other genotypes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The genome study reanalysed published gene-expression data from a resistant Timor Hybrid line and a susceptible cultivar after inoculation with rust. It found 723 differentially expressed genes, most of them associated with defence.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In the shared introgressed region on chromosome 4 it names strong candidate genes: one homologue of *RPP8*, six of *CPR1* and one *LRK10L*.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> These are candidates, not confirmed SH genes.

How many genes act in one accession was tested in a 2009 Brazilian inheritance study. Catuaí Amarelo IAC 64 (SH5, susceptible) was crossed with HdT UFV 443-03; the F1 plant was selfed (246 F2 plants) and backcrossed to the resistant parent (87 plants) and to the susceptible parent (115 plants).<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> Leaf discs were inoculated with race II (v5) and with pathotype 001, a single-pustule isolate from the cultivar Oeiras.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> With race II, the F2 split 235 resistant to 11 susceptible, which fitted both 15:1 and 61:3. The susceptible backcross split 80:35, which fitted 3:1 (χ² = 1.811, P = 0.178) and rejected 7:1.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> Pathotype 001 gave the same picture (F2 231:15; backcross 85:30). The authors conclude that two dominant, independent genes control the resistance of this accession to both inocula.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The same paper reports earlier Brazilian work with one dominant gene in accession UFV 427-15 and three in six other accessions. It explains the difference by the heterogeneous origin of the accessions, plants collected on Timor by the CIFC in the 1950s.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

## Rust variability and resistance breakdown

The 2009 study also showed the limits of race typing. Pathotype 001 reacted as race II on the CIFC differential set, yet it infected Oeiras and Icatu Vermelho, which race II did not.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The authors read this as one or more virulence genes the differentials do not detect, possibly a new race.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> They cite earlier work in which isolates from Catimor, Sarchimor and the cultivar Colombia had been typed as race II but carried further virulence genes.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

A 2013 study analysed 91 rust isolates collected in 2007 from *C. arabica*, *C. canephora* and Timor Hybrid or Icatu derivatives in Minas Gerais, Espírito Santo and São Paulo, Brazil, with 100 AFLP markers.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Every isolate had its own multilocus genotype. Of the genetic variance, 99.56% lay within host groups and 0.44% between them (G<sub>ST</sub> = 0.026).<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Random mating could not be rejected for the isolates from *C. canephora* (I<sub>A</sub> = 0.225, P = 0.123), which the authors take as support for sexual recombination.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> They argue that varieties bred from canephora × arabica hybrids share common resistance genes, which favours new, complex races able to infect both species.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The paper notes that several cultivars from these hybrids have lost their resistance to new races, and that race XXXVII (v2,5,6,7,9) was first detected in Brazil in 2008.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

Against coffee berry disease, the Timor Hybrid carries one gene at the T locus, with intermediate action.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the World Coffee Research catalogue, of the Catimor lines introduced to Malawi only Catimor 129 showed some resistance to coffee berry disease.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## One plant or many

Breeding lines trace back to accessions held at CIFC. Accession CIFC 4106 has been considered the first plant, found in 1927.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2013 study measured nuclear DNA content, base composition and chromosome number in CIFC 4106 and five other accessions.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> All CIFC 4106 plants were alike; the other five differed in DNA content.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The authors suggest that CIFC 4106 is an allotriploid, formed from a reduced germ cell of each species, and that the other accessions arose from it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

This sits uneasily with the older account that the Timor Hybrid has arabica’s chromosome number.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Both can be true if the first plant was triploid and the populations used in breeding are its tetraploid descendants. No study consulted for this entry tested that sequence directly.

## Cup quality in trials

Introgressed varieties have a reputation for lower cup quality. According to a coffee science textbook, the quality of some is debated; it names Costa Rica 95 as inferior to traditional varieties.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Trials give a mixed picture, line by line:

- **CIRAD and IRD, 2003.** A trial compared Timor Hybrid-derived lines and F1 hybrids with non-introgressed controls. Lines differed significantly in caffeine, chlorogenic acids, fat, trigonelline and sucrose, in acidity and in overall standard.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Two lines were poorer than the controls in sucrose and acidity. Two highly introgressed lines did not differ from the controls.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Lines carried 1–37 introgressed AFLP markers, and marker number was not correlated with chemistry or cup attributes.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Cenicafé, 2009.** A panel of five tasters scored 34 F5 and F6 component lines of Castillo against Típica, Borbón, Tabi and Caturra on 10 attributes.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The progenies formed similarity groups with the traditional varieties, and some groups scored statistically higher than the controls on some variables.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Brazil, 2020.** A trial cupped 16 genotypes: three arabica, seven Timor Hybrid accessions and six Timor Hybrid × arabica cultivars.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It found no significant differences in cup attributes between Bourbon and the cultivars derived from the Timor Hybrid.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The trials agree on one point: quality depends on the line selected, not on the presence of robusta DNA as such. Selection can keep resistance without a drop in cup quality.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry compared Catimor and Sarchimor groups as wholes.

## Open questions

- **Ploidy of the original plant.** Whether the first plant was triploid, and how the fertile breeding populations arose from it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Which genes give resistance.** The genome study names candidate genes in the chromosome 4 region; which of them correspond to SH6–SH9 is not established.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Durable resistance.** With races overcoming several derivatives, breeders look for new resistance sources, including more robusta material.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2013 Brazilian rust population study found high genotypic diversity and gene flow, which it links to a high potential of the fungus to adapt.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

<p class="meta">Theoretical and Applied Genetics 107, 387–394 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00122-003-1203-6">doi:10.1007/s00122-003-1203-6</a></p>
