## How it is done

1. **Drying the whole cherry** – the harvested cherries are dried in the sun<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> – on patios or raised beds<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> – or in drying machines.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
2. **Thin layers, turned often** – the Codex code of practice recommends layers of only 3–5 cm, turned constantly during the day. This speeds up drying and reduces the risk of mould.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
3. **Protection** – at first, wet cherries need ventilation at night to avoid condensation. After about three days they can be heaped and covered at night or in rain so that they do not get wet again.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Practice varies: some guides cover every night (see Deep dive).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
4. **Drying to a safe level** – drying takes about three to four weeks: 18–20 days in one FAO manual, over three weeks under favourable conditions in a 2026 review,<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> and 20–30 days in the sun in a 2023 review, which gives the moisture falling from 60–65% (wet basis) to about 12%.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> A São Paulo state manual sets the end point at 11% moisture (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The Codex code considers parchment coffee protected from fungal damage below 12.5% moisture (wet basis) and a water activity of at most 0.67–0.70.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
5. **Hulling** – the dried husk (skin, pulp, mucilage and parchment) is removed mechanically, together with as much silverskin as possible.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Effect on the cup

Whether coffee is processed wet or dry creates typical differences in quality and flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2024 study found that some Arabica varieties, such as Bourbon and Pacamara, scored better for flavour when processed natural.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## What happens inside the drying cherry

**Microbes.** Natural processing hosts a different microbial community from washed processing. In a study in Ecuador, acetic acid bacteria (*Acetobacter* and *Gluconobacter*) were the most abundant, together with *Pichia* and other yeasts (*Candida*, *Starmerella*, *Saccharomycopsis*). Their products – gluconic acid and sugar alcohols – built up in the drying outer layers of the cherry.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**The seed.** The seed stays alive during drying and starts germination processes, just as in washed coffee – but later: in dry processing, germination activity peaked about one week after processing began, compared with about two days in wet processing.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Sugars.** Natural coffees generally contain more glucose and fructose than washed coffees. A study of washed coffee during drying showed that this difference is due – at least in part – to metabolic processes, not to sugars leaching into the process water.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Drying risks

- **Mould and ochratoxin A** – whole cherries take weeks to dry. Thin layers and frequent turning are among the measures the Codex code gives against fungal growth.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Rewetting** – coffee can lose water quickly at midday and reabsorb moisture at night, and condensation can form inside passive dryers that are poorly ventilated.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Rewetting favours fungal growth and ochratoxin A.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Mixed lots** – the Codex code advises against mixing different types of coffee or coffee from different harvest days.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Uneven or too-fast drying** – rapid, uneven moisture removal in mechanical dryers can cause casehardening, a hard outer shell that traps moisture inside; uneven or rapid drying can also degrade delicate aromatics and diminish fruity or floral notes.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Too-high temperature** – the quality of natural coffee depends on preserving the cell membranes of the seed, which can be damaged during drying.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A São Paulo state manual separately limits the temperature of the coffee mass in dryers to 45 °C, and to below 30 °C when many green fruits are present.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Alves et al. (2017) state, citing earlier work, that coffee-mass temperatures above 40 °C cause thermal damage that lowers quality (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Mixed evidence on flavour

The 2024 study that found benefits for Bourbon and Pacamara also found, across all Arabica samples, a tendency towards lower flavour scores for natural processing – a result that was not statistically significant.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The effect of the natural process on the cup depends on the coffee it is applied to.
