brief:
  - Water is the main ingredient of brewed coffee. Its minerals affect flavour and how long machines last.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
  - Two numbers describe most waters for coffee – total hardness (calcium plus magnesium) and alkalinity (the capacity to neutralise acid, mostly bicarbonate).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
  - High alkalinity neutralises coffee acids and flattens the cup, more in filter coffee than in espresso, because filter uses far more water per gram of coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
  - The SCA recommends total hardness of 50–175 ppm and alkalinity of 40–75 ppm as CaCO₃, pH 6–8, odour-free and hygienic; alkalinity below 40 ppm raises the risk of corrosion.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
  - pH and “TDS” meter readings alone say little about how a water will brew.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

contested:
  - 'Does hardness raise extraction? A density-functional model of how sodium, magnesium and calcium bind coffee compounds<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> is read by the SCA handbook as implying higher extraction with harder water, but laboratory tests reported in the handbook found no effect measurable with a coffee refractometer, only on flavour balance and aroma.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Adding magnesium and calcium salts at drinking-water levels changed four coffee acids little.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The SCAA handbook reports an older study in which water with sodium bicarbonate gave a stronger brew than water with calcium chloride.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>'
  - 'How soft is too soft: suggested hardness optima collected by the SCA range widely, from a World Brewers Cup recommendation at the soft end to Colonna-Dashwood and Hendon at the hard end.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Some cuppers use water with hardness and alkalinity far below the SCA range; the SCA advises strongly against such water in espresso machines because of corrosion.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A study of filter coffee recommended medium-hard water because it gave the most volatile compounds, not on grounds of sensory preference; bitterness dominated only in coffee made with hard water.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>'
  - 'TDS versus hardness and alkalinity: the SCAA standard is built around a TDS of 150 mg/L,<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> while the SCA 2018 handbook calls TDS on its own not meaningful for coffee extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The SCAA standard also sets a pH target of 7.0 with an acceptable range of 6.5–7.5, narrower than the SCA’s 6–8,<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> and the SCAA handbook’s text and table disagree on sodium (20 mg/L in the list, 10 mg/L in the table).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The table in the 2011 SCAA handbook, credited to the 2009 committee, also differs from the standard document: calcium hardness target 3–4 grains (51–68 mg/L), pH target 7–8 with an adequate range of 6.5–7.5, total alkalinity adequate at 10–100 mg/L and sodium adequate below 30 mg/L.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>'
  - 'Carbon dioxide in water at equilibrium with air: the SCA handbook gives 0.4 mg/L at 20 °C (a single trade source);<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> our own calculation from published Henry’s law constants gives about 0.7 mg/L (see Carbonate chemistry).<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>'
  - 'Softened water: the SCAA handbook advises against brewing with softened water, citing slower flow through the coffee bed;<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> the SCA 2018 handbook lists the softener as a standard treatment that lowers hardness but leaves alkalinity unchanged.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>'
  - 'Conductivity reference temperature: the SCA handbook uses 20 °C;<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> a USGS study gives 25 °C as the typical reference.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Readings from meters with different reference temperatures or conversion factors are not directly comparable.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>'

## Why water matters

Water is the main ingredient of brewed coffee; everything else is the coffee solids it extracts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its quality can mask a coffee’s flavour. In dilute brews such as filter coffee, unsuitable water can overpower acidity and leave the cup dull.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Water carries tastes and odours of its own, and some of its dissolved substances change how brewing proceeds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Water also decides how long machines last. Even with treatment, many espresso machines break down through scale that blocks valves and jets, or, more rarely, through corrosion.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Water treatment for coffee has long been aimed mainly at protecting equipment; the SCA handbook also sets out to treat water for flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Drinking-water rules are written for safety, not for coffee. Tap water that meets them can still make poor coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Where the minerals come from

Rain is slightly acidic: carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in it and forms weak carbonic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> More carbon dioxide is taken up from organic matter in the soil, and this acidic water partly dissolves carbonate rocks such as limestone.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> It leaves with calcium, magnesium and hydrogen carbonate (bicarbonate) ions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Silicate rocks such as granite, feldspar or quartz barely dissolve, so water from them stays soft.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

How much dissolves depends on the rock, on how long the water stays in contact with it and on the grain size of the minerals.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Groundwater is therefore usually harder than river or lake water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Calcium and magnesium are the two principal ions of hardness, and limestone and chalk are their most common sources.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Other rocks add other ions. Gypsum (calcium sulfate) raises hardness without adding alkalinity; intruding sea water adds sodium and bicarbonate, which raise alkalinity without adding hardness.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Hardness and alkalinity

Most of what matters in coffee water comes down to two numbers.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Total hardness** is the amount of dissolved calcium and magnesium.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It is what forms scale with carbonate when water is heated.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Alkalinity** is the water’s ability to neutralise acids.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It is measured as the amount of acid needed to bring a sample down to a low end point – between about pH 4.5 and 4.0 in the USGS field method,<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> pH 4.3 in the SCA handbook – and in most waters it is mostly bicarbonate.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In the cup, alkalinity is the more important of the two. The higher the alkalinity, the lower the perceived acidity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At high alkalinity, bicarbonate neutralising coffee acids releases much carbon dioxide, which can slow the flow and lead to over-extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Older trials found that coffees brewed with water containing carbonates were the most bitter and tasted flat, and that more carbonates lengthened the brewing time.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A review of espresso brewing discusses the role of alkalinity and of individual cations in its sensory properties.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Hardness affects flavour balance and aroma.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Whether it also raises how much coffee is extracted is disputed (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

**Carbonate hardness is not the same as alkalinity.** Carbonate hardness is the part of the hardness that can leave as scale. It equals whichever of total hardness and alkalinity is lower.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In most tap waters hardness is slightly higher than alkalinity, so the two terms happen to coincide. In softened or salt-influenced water they do not.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Many test kits sold as “carbonate hardness” kits actually measure alkalinity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Why pH misleads.** pH tells how acidic or basic the water is at that moment.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It does not tell how much acid the water can absorb, just as a room’s temperature does not tell how much heat it takes to warm it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The pH of most tap waters is set largely by excess dissolved carbon dioxide; left standing in an open container for a day, such water loses carbon dioxide and its pH rises markedly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Even a little alkalinity buffers far more acid than the difference between pH 5 and pH 7.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Our own arithmetic: 25 ppm as CaCO₃ is 0.5 mmol/L of acid-neutralising capacity, while going from pH 7 to pH 5 adds only about 0.01 mmol/L of hydrogen ions – about 50 times less.

**Why TDS meters mislead.** A “TDS meter” measures electrical conductivity and converts it with an assumed factor.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The factor depends on which salts are dissolved, and the reading says nothing about what they are.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Conductivity is still useful to check that a supply or a treatment stays stable.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Units

Hardness and alkalinity are usually given as if all the ions were calcium carbonate: ppm (mg/L) as CaCO₃, or as German, French or English degrees.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> These units count ions, not their weight. Bottled-water labels instead give each ion in mg/L, which cannot simply be added up.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

| Calcium, mg/L | 2.497 | ppm as CaCO₃ (hardness) | SCA<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>, United Utilities<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> |

The factors follow from atomic masses and the definitions of each degree.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The UK utility factsheet rounds the same conversions (×0.056 to German, ×0.1 to French, ×0.07 to Clark degrees, ×0.4 to calcium);<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> the SCAA standard equates 4 grains with 68 mg/L.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> We checked the magnesium, hydrogen carbonate and mmol/L factors from molar masses (CaCO₃ 100.09, Mg 24.305, HCO₃⁻ 61.02 g/mol; one hydrogen carbonate neutralises half as much acid as one carbonate). A shortcut from the SCA: ppm as CaCO₃ divided by 20, plus ten per cent, gives °dH;<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> by our arithmetic it is within about 2% of the exact factor. Example from a bottle label: 50 mg/L calcium, 10 mg/L magnesium and 100 mg/L hydrogen carbonate give a total hardness of 166 ppm and an alkalinity of 82 ppm as CaCO₃ (SCA example,<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> checked with the factors above).

## Published targets

**SCAA standard (2009).** The SCAA standard “Water for Brewing Specialty Coffee” gives a target and an acceptable range for each characteristic; the target is the most desirable point, and any value within the range meets the standard.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> It asks for clean, fresh, odour-free and clear water without chlorine, with TDS 150 mg/L (acceptable 75–250 mg/L), calcium hardness 68 mg/L or 4 grains (acceptable 17–85 mg/L, 1–5 grains), total alkalinity 40 mg/L (acceptable at or near 40 mg/L), pH 7.0 (acceptable 6.5–7.5) and sodium 10 mg/L (at or near 10 mg/L).<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> It specifies calcium hardness, not total hardness. The standard specifies TDS measured with a ‘4-4-2’ conversion.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The SCAA handbook traces the standards to testing at the former Coffee Brewing Center; its own table of them gives some different values (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**SCA Water Quality Handbook (2018).** The handbook by Wellinger, Smrke and Yeretzian, uses total hardness and alkalinity as the key parameters, and treats TDS as not meaningful on its own.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It recommends water that is odour-free and hygienic, with total hardness 50–175 ppm as CaCO₃, alkalinity 40–75 ppm (2.2–4.2 °dH) and pH 6–8.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The handbook gives the hardness range as 2.9–9.8 °dH; by its own factor, 50 ppm is 2.8 °dH. For espresso machines and boilers it recommends a narrower “core zone”, bounded by the corrosion limits and by a maximum of 12 mg/L of scale in a brew boiler or 30 mg/L in a steam boiler at 130 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Other published views.** Recommendations collected by the SCA differ widely on hardness – the softest from a World Brewers Cup recommendation, the hardest from Colonna-Dashwood and Hendon – but agree much more closely on alkalinity; the SCA’s own range, 40–75 ppm, reflects this.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> All of them put total hardness above alkalinity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Most were made for espresso.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

These are recommendations, not laws of nature. The SCA stresses that the sensory part of the target is largely subjective and that too few data exist to describe the effect of hardness and alkalinity across origins, roasts and brew methods.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

No single filter suits every water. Each treatment moves a water in its own direction on the water chart (see Check your water).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Your local water

Tap water differs from town to town. How hard it is depends on where it comes from: rain picks up lime from the rock it seeps through, so groundwater and spring water tend to be harder than treated lake water.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Bottled waters differ just as much.

The numbers you need are usually published. Water suppliers report hardness in their water analyses, and bottle labels list calcium, magnesium and hydrogen carbonate in mg/L, which can be converted to ppm as CaCO₃.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The tool above does this conversion.

Countries report hardness in different units and sort it into different classes. These classes were written for households and appliances, not for coffee, so a water called “soft” locally may still be too hard or too alkaline for brewing:

| Where | Unit used | Classes, converted to ppm as CaCO₃ | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States | mg/L (ppm) as CaCO₃; grains per gallon | Soft 0–60; moderately hard 61–120; hard 121–180; very hard above 180 | USGS<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> |
| Germany | mmol/L of calcium carbonate | Soft below 150; medium 150–250; hard above 250 (law: below 1.5, 1.5–2.5, above 2.5 mmol/L) | Detergents law (WRMG §9)<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> |
| Switzerland | French degrees (°fH; 1 °fH = 10 ppm) | Soft below 150; medium hard 150–250; fairly hard 250–320; hard 320–420; very hard above 420 | Kanton Zürich<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>, SVGW<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> |

In these terms the SCA range for total hardness, 50–175 ppm,<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> starts in the softest class everywhere. It ends in the medium class in Germany and Switzerland, but its upper part (121–175 ppm) already counts as hard in the US classes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> Alkalinity, the more important number for coffee, is often not in these classes at all; look for it in the full water analysis.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Household softening follows its own rules. In Switzerland, for example, the SVGW advises against softening drinking water below a certain hardness and sets a minimum residual hardness for softened water.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Such rules protect pipes and appliances; for coffee, the SCA puts the most weight on alkalinity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Carbonate chemistry

Four equilibria tie carbon dioxide, alkalinity and scale together.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

1. CO₂ (dissolved) + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ (carbonic acid)
2. H₂CO₃ ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺
3. HCO₃⁻ ⇌ CO₃²⁻ + H⁺ (only relevant in strongly basic water)
4. Taken together with limestone: CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ Ca²⁺ + 2 HCO₃⁻

Equations 1–3 are given in the SCA handbook, which lumps dissolved CO₂ and carbonic acid together, as is customary in water chemistry.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Equation 4 is the sum of dissolution and protonation that the handbook describes in words: acidic water dissolves carbonate rock and acquires calcium and hydrogen carbonate.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Read forwards, it explains how limestone becomes hardness and alkalinity. Read backwards, it is scale: heating drives off CO₂, and calcium carbonate precipitates.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Normal rain has a pH of about 5.6 because carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in it;<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> the SCA handbook explains the slight acidity of pure water in contact with air the same way.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Most tap water carries carbon dioxide in excess of that equilibrium, which strongly influences its pH.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Water in equilibrium with air holds much less. Our own calculation from Henry’s law gives about 0.7 mg/L: with a solubility constant of 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ mol/(m³·Pa) at 25 °C and a temperature coefficient of 2300 K,<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> air with 400–420 ppm CO₂ (40.5–42.6 Pa) gives 0.69–0.73 mg/L at 20 °C. The SCA handbook gives a lower value (see Contested). Stirred in an open container for a day, a hard tap water in one of the handbook’s examples lost most of its excess carbon dioxide and its pH rose markedly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Charge must balance. Calcium carbonate removed as scale takes one calcium ion but two hydrogen carbonate ions, because each calcium carries two charges.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> This is why units based on equivalents, such as ppm as CaCO₃, make hardness, alkalinity and scale directly comparable.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Why filter needs less alkalinity than espresso

Filter coffee uses far more water per gram of coffee than espresso, so the same water brings far more buffer into contact with the same acids.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The SCA assumes a ratio of coffee to beverage of about 1:15 for filter and 1:2 for espresso (see [Brew ratio and strength](/entries/brew-ratio-and-strength/) for the different ratios).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It concludes that espresso tolerates up to about 150 ppm alkalinity before buffering becomes apparent, while filter needs less.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Our own arithmetic, from these cited numbers: 1 g of coffee brewed at 1:15 with water of 50 ppm alkalinity meets 0.75 mg of acid-neutralising capacity (as CaCO₃), or about 0.015 mmol of bicarbonate. At 1:2 it meets 0.10 mg – 7.5 times less. Espresso brewed with 150 ppm water still meets only 0.30 mg per gram. This simple calculation uses beverage weight and ignores water held back in the grounds.

Hardness behaves differently. The SCA finds that total hardness has less impact than alkalinity and that even hard water can give a high-quality filter brew.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Scale and corrosion

**Scale.** Scale forms where calcium meets carbonate and heat, mostly as calcium carbonate; at very high pH magnesium hydroxide can form too.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It lowers heat transfer and clogs valves and jets, especially in hot-water sections.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Gypsum-rich water forms scale of a different kind.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Silica scales regardless of pH and alkalinity, and is hard to remove.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Lower pH means less scale.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The SCA estimates maximum scale from a modified Langelier index and turns it into a yearly amount from the number of drinks.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Our own arithmetic, following its example: at 100 drinks a day and 0.1 L per drink including flushing, a machine uses about 3,650 L a year, so water able to deposit 30, 100 or 200 mg/L of scale can leave about 110, 365 or 730 g. The estimate holds best when the machine stands hot for hours; at high throughput the water does not stay hot long enough for all of it to form.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Corrosion.** The SCA lists the conditions that make corrosion likely:<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- alkalinity below 40 ppm as CaCO₃, which leaves too little buffer;
- pH below 6 or above 8;
- chloride, sulfate or nitrate above about 80% of the alkalinity in equivalents (a Larson–Skold index above 0.8). For gypsum waters this becomes: total hardness no more than 80% higher than alkalinity;
- for salt-influenced waters, electrical conductivity (µS/cm) more than three times the alkalinity (ppm as CaCO₃). The SCA derives this rule from measured waters, among them tap waters near Barcelona.

Chloride is a particular concern. The SCA advises calcium or magnesium sulfate rather than chlorides for hardening water used in espresso machines, because chlorides increase the chance of corrosion; espresso machine makers often advise against water with significant amounts of chloride.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Soft water that is not stabilised tends to corrode pipes in general.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Decarboniser limits.** A decarboniser turns bicarbonate into carbonic acid. In an open kettle the CO₂ escapes when the water is heated, but in a closed espresso system it stays.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Removing a large amount of alkalinity therefore leaves much dissolved CO₂; the water turns acidic, corrosion risk rises, and espresso can foam with large, short-lived bubbles.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Our own arithmetic: removing 250 ppm of alkalinity (as CaCO₃) converts 5 mmol/L of hydrogen carbonate into 5 mmol/L of CO₂, about 220 mg/L. The SCA therefore advises setting the bypass so that output alkalinity is 40–70 ppm for waters up to 250 ppm, and so that alkalinity falls by no more than 200 ppm for harder waters.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The SCAA handbook adds that corrosion in brewers is rarely caused by natural water: acid flux from soldered copper pipe joints is a more usual culprit.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Off-flavours and contaminants

- **Chlorine and chloramines** used for disinfection give unpleasant flavours and should be removed, most simply with activated carbon.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Chlorine can react with coffee phenols to form chlorophenols, which have a medicinal taste.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Iron** turns coffee greenish. In a 1955 study, a difference could be seen side by side at 1 ppm, and above 7 ppm coffee with cream had a clearly unpleasant greenish cast.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **Lead** is toxic. Only reverse osmosis and deionisation remove it completely.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Taste thresholds.** In water, the taste threshold for calcium is 100–300 mg/L, depending on the anion.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In the 1955 study, coffee made with water containing single ions at their thresholds in water could not be told apart from coffee made with distilled water.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Measuring water

**Drop titration.** Outside a laboratory, hardness and alkalinity are measured by titration, as two separate tests.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Hardness is titrated with EDTA; alkalinity with a strong acid such as hydrochloric acid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Reagent is added drop by drop until the colour changes completely, and the drops are counted.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The SCA recommends a resolution of at least 20 ppm (1 °dH). Doubling the sample volume halves the value of each drop, which helps with low-mineral treated waters.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Conductivity meters.** Electrical conductivity depends on temperature: depending on the salts present, it rises by about 1–3% per °C.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Measurements are therefore referred to a standard temperature, typically 25 °C, and field meters often correct with a fixed coefficient of 0.019–0.020 per °C.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The SCA handbook uses 20 °C as its reference and warns that many cheap meters do not correct at all.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Converting conductivity to TDS adds a second uncertainty. In the natural waters studied by Hem of the US Geological Survey, the ratio of dissolved solids (mg/L) to conductivity (µS/cm) ranged from 0.54 to 0.96.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> It is much higher for dissolved calcium carbonate than for table salt.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The heritage SCAA standard assumed one fixed factor, but many meters use another or a variable factor.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The SCA therefore treats a TDS estimate as rough, with large deviations possible.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Daily checks.** Once a treatment is set, a conductivity meter can track it. For decarbonisation, conductivity falls roughly in proportion to the alkalinity removed, which the SCA confirmed on Swiss tap waters.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A softener barely changes conductivity, so conductivity cannot track it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Making water from salts

Recipe waters start from almost pure water and add salts in known amounts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Sodium hydrogen carbonate raises alkalinity only; magnesium or calcium sulfate or chloride raises hardness only.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> As an example, the SCA dissolves 3 g of pure sodium bicarbonate in 1 L of reverse-osmosis water to make a concentrate of 100 °dH alkalinity; 10 g of it in 1 L of water adds 1 °dH.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> We checked the arithmetic from molar masses: 3 g of NaHCO₃ is about 35.7 mmol, 35.7 mmol/L of hydrogen carbonate is about 100 °dH, and diluting about 10 g in a litre is roughly a hundredfold dilution.

Mixing follows a lever rule. For a target alkalinity, the share of water 1 is (alkalinity of water 2 − target) ÷ (alkalinity of water 2 − alkalinity of water 1); the same formula works for hardness.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The SCA’s worked example mixes a water of 5 ppm alkalinity with one of 150 ppm to reach 60 ppm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The handbook prints 64.1% of the soft water, but its own formula gives 62.1% (our own arithmetic): (150 − 60) ÷ (150 − 5) = 90 ÷ 145 = 0.621, and 0.621 × 5 + 0.379 × 150 = 60 ppm.

## Open questions

- **Calcium versus magnesium.** Theory and preliminary experiments suggest magnesium extracts slightly more than calcium, but its effect on sensory properties remains unclear.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The total-hardness concept treats the two as one.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Brew ratio, temperature and pressure.** The SCA expects the optimum to shift with brew ratio and with temperature and pressure, and calls for correction factors or separate zones.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Roast style.** Anecdotal evidence suggests that harder, more alkaline water can partly compensate for underdeveloped roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry tested it.
- **Local taste.** Where tap water is very soft, preferences and roasting styles may have adapted to it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **The evidence behind TDS 150 mg/L.** The SCAA target rests partly on a small blind tasting by committee members, which the handbook itself says cannot be considered statistically valid.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Hendon et al., 2014 – Cations and extraction.</strong> Uses density functional theory to calculate how strongly sodium, magnesium and calcium ions bind to five coffee acids, caffeine and the aroma compound eugenol. The calculations are meant to explain the mechanism of extraction and suggest an ideal mineral composition; they are a model, not a brewing trial.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 62(21), 4947–4950 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf501687c">doi:10.1021/jf501687c</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Navarini & Rivetti, 2010 – Water quality for espresso.</strong> Reviews what was known about how water and coffee components interact during espresso brewing. It discusses the role of alkalinity and of selected cations in the sensory properties of espresso.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry 122(2), 424–428 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2009.04.019">doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2009.04.019</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Lockhart et al., 1955 – Water impurities and flavour.</strong> Determined taste thresholds of common ions in water with experienced panels, then brewed coffee with water at those levels. Coffee made with water containing single ions at their water thresholds could not be told apart from coffee made with distilled water; iron also gave the coffee a greenish colour.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 20(6), 598–605</p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bratthäll et al., 2024 – Divalent cations and coffee acids.</strong> Added magnesium and calcium salts before or after brewing and measured citric, malic, lactic and quinic acids by GC–MS and NMR. At drinking-water concentrations the acids varied little; only ten-fold higher concentrations gave clearer differences.</p>
<p class="meta">Heliyon 10(5), e26625 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e26625">doi:10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e26625</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Dadalı & Elmacı, 2021 – Hardness and filter coffee.</strong> Brewed filter coffee with four waters – bottled, purified, medium hard and hard – and compared volatile compounds and sensory intensities. Sourness was strongest with soft water and weakest with hard water; bitterness was reported as the dominant taste only for hard water (3.63). Medium-hard water gave the most volatile compounds, which is why the authors recommend it; the study did not measure preference.</p>
<p class="meta">Turkish Journal of Agriculture – Food Science and Technology 9(2), 312–320 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.24925/turjaf.v9i2.312-320.3885">doi:10.24925/turjaf.v9i2.312-320.3885</a></p>
</div>
