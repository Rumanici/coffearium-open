## What grading and sorting do

After hulling, green coffee is still a mixture: large and small beans, flat beans and peaberries, dense and light beans, sound and defective ones, and some stones, sticks and husk. During dry processing the beans are classified by size, shape, weight and colour, and recombined into the commercial preparation the buyer asks for.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> A preparation is a combination of bean sizes and shapes with a stated maximum number of defects.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

Two different things happen here. **Sorting** is physical: machines and people separate beans. **Grading** is a judgement on a sample: the beans are sieved and the defects counted, and the lot receives a grade or type.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Grades can also depend on altitude or region, botanical variety, wet or dry preparation, and bean size, sometimes with shape and colour.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Sizing: screens in 64ths of an inch

Beans are sized on perforated metal screens. ISO sieves are defined by metric apertures; the traditional screen number is the nearest round-hole diameter (or slot width) in 64ths of an inch.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Brazilian regulation writes it out directly: screen 14 is 14/64 inch.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> One step on the scale is therefore 1/64 inch, about 0.4 mm.

Flat beans and peaberries need different screens. A flat bean passes a round hole by its width; a peaberry is rounder and is separated on slotted (oblong) holes.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The Brazilian standard sorts flat beans (*chato*) on round-hole screens 19, 18, 17, 16, 15, 14 and smaller, and peaberries (*moca*) on oblong-hole screens 13 down to 8; its laboratory series also includes round screens 13 and 10 for flat beans.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

For a size analysis, ISO 4150 takes 300 g of the laboratory sample, weighed to 0.1 g, and shakes the nest of sieves for 3 minutes at 110–130 cycles per minute.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The screens most often used are 18, 17, 16, 15, 14 and 12.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the SCAA method reproduced by FAO, specialty and premium lots may deviate by at most 5% above or below the screen size stated.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Size and altitude grades by origin

Many producing countries name their export grades after size, altitude or both.

- **Kenya** grades internally as E, AA, AB, PB, C, TT and T, on bean size and density, and then splits these by liquor quality.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Colombia** reported five grades to the ICO – Premium, Supremo, Extra, Excelso and Caracol – each defined by screen size.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Central America** uses altitude bands: Strictly Hard Bean (SHB) in Guatemala and Strictly High Grown (SHG) in El Salvador are the highest bands; El Salvador's lower ones are High Grown and Central Standard.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> "European preparation" (EP) allows fewer defects than "American preparation".<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In the ICO's 2018 compilation of national quality standards, Guatemala is recorded as not yet implementing quality standards for coffee, so these names are trade usage rather than regulation there.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Brazil** classifies by screen and by "type", a count of defect equivalents in 300 g, from type 2 (cleanest) to type 8.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The altitude limits and defect allowances behind SHB, SHG, EP and American preparation are given in one trade guide consulted for this entry; no second source was found, so the numbers are left out here.

## Density and colour sorting

**Gravity (density) tables.** A gravity separator has a perforated deck that slopes gently in two directions and is shaken by an eccentric drive.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Air blown up through the deck lifts and loosens the layer and brings the lighter particles to the top.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The vibration walks the heavier particles towards the higher side of the deck, while the lighter ones move down towards the lower corner.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> An adjustable gate sets the feed rate, and movable dividers at the discharge edge set which weight ranges go to which outlet.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The patent describes a general gravity separator and names coffee among the materials such devices have long separated;<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> no coffee-specific trial of table settings was consulted for this entry.

**Optical sorters.** In a gravity-fed sorter, individual coffee beans run down a steep channel or chute.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Near the bottom, an optical sensor views each bean as it passes, against a diffusing background panel, and detects off-standard items.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> After a very short delay an ejector fires and blows the detected bean out of the stream.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Commercial sorters work in monochromatic mode (one wavelength), bichromatic mode (two wavelengths) or both.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Sensing can be partly or wholly outside the visible range; the patent notes that infrared sensing is common.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> High-speed optical sorting is used for coffee, beans, peanuts, peas and rice.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Ultraviolet light.** Under UV light some green coffee beans show a blue fluorescence, and the trade has long used this to spot faulty beans.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In a study from Brazil, a larger share of fully fluorescent beans in a natural specialty lot had a negative effect on the final cup score and on astringency.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Counting defects

A defect count turns a sample into one number. Each defect type has an equivalence: how many of it make one "full defect". The weights differ by system.

- **SCAA, as reproduced by FAO.** Primary defects count heavily: one full black, one full sour or one pod/cherry is one full defect.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Secondary defects count lightly: five broken or chipped beans, five floaters or five shells make one full defect.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Specialty grade allows at most 5 full defects and no primary defects, no quakers, and moisture between 9 and 13% (basis not stated); it must also show at least one distinctive attribute in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Premium grade allows up to 8 full defects.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Brazil (MAPA).** Defects are intrinsic (bean defects) or extrinsic (foreign matter and husk). One black bean is one defect; two sour (*ardido*) beans, five immature (*verde*) beans or three shells make one.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A large stick, stone or clod counts as five.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **ISO 10470.** The ISO defect reference chart sorts defects into five categories and applies to any species, variety and processing.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Its coefficients weigh each defect's effect on mass loss and on sensory quality, for use in contracts or lot classification.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **ICO export floor.** The ICO quality programme excludes Arabica with more than 86 defects per 300 g, and any coffee below 8% or above 12.5% moisture measured by ISO 6673.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Where defects come from

Grading standards define defects mostly by what they look like. In the Brazilian regulation only the sour bean is defined with a cause.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

- **Black (*preto*).** A bean or piece of bean of opaque black colour.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It is a primary defect in the SCAA system.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The sources consulted here do not assign a cause.
- **Sour (*ardido*).** A bean or piece of bean with a brown colour in various shades, which the regulation attributes to fermentation processes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Also primary in the SCAA system.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Which processing faults (over-fermentation, delayed processing) cause it belongs to fermentation and harvesting; no source consulted here tests them.
- **Immature (*verde*).** An immature bean, with silverskin stuck to it and a closed ventral groove.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Quakers – beans that roast pale – are barred from SCAA specialty grade.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Insect-damaged (*brocado*).** A bean damaged by the coffee berry borer, with one or more holes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Poorly filled (*mal granado*).** A bean with incomplete formation and little mass.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Shell (*concha*).** A shell-shaped piece formed when interlocked beans separate.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Broken (*quebrado*).** A piece of bean of variable shape or size.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Breakage in the huller is covered under hulling and dry milling.
- **Floater.** A secondary defect in the SCAA table.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The machines match these visual definitions. A gravity table separates particles by weight,<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> and a colour sorter rejects beans whose reflectance at one or two wavelengths falls outside the standard.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> How efficiently each machine removes each defect type in coffee was not measured in the sources consulted. In seeds generally, surface discoloration caused by fungi is detectable in the visible range, whereas internal attributes are detectable in the near infrared.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Size, density and cup quality

Trade grades rank larger screens higher.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The evidence on cup quality is mixed. In a Mexican study of three Catimor varieties, flat beans on screen 13 scored almost seven points higher than peaberries of the same screen in the Colombia variety.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> But small beans (screens 13 and 14) not separated by shape scored equal to or higher than bigger beans, and the effect of size and shape depended on the variety.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In this study, size alone did not predict cup quality.

In the Brazilian UV study, more fully fluorescent beans had a negative effect on the final score and astringency, and partially fluorescent beans had a negative effect on score, astringency, sweetness, acidity, bitterness and aftertaste.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Measurement at grading

- **Sample.** 300 g for sieving (ISO 4150) and for Brazilian classification; 300 g or 350 g for the SCAA/SCA defect count, depending on the source.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **Moisture.** ISO 6673 is the reference method named in the ICO export rule for green coffee; accepted range 8–12.5%.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> SCAA specialty grade asks for 9–13% (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Water activity.** The SCA's archived (heritage) green coffee standards set no moisture range; for specialty grade they set a water-activity limit instead: the beans must measure lower than 0.70 aw.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> See moisture and water activity for the safe ranges.
- **Bulk density.** No grading standard consulted for this entry sets a limit.
- **Visual examination.** ISO 10470 provides the reference chart for identifying defects.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Open questions

- Coffee-specific settings for gravity tables (deck slopes, air, vibration, recirculation of the middle fraction) have not been published in the sources found.
- The causes of black and sour beans, and the share each processing step contributes, need primary studies linked to this entry.
- How well colour sorters remove stinkers and mouldy beans, and at what loss of sound beans, is not reported in the sources consulted.
- Whether density predicts cup quality better than screen size has not been tested head to head in the sources consulted.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Luna González et al., 2019 – Bean size, shape and cup quality.</strong> Three Catimor varieties grown in Mexico were separated by screen and by shape (flat, peaberry, mixed) and cupped. Shape and size changed the scores, but not always in favour of large beans: small, unseparated beans often cupped as well as or better than larger ones, and the effect depended on variety.</p>
<p class="meta">International Journal of Food Properties 22, 758–767 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/10942912.2019.1603997">doi:10.1080/10942912.2019.1603997</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Sarmento et al., 2023 – Fluorescent beans under UV light.</strong> A natural specialty Arabica lot was split into treatments with increasing shares of beans that fluoresce under ultraviolet light, which were then analysed and cupped. Fluorescent beans had a negative effect on the final score and several sensory attributes, which supports the trade's long-standing use of UV lamps to find hidden defects.</p>
<p class="meta">Research, Society and Development 12(8) · <a href="https://doi.org/10.33448/rsd-v12i8.42930">doi:10.33448/rsd-v12i8.42930</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Pasikatan & Dowell, 2001 – Optical sorting of seeds.</strong> Reviews how optical sorters detect insect- and fungus-damaged seeds, from monochromatic and bichromatic visible sorting to near-infrared and fluorescence methods. It names coffee among the commodities sorted at high speed.</p>
<p class="meta">Applied Spectroscopy Reviews 36(4), 399–416 · <a href="https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/30200525/348%20sorting%20systems%20optical%20methods.pdf">USDA-ARS full text</a></p>
</div>
