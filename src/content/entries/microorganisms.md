## What lives on a coffee cherry

A harvested cherry carries a mixed community of bacteria, yeasts and filamentous fungi. Once the fruit is pulped or left to dry, these microbes grow on the sugars and pectin of the pulp and mucilage.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Six groups matter most:

- **Lactic acid bacteria** – *Leuconostoc*, *Lactococcus*, *Lactobacillus* and *Weissella*. They dominate most wet fermentations.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Yeasts** – the yeast community often includes *Candida*, *Hanseniaspora*, *Pichia* and *Saccharomyces*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> *Torulaspora delbrueckii* appears sporadically.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Acetic acid bacteria** – *Acetobacter* and *Gluconobacter*, most abundant in dry processing.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Enterobacteria** – genera such as *Pantoea*, *Erwinia* and *Klebsiella*.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Their numbers rise at the start of wet fermentation and then fall.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- ***Bacillus*** – one of several bacterial genera with pectin-degrading activity.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Filamentous fungi** – moulds such as *Aspergillus*, *Penicillium*, *Fusarium*, *Mucor* and *Rhizopus*.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Their presence is undesirable.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## What the microbes do

**Break down mucilage.** Pectin-degrading (pectinolytic) activity has been found in the bacteria *Klebsiella*, *Erwinia*, *Bacillus* and *Lactobacillus*,<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> and in yeasts: several *Pichia* strains produce the enzyme polygalacturonase.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Yeasts are considered key both for pectinolysis and for aroma precursors.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Consume sugars.** In wet fermentation, microbes use sucrose first, then glucose and fructose, then citric and malic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Make metabolites.** Lactic acid bacteria produce lactic acid; yeasts produce ethanol and aroma compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In a Colombian fermentation, lactic acid and acetaldehyde were the main end products of the lactic acid bacteria and of *Pichia*, respectively.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Lactic acid, acetic acid and mannitol formed in the mucilage were later found inside the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Spoil.** Too much acetic acid, or propionic and butanoic acids, cause vinegary and onion-like defects.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Some moulds give mouldy, earthy flavours, and some *Aspergillus* species produce ochratoxin A.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Succession in wet and dry processing

**Wet processing.** In Ecuador, lactic acid bacteria were the most common group during wet processing, with enterobacteria and the yeasts *Pichia* and *Starmerella*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A cherry-to-cup study there found lactic acid bacteria already prevalent before fermentation began; the pooled cherries and depulped beans carried high counts of lactic acid bacteria, acetic acid bacteria, enterobacteria and yeasts alike.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The standard 16-hour fermentation was marked by an initial presence of enterobacteria (and, to a lesser degree, acetic acid bacteria), by *Leuconostoc* as the prevalent lactic acid bacterium, and by a transient peak of *Lactococcus lactis*.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Enterobacteria and acetic acid bacteria then declined steadily.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In the extended 64-hour fermentation, the lactic acid bacteria shifted from *L. pseudomesenteroides* to lactobacilli: *Lactobacillus vaccinostercus*, *L. brevis* and *L. plantarum*.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> *L. pseudomesenteroides* and the yeast *Pichia kluyveri* were important community members, whether or not the beans were soaked afterwards.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In Colombia, *Leuconostoc* dominated the whole process and peaked at 84% of the bacterial community after 24 hours; the yeast *Pichia nakasei* dominated the fungi.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Dry processing.** On drying whole cherries, acetic acid bacteria (*Acetobacter*, *Gluconobacter*) were most abundant, with *Pichia*, *Candida*, *Starmerella* and *Saccharomycopsis* yeasts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Heaping the cherries favoured *Acetobacter*, *Gluconobacter* and the yeast *Saccharomycopsis crataegensis*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Place, altitude and method

The community is not the same everywhere. The Colombian study found more than 50 genera never before reported in coffee fermentation, associated with soil, seawater, plants, insects and human contact.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The authors of a Chinese trial note that the effect of any processing parameter depends on variety and local geographical conditions.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Altitude alone can shift the community. In Brazil, natural-processed coffee of the same variety from one region, all given the same self-induced anaerobic fermentation, showed *Gluconobacter* and *Weissella* dominating at 800 and 1,000 m and more acetic acid at 1,200 and 1,400 m; the cold-adapted yeast *Cystofilobasidium* was the main fungus at all altitudes.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Metabolic pathways

**Homofermentative and heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria.** Homofermentative lactic acid bacteria turn sugars mainly into lactic acid. Heterofermentative ones also produce ethanol, esters and diacetyl.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In coffee, *Leuconostoc*, *Lactobacillus* and *Lactococcus* are named among the heterofermentative producers of alcohols, aldehydes and esters.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A rise in mannitol in the mucilage is a marker of heterofermentative *Leuconostoc* activity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Alcoholic fermentation by yeasts.** In fermented foods generally, yeasts ferment sugars mainly to ethanol and carbon dioxide, with higher alcohols, esters and aldehydes as secondary products.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Inoculating *Pichia fermentans* raised ethanol, acetaldehyde, ethyl acetate and isoamyl acetate and lowered lactic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Dominant inoculated yeasts used the mucilage sugars faster and more completely, giving two to three times more glycerol, alcohols, aldehydes, esters and organic acids in the fermented green beans.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Acetic oxidation.** In cocoa fermentation, acetic acid bacteria (most often *Acetobacter pasteurianus*) oxidise the ethanol made by yeasts and convert lactic acid into acetic acid and acetoin.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In dry-processed coffee, acetic acid bacteria were linked with gluconic acid and sugar alcohols building up in the drying outer layers of the cherry.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At lower concentrations acetic acid tastes clean and sweet; at high concentrations it becomes vinegary.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## How microbes are studied

**Culture-dependent methods** grow microbes on selective agar media, count them and identify isolates. Zhang et al. used this approach for five target groups.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In Yunnan it found *Leuconostoc* as the most prevalent lactic acid bacterium.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Isolates obtained this way can later be tested as starters.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Culture-independent methods** read DNA directly from the sample. Earlier work used DGGE fingerprinting.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Current studies use amplicon sequencing of marker genes – the V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene and the fungal ITS1 region<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> or the 18S rRNA gene.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> These methods can reveal wide diversity: the Colombian study found 160 bacterial genera from 10 phyla.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The Ecuadorian and Chinese studies combined both approaches.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Starter cultures

A starter culture is a selected strain added to steer fermentation. The main studies so far:

- **Semi-dry processing, Arabica** – *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (two strains), *Candida parapsilosis* and *Pichia guilliermondii*. The inoculated yeasts persisted throughout fermentation, and the inoculated coffee showed a caramel flavour absent from the control.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Wet processing on-farm** – *Pichia fermentans* YC5.2 gave coffees with intense vanilla taste and floral aromas.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Wet processing, Australia (lab)** – *Hanseniaspora uvarum* and *Pichia kudriavzevii*, isolated from coffee fermentations. The inoculated coffees, especially with *P. kudriavzevii*, scored higher for flavour, aroma and acidity, though for most attributes, including the overall score, the differences were not statistically significant.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Canephora** – *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*, *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum*, *Torulaspora delbrueckii* and *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* have been tested alone and in co-culture; in another trial *Bacillus licheniformis*, alone and with the yeast *Meyerozyma guilliermondii*, correlated with volatiles such as 5-methylfurfural, which the review links to almond, caramel and chocolate notes.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Ochratoxin A** – in coffee only *Aspergillus* species produce it: *A. ochraceus* and relatives, *A. niger* and relatives, and *A. carbonarius*.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> These belong to sections *Circumdati* and *Nigri*.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> *Penicillium* species also make OTA, but mainly in temperate and colder zones; they are not involved in coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> *Penicillium* on coffee matters for off-flavours, not OTA.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The key control is to limit the time coffee spends on the drying yard at a water activity of 0.80–0.95.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Over-fermentation** – high acetic acid gives a vinegary taste; propionic and butanoic acids give an onion flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Mould off-flavours** – *Aspergillus* section *Nigri* and *Penicillium crustosum* produced mouldy, earthy flavours.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Open questions

- How much the metabolites that reach the seed shape flavour after roasting is still being researched.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- Whether a starter truly takes over, or coexists with the resident community, differs between studies (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- Many genera recently found by sequencing have unknown roles in coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>De Bruyn et al., 2017 – Microbiota of wet and dry processing.</strong> Follows Typica coffee in Ecuador through wet and dry variants with DNA sequencing and metabolite analysis. Lactic acid bacteria led wet processing; acetic acid bacteria and yeasts led dry processing, and several microbial metabolites reached the seed.</p>
<p class="meta">Applied and Environmental Microbiology 83(1), e02398-16 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.02398-16">doi:10.1128/AEM.02398-16</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Zhang et al., 2019 – From cherry to cup.</strong> Tracks the microbiota and metabolites of wet processing in Ecuador with plating and amplicon sequencing. Lactic acid bacteria were already prevalent before fermentation; enterobacteria and acetic acid bacteria were present early and then declined, <em>Leuconostoc</em> led the standard fermentation with a transient <em>Lactococcus</em> peak, and lactobacilli took over in extended fermentation. Longer-fermented coffee had more notes preferred by consumers.</p>
<p class="meta">Applied and Environmental Microbiology 85(6), e02635-18 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.02635-18">doi:10.1128/AEM.02635-18</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>de Oliveira Junqueira et al., 2019 – Colombian fermentation by sequencing.</strong> The first amplicon-sequencing study of spontaneous Colombian fermentation. It found very high bacterial diversity, <em>Leuconostoc</em> dominance and <em>Pichia nakasei</em> as the main yeast.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 9, 8794 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-45002-8">doi:10.1038/s41598-019-45002-8</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Evangelista et al., 2014 – Yeast starters in semi-dry processing.</strong> Inoculates Arabica with four yeast strains and follows them with DGGE. The starters persisted and gave a caramel note not found in the control.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Microbiology 44, 87–95 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fm.2014.05.013">doi:10.1016/j.fm.2014.05.013</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Elhalis et al., 2021 – Yeast starters in wet fermentation.</strong> Compares fermentations inoculated with <em>Hanseniaspora uvarum</em> or <em>Pichia kudriavzevii</em> with a spontaneous control. The starters used sugars faster and raised flavour metabolites in the beans; cup scores were higher, but most differences were not statistically significant.</p>
<p class="meta">Frontiers in Microbiology 12, 713969 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2021.713969">doi:10.3389/fmicb.2021.713969</a></p>
