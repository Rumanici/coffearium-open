## What a coffee seed is made of

The green coffee bean is a seed. It is plano-convex: one side flat with a lengthwise furrow, the other side rounded.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In *C. arabica* it is 10–18 mm long and 6.5–9.5 mm wide.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Inside the fruit, each seed sits in a hard shell, the endocarp, which becomes the parchment after drying.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The fruit layers around it are described in [Coffee cherry anatomy](/entries/coffee-cherry-anatomy/).

Most of the seed is endosperm, the tissue that is later roasted.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It is alive, with a hard outer region and a soft inner region.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The embryo is small, only 3–4 mm long.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It has an axis and two cotyledons, and it lies close to the convex surface.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The embryo carries few reserves of its own. It depends on the endosperm for nutrients.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The silverskin is the thin seed coat, also called the spermoderm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to a coffee science textbook chapter, part of it sits inside the fold of the bean and is set free in grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## A seed that is still alive

A seed is viable when it can still germinate. Coffee seeds stay alive through harvest and processing.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> How they sprout is covered in [Germination and seedlings](/entries/germination-and-seedlings/).

Viability does not last long. Arabica seeds lose it quickly after 4–6 months at ambient temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The parchment makes a difference. In a 2008 study, washed, natural and semi-dry processed coffees were stored for two years; the washed coffee was kept either in parchment or hulled.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Seeds kept in parchment stayed viable for more than a year; all the other coffees lost viability within 6 months.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The authors suggest that this loss of viability contributes to the fading aroma of old green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Why coffee seeds are hard to store

Seed scientists sort seeds by how they store. Orthodox seeds are shed dry and tolerate further drying.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Recalcitrant seeds are shed wet and die when dried.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Coffee fits neither group. A 1990 study suggested a third, intermediate category for it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Coffee seeds survive partial drying but not complete drying.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> When dry, they are also injured by cold.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a 1979 study, moist arabica seeds stored at 5 °C lost their viability at once.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Cold storage of dry seed, the usual seed-bank method, therefore does not keep coffee for long.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## How coffee diversity is kept

Because the seeds are hard to store, coffee germplasm is kept in field collections: living trees in field genebanks.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Field collections need land and labour, and they are exposed to environmental hazards and pathogens.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the 2017 global conservation strategy for coffee, collections share their material mainly as seed.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The long-term alternative is cryopreservation: storing seeds in liquid nitrogen.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For seeds that cannot be stored in the usual way, it is the only method available for long-term conservation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the same strategy, some coffee genebanks already keep part of their collection this way.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The Ethiopian collections are described in [Ethiopian landraces](/entries/ethiopian-landraces/).

## Endosperm and embryo in detail

**Origin of the tissues.** Up to mid-development, the young fruit consists mainly of pericarp and perisperm.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The perisperm then gradually disappears and is replaced by the endosperm, the true seed.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The endosperm is liquid at first and hardens as the fruit matures.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The silverskin is described as a remnant of the perisperm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How the fruit grows is covered in [Fruit development](/entries/fruit-development/).

**Reserves.** The main reserves of the seed are storage proteins, sucrose and complex polysaccharides.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The endosperm cell walls consist of cellulose and hemicelluloses, mainly insoluble mannans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The lateral endosperm is extremely hard because the mannan is laid down as very thick cell walls.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The endosperm cap, in front of the radicle tip, has thinner walls.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> These cell walls are what roasting later breaks open; see [Porosity and cell structure](/entries/porosity-and-cell-structure/).

**Embryo.** The embryo has an axis and two adherent, heart-shaped cotyledons.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Polyembryony and empty seeds have been reported at a frequency of 1.2%, in a 1941 study cited by the 2006 review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The endocarp drastically inhibits germination, so it is usually removed before germination tests.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Desiccation and cold

The 2006 review gives seed water content on a dry-weight basis: grams of water per gram of dry matter (g/g).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The figure compiles the values from the 2006 review.

<figure>
<div data-figure="seed-anatomy-and-storage-water-content"></div>
<figcaption>Water contents at which coffee seeds were found to survive, lose half their viability or die, in g of water per g of dry weight, as compiled in a 2006 review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup></figcaption>
</figure>

**How dry.** Ellis and colleagues gave about 0.09 g/g as the lowest water content tolerated by *C. arabica* seeds, 0.10–0.12 g/g for *C. canephora* and 0.24 g/g for *C. liberica*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Their 1990 study tested four arabica cultivars after hermetic storage for up to 12 months.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Most seeds of one cultivar withstood drying to 5–6% moisture content (wet basis), while the other three were much more sensitive.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Species differ.** A 1999 study modelled desiccation sensitivity in nine *Coffea* species.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The water content at which half of the initial viability is lost (WC50) ranged from 0.05 g/g in *C. pseudozanguebariae* to 0.38 g/g in *C. humilis*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A follow-up study linked this to climate.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In simulations from rainfall records, seed development lasted about as long as the wet season, so mature seeds are shed at the start of the next dry season.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The mean number of dry months that seeds must survive after shedding was significantly correlated with their desiccation sensitivity; seed water content at maturity was not.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Cold.** Arabica seeds stored at 5 °C with 0.15–0.50 g/g lost their viability at once (1979 study); the review calls this an apparent predisposal to chilling injury.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Ellis and colleagues found survival at −20 °C only at about 0.10–0.12 g/g.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the 1990 study, seeds lost viability faster when cool or below 0 °C and dry than when warmer or moister – the reverse of orthodox seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Viability in storage

A 2024 Brazilian study stored seeds of ten arabica cultivars for 24 months.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The seeds were pulped, fermented for 24 hours and dried to 35 ± 1% moisture (wet basis).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> They were sealed in 0.10 mm plastic and kept at 25 ± 2 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The parchment was removed for the germination test.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

<figure>
<div data-figure="seed-anatomy-and-storage-germination"></div>
<figcaption>Germination of arabica seeds of ten cultivars at harvest and after 24 months of storage in sealed plastic at about 25 °C. Where both values are close, they are given together: at harvest · after 24 months.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup></figcaption>
</figure>

At harvest, germination was 87–99%.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> After 24 months it ranged from 2% (Japi) and 9% (Caturra IAC-476) to 99% (Catuaí IAC-62).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The authors conclude that 24 months of storage lowers germination.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Catuaí IAC-62 is the one cultivar whose germination rose, from 87% to 99%.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

The 2008 storage study adds the effect of the parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> It also found that glucose and fructose fell slightly and glutamine fell clearly during storage, and that these changes did not follow the loss of viability.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Re-imbibed seeds turned bluish green, which the authors attribute possibly to oxidised chlorogenic acids reacting with amino compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Cryopreservation

**The protocol.** The IRD protocol (Institut de Recherche pour le Développement, Montpellier) dries seeds to 0.20 g/g, then freezes them in two stages.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A slow cooling step at 1 °C per minute comes first, then immersion in liquid nitrogen; the seeds are rewarmed fast and rehydrated slowly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**What decides survival.** A 2006 study found that survival depends on the quality of the seed lot and on the rewarming regime.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Slow rewarming in ambient air was highly damaging.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Drying at 81% relative humidity gave much higher survival than the 78% used until then.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> With precooling in a dry-ice bath for 25 min and rewarming in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min, survival reached 89%, not significantly different from the initial viability.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Genotypes differ.** A 2005 study froze seeds of 30 wild arabica accessions from the CATIE field genebank in Costa Rica.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Seed viability after cryopreservation varied from 0 to 74% between accessions.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The embryos survived well; the losses were due to damage to the endosperm only.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Why storage in parchment helps.** The 2008 study shows the difference; the abstract consulted for this entry does not give the mechanism.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Cultivar differences in longevity.** In the 2024 study, germination after 24 months ranged from 2% to 99% between cultivars; no study consulted for this entry explains the spread.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Cryopreservation at genebank scale.** Survival differs widely between accessions and depends on the endosperm.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> According to the 2017 conservation strategy, field collections still hold most accessions.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

<p><strong>Selmar, Bytof & Knopp, 2008 – Storage of green coffee.</strong> Stored washed, natural and semi-dry processed arabica for two years, the washed coffee in parchment or hulled, and followed viability with a tetrazolium test, sugars and amino acids. Seeds in parchment stayed viable for more than a year; all other coffees lost viability within 6 months.</p>
<p class="meta">Annals of Botany 101(1), 31–38 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcm277">doi:10.1093/aob/mcm277</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Dussert & Engelmann, 2006 – Surviving liquid nitrogen.</strong> Showed that seed-lot quality and the rewarming regime decide whether arabica seeds survive cryopreservation. Drying at 81% relative humidity, dry-ice precooling and fast rewarming gave 89% survival.</p>
<p class="meta">CryoLetters 27(3), 169–178</p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Vasquez et al., 2005 – Wild arabica in liquid nitrogen.</strong> Froze seeds of 30 accessions from the CATIE field genebank. Survival ranged from 0 to 74% between accessions, and the damage was in the endosperm, not the embryo.</p>
<p class="meta">Seed Science and Technology 33(2), 293–301</p>
</div>
