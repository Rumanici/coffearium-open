## What a roast profile is

A roast profile is the course of temperature over time during a roast. Roasters log the temperature from a probe in the bean mass at set time intervals, together with the time of first crack, from the charge (coffee entering the roaster) to the drop (coffee leaving it).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The word is used in two senses: the planned path of the beans through the machine, and the flavour that path is meant to produce in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

A roaster steers four main parameters: the degree of roast (final product temperature), the roasting time, the shape of the time–temperature curve and the air-to-bean ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Two roasts can reach the same colour in the same total time by different paths, and the path changes how much water leaves the bean early, the reactions that follow and the final bean structure.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Where the overview of roasting describes what happens inside the bean, this entry is about the curve itself: how it is measured, how it is read and how it is controlled.

## What the probes measure

Real bean surface or core temperature is hard to measure. Production roasters therefore use a probe placed where it stays in contact with the beans but also with hot air. Its reading is always a mix of bean surface and air temperature: good enough for process control, but specific to the machine design, so values from one roaster cannot simply be compared with another.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Bean probe.** The thermocouple in the bean mass. Its readings, logged over time, are the basis of the profile.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Research roasters are sometimes fitted with extra sensors to follow bean temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Air probes.** Roasters also log air temperatures. A pilot-scale air roaster, for example, recorded inlet air, outlet (exhaust) air and product temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Conventional automatic control holds a set hot-air temperature by adjusting the burner.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A flame change takes longer to change the roasting environment than an airflow change.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Bean core.** Core temperature has been measured in small-scale experiments but is usually not possible in industrial roasting<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>; a fine thermocouple is pushed into a drilled hole. In a laboratory fluid bed, the bean core never reached the set air temperatures of 260 °C and 228 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**The dip after charge.** Before the charge, the roaster is preheated (in one study to 210 ± 5 °C for at least 30 min).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> When the green coffee goes in, the reading drops, because beans and air at ambient temperature (about 20 °C) surround the probe.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The lowest point is the **turning point**; after it, the reading rises quickly because the temperature difference between the hot air and the cool beans is large.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A thermocouple also takes time to respond, and one heat-transfer model adds a correction for exactly that.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The dip and the turning point therefore describe the probe in its machine as much as the beans. The roasting overview's warning applies with full force here: the reading depends on probe position, air and machine design, so a turning point is a useful marker for comparing batches on one roaster, not a bean temperature to compare between roasters.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Rate of rise

The **rate of rise** (RoR) is the slope of the bean-probe curve: how much the temperature climbs per unit of time. Mathematically it is the first derivative of temperature over time. One study logged it with roasting software as the temperature increase per 30 s.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

- **It peaks just after the turning point.** In the same study, the "initial RoR" was defined as the highest positive RoR immediately after the turning point.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **It usually falls.** As the beans heat up, the gap between air and bean narrows and so does the heat flow. In heat-transfer models, the heat flux at the bean surface is proportional to the difference between the roasting temperature and the bean surface temperature<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>, and in an air roaster at low load, the rate of change of product temperature tended to zero.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **But a falling RoR is a choice, not a law.** In seven profiles of one coffee roasted on a 5 kg drum, the initial RoR was 5.26–10.37 °C per 30 s. The final RoR, over the last minute, ranged from 0.08 °C per 30 s in a "fast start" profile to 5.32 °C per 30 s in an "extended Maillard" profile.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **It reads the probe, not the bean.** Because the RoR is computed from the bean-probe reading, it inherits that reading's machine-specific mix of bean and air temperature<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> and the thermocouple's response time.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> How the interval and smoothing settings of logging software shape the RoR has not been studied in the sources found.

Roasters act on it: at first crack, for example, a roaster may adjust the flame and log the change to finesse the rate at which heat rises.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Practitioner terms for particular RoR shapes – the "crash" and the "flick" – are explained in the Deep dive.

**Development time ratio.** In the trade press, some roasters express development as a share of the total roast time, the development time ratio; this is a practitioner convention.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The studies that tested development time worked in absolute seconds, not ratios.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Converted from the timings of one study (first crack at about 570 s, development 90–390 s), its four roasts spanned roughly 14% to 41% (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> No peer-reviewed test of the ratio itself was found.

**Second crack.** Lighter roasts are cooled soon after first crack, darker ones near second crack.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Some research profiles run through it: in the titratable-acidity study above, all roasts lasted 16 min and continued past second crack.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Logging and comparing profiles

A roast log can be kept with a stopwatch, notebook and pencil, or on a laptop.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It should hold at least the bean-probe temperature at set intervals and the time and temperature of first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Logging software records the curve and the RoR automatically; studies cited here used commercial roasting software for this.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The senses still matter: if a probe fails or drifts out of calibration, a roaster who can read colour, size, texture and aroma from the trier can still finish the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Comparing profiles between machines is hard.** The probe reading is specific to the machine design.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Airflow, drum speed and the air-to-bean ratio change how heat reaches the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Studies define roast phases by events the beans show – colour change, first crack, second crack<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> – and the sensory studies report final colour, not probe temperatures alone.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> When researchers did make the bean temperature follow the same curve on a drum and a laboratory fluid bed, the kinetics of aroma formation were similar in both.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Why the curve bends

**Heat flows down a temperature difference.** Single-bean models treat the heat flux into the bean as proportional to the difference between the roasting temperature and the bean surface.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> At charge the difference is largest, which is why the reading climbs fast after the turning point<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>; as the bean warms, the difference and the heat flow shrink<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>, and how the rate of rise develops then depends on how the roaster sets the heat.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Water slows the early roast.** Evaporating water absorbs heat: in a 2025 single-bean model, evaporative cooling starts at 100 °C, and without heat generation the model underestimated bean temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Early in the roast a lot of energy goes into water; later the process becomes progressively more exothermic and needs much less heat input.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The same 2025 model assumed, from earlier literature, heat generation inside the bean above 150 °C<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>; where exactly the switch happens is disputed (see the roasting overview).

**Inside the bean.** A 2017 study compared two single-bean models. The one adapted from earlier work predicts that after the first minute, temperature and moisture inside the bean become nearly uniform; the authors' new model, whose drying front they consider more realistic, shows a much slower drying front.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In extreme fast roasting, however, a steep gradient can leave the surface over-roasted and the core under-roasted.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No probe in the bean mass can see this.

**Probe lag.** A thermocouple in the roasting chamber responds slowly. A spouted-bed model added a thermal response coefficient to correct for it, and the authors note that using the thermocouple reading as bean temperature reduces the accuracy of the fitted parameters.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Steering the curve

**Air-temperature control versus product-temperature control.** Traditional automation sets and holds a hot-air temperature, in one stage or several. The beans' temperature then varies from batch to batch with green-coffee moisture, weather and a cold roaster. More advanced systems follow a product-temperature master curve stored in the recipe and adjust the energy input continuously, so the beans see the same temperature development every batch.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Manual levers.** On small drum roasters the artisan works mainly with the flame and the trier; drum speed and airflow are used less often. Airflow changes convection almost instantly, whereas a flame change takes longer to reach the beans. Too high a drum speed can press beans against the hot wall and scorch the flats.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A specialty roast typically starts near maximum burner output and then steps the power down to slow the browning reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

In a multivariate study on a professional roaster, the variables tested were initial drum temperature, the airflow opening, drum speed, the rate of increase in gas pressure to the burners, and the time after first crack; time after first crack had the largest influence on both sensory and chemical data.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Shapes of the curve.** Keeping final colour and total time constant, a slow-start-fast-final profile usually gave more bean volume and porosity than a traditional one, sometimes a higher extraction yield, and a statistically different flavour profile.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2024 titratable-acidity study compared seven profiles named fast start, slow start, medium, production, exaggerated flick, negative rate of rise and extended Maillard; fast start meant high initial heat followed by a constant deceleration, slow start the reverse.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Crash and flick

Two RoR shapes have practitioner names. In practitioner usage, a **crash** is a sudden plunge of the bean-temperature RoR during first crack; a **flick** is the RoR recovering and turning upwards again towards the end of first crack or the end of the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> One practitioner course claims that a crash can make a coffee slightly less round and sweet and that a flick leads to roasty or ashy flavours.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

The evidence is thin. No peer-reviewed sensory study of crashes or flicks was found. The one peer-reviewed study that roasted an "exaggerated flick" profile measured titratable acidity, not flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Neither the mechanism of the crash nor whether it reflects the beans or the probe has been established in the sources found; the probe reading is shaped by heat released inside the beans, evaporative cooling and the thermocouple's response time.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Profiles and flavour: what research shows

**Colour first.** Eight sensory studies on a 1 kg drum and a 15 kg Loring roaster found that, in the two studies that varied both, roast colour predicted flavour better than timing.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors note that colour measurement is often left out of quality control in specialty roasteries.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Then development time.** At constant colour, development time had a larger effect on flavour than time to first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In a follow-up at one colour, development of 90–390 s shifted the cup from fruity and acidic towards roasty and bitter, and the acids measured generally declined with longer development.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> To keep the colour the same, the end temperature had to be lowered as development got longer.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Path matters at fixed colour.** On the same raw material and roaster, roasting to the same degree along different time–temperature paths gives different flavour profiles.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> High-temperature short-time roasting changed bean physical properties and the kinetics of aroma formation compared with low-temperature long-time roasting<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>; a doctoral thesis on hot-air roasting called the temperature profile the most crucial parameter in process design.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Acidity over the roast.** In a 5 kg drum, titratable acidity rose from the start of the roast, peaked at first crack whatever the profile, and had fallen back almost to its initial value by the onset of second crack. The profile changed how fast this happened, but the peak itself differed little between profiles.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Same curve, different machine.** When a drum and a laboratory fluid bed followed the same bean-temperature profile, aroma formation was similar.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The catch for practice is that production probes do not show the bean temperature directly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Stalling and baking.** A "baked" roast stretches the time after first crack with little or no rise in temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> On the curve this is a flat line with a rate of rise near zero.
- **Underdevelopment.** In a defect study, an underdeveloped roast was made with a much lower air temperature at charge (135 °C) and 8 min more roast time; the stalling early in the curve was meant to suppress typical coffee aromas, yet most aroma compounds ended up at levels comparable to the normal roast.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Running too fast.** Very fast heating can over-roast the surface while the core stays under-roasted, giving burnt and greenish notes together.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Trusting the probe too far.** Probes can fail or drift out of calibration; opening the trier also lets cool air into the drum and can change the final stages of the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Copying profiles between roasters.** Because readings are machine-specific, a profile copied as numbers from another roaster cannot be expected to reproduce the same bean temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **RoR shapes and flavour.** No peer-reviewed sensory test of crashes, flicks or a declining RoR as such was found; the one study using these profiles measured acidity only.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Development time ratio.** The ratio is promoted in the trade press<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>, but the studies found tested development time in seconds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **From probe to bean.** Models can estimate bean temperature from operating conditions<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>, but production roasters still log a machine-specific probe reading.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Software settings.** How the interval and smoothing chosen in logging software change the displayed RoR, and the decisions roasters take from it, has not been studied in the sources found.
- **Range of coffees.** The sensory evidence comes mainly from washed Arabica on a few drum and convection roasters.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
