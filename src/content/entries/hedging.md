## What hedging is

Coffee prices are unstable.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A business that owns coffee it has not yet sold loses when prices fall. A business that has sold coffee at a fixed price, but has not yet bought it, loses when prices rise.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Both carry price risk.

A hedger offsets that risk on a futures market. The US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) describes a hedger as someone who takes futures positions opposite to their cash-market positions. In coffee, the cash market is physical coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2017 coffee textbook chapter defines hedging as the temporary purchase or sale of futures in place of a later physical transaction.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The idea is simple. When the price of the physical coffee moves one way, the futures position moves the other way. The loss on one leg is largely balanced by the gain on the other.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to the International Coffee Organization (ICO), a futures hedge lessens price risk but does not remove it completely.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

How the futures contracts themselves work – lot sizes, delivery months, the clearing house – is explained in [Coffee futures](/entries/coffee-futures/). This entry is about using them.

## Who hedges, and in which direction

The International Trade Centre (ITC) states that producers, exporters, importers and roasters all need a strategy for price volatility. Most such strategies use coffee futures and options.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Which side of the market a firm takes depends on its physical position, not on its role.

- **Short hedge.** A firm that owns coffee, or has bought it at a fixed price, sells futures. Growers and importers tend to be short hedgers.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Long hedge.** A firm that has sold coffee at a fixed price but not yet bought it buys futures. The CFTC defines a long hedge as buying futures to protect against higher costs.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Roasters with fixed-price customer contracts are typical long hedgers.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Exporters and traders.** According to the same textbook chapter, exporters often lean to the long side. They have usually sold more coffee forward than they have bought and fixed.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> An exporter holding unsold stock hedges short instead.

## A worked example (hypothetical)

The numbers in this example are invented, round values chosen for illustration. They are not market prices and not a recommendation.

An exporter holds unsold washed arabica, equal in weight to one Coffee “C” futures contract. One contract is 37,500 lb, or about 17 tonnes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In month 0, futures stand at 300 US cents per lb. The exporter expects to sell the coffee at futures plus a differential of 10, so at 310 ¢/lb. To protect that value, it sells one futures contract at 300.

| Step (hypothetical) | A · Prices rise | B · Prices fall |
|---|---|---|
| Month 0: futures sold | 300 ¢/lb | 300 ¢/lb |
| Month 2: futures price | 340; futures loss of 40 ¢/lb paid in margin calls | 280; futures gain of 20 ¢/lb credited |
| Month 4: physical sale (futures + differential 8) | 330 + 8 = 338 ¢/lb | 260 + 8 = 268 ¢/lb |
| Month 4: futures bought back | at 330: loss of 30 ¢/lb | at 260: gain of 40 ¢/lb |
| Net price | 338 − 30 = 308 ¢/lb | 268 + 40 = 308 ¢/lb |

In both cases the exporter ends at 308 ¢/lb: the futures price of the hedge (300) plus the differential at the time of sale (8). Without the hedge it would have received 338 or 268. The hedge gave up the gain in case A to avoid the loss in case B (our arithmetic on invented numbers).

Two things did not work out exactly. The expected 310 became 308, because the differential fell from 10 to 8. And in case A, the exporter had to pay the futures loss in cash in month 2, before it sold any coffee. The next two sections explain both.

## What a hedge cannot cover

**The differential.** A futures hedge protects only the futures part of the price. The gap between one lot and the futures price is the differential, also called the basis. It can widen or narrow while the hedge is open. The CFTC calls this basis risk.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A World Bank paper describes the basis as a source of uncertainty that the farmer cannot remove.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Fairtrade’s price risk guidance names the same differential risk for producer organisations.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> What moves differentials is explained in [Differentials](/entries/differentials/).

**Cash for margins.** Futures gains and losses are settled every day. A hedger deposits money with a broker when the position is opened. It must pay in more – a margin call – when the futures price moves against it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In a short hedge, a rising market brings margin calls at once. The matching gain on the coffee comes only when it is sold (our reading of the example). In early 2025 the ICO suggested that higher operating costs may have led some traders to liquidate positions.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Quantity and quality.** A futures contract is a fixed quantity of exchange-grade coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A hedge on a crop not yet harvested assumes the crop will come in. The textbook chapter warns that a forward sale before harvest brings new risks if yields, weather or disease prevent delivery.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Why most smallholders do not hedge directly

Futures markets were built for large, creditworthy traders. One Coffee “C” contract is 37,500 lb of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> That is about 284 bags of 60 kg (our calculation). A 2004 World Bank review notes that futures contracts cover large volumes and that trading them involves large fixed costs.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Money is a second barrier. A hedger must place a deposit and be able to meet margin calls.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2017 textbook chapter states that hedging tools are open only to those with enough money to pay for them.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Fairtrade’s guidance adds that many small producer organisations lack the knowledge and resources to hedge themselves.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

Smallholders therefore reach price risk tools indirectly. A World Bank paper expects access through a marketing cooperative that hedges future sales on the farmers’ behalf.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Other routes in the sources consulted for this entry:

- **Forward contracts with a buyer.** According to the textbook, a contract agreed before harvest is the only feasible instrument for most producers and exporters. It also serves as collateral for credit.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Exporters and buyers who hedge.** According to Fairtrade’s guidance, most first buyers of Fairtrade coffee are traders who often hedge the price risk.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Price-hedged credit.** In Guatemala, the national coffee association Anacafé facilitated price-hedged credit for smallholder producers, according to the 2004 World Bank review.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Minimum prices.** A minimum-price contract gives a floor without a futures account. Fairtrade sets a minimum price for certified coffee; from 1 December 2026 it is USD 2.00 per lb FOB for conventional washed arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Details are in [Certifications and labels](/entries/certifications-and-labels/).

How the futures part of a physical contract is fixed is explained in [Price fixation](/entries/price-fixation/).

## Options as price insurance

An option gives its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) a futures contract at a set price.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A put works like insurance for a seller: it sets a floor, and the seller keeps the gain if prices rise. The buyer pays a premium up front; a World Bank paper notes that a producer who has bought an option carries no counterparty risk on it.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Coffee “C” options cover one futures contract of 37,500 lb.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The premium is the cost: a World Bank paper notes it can be expensive exactly when protection is most needed.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Options get their own planned entry, Options on coffee futures.

## Who hedges what

| Hedger | Physical position | Futures hedge | Main risk left | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Producer or cooperative with an unsold crop | Long (owns or will own coffee) | Sell futures (short hedge), directly or through a buyer | Differential; yield shortfall; margin cash | <sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> |
| Importer holding unsold stock | Long | Sell futures | Differential at resale | <sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> |
| Exporter with forward sales not yet bought | Short physicals | Buy futures | Differential at purchase | <sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> |

The textbook chapter works through a full chain: a roaster long one September contract, a Brazilian cooperative short one contract, and a farmer who sold forward to the cooperative. When the coffee ships, the roaster passes its long futures to the cooperative as an exchange for physicals. Both hedges close at one price.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The same example is summarised in [Coffee futures](/entries/coffee-futures/) and [How a coffee price is made](/entries/how-a-coffee-price-is-made/).

## Margin calls and liquidity risk

A hedge removes price risk and replaces part of it with funding risk. The World Bank paper of 2002 names both obligations: a deposit when the contract is sold, and possible additional margin calls if the futures price moves against the position.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> For a short hedger, the money flows out when prices rise, which is also when the unsold coffee is worth more. The cash gap lasts until the coffee is sold or can be borrowed against.

The clearing house can raise margins when markets are volatile.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> On 6 December 2024 ICE Futures U.S. raised the initial margin for every Coffee “C” month listed in its notice. For the March 2025 contract it rose from USD 8,096 to USD 9,499 per contract.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> A higher initial margin means more cash tied up for each lot hedged.

In February 2025 the ICO composite indicator averaged 354.32 US cents per lb, and the New York arabica market averaged 388.18 US cents per lb (average of the second and third positions; both nominal).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The ICO’s report for that month notes another rise in ICE margin requirements. It states that the liquidity required and the need for more trade credit raise the cost and risk of doing business, and that banks may be reluctant to lend more.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> It suggests that these higher operating costs may have led some traders to liquidate their positions.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry gives the total of coffee margin calls in 2024 or 2025.

Banks look at hedges from the other side. The ITC states that most banks insist on a solid risk management strategy from coffee operators using borrowed funds.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> According to the textbook chapter, creditors are more willing to lend against hedged stocks, because the hedge sets a reference price for them.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Basis risk in more detail

The basis is the cash price minus the futures price.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A short hedger ends with the hedge price plus the basis at the time of sale; a long hedger pays the hedge price plus the basis at the time of purchase (our arithmetic, as in the example). A hedge thus turns a risk on the whole price into a risk on the basis alone. The 2004 World Bank review defines basis risk as local prices failing to move with exchange prices.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Hedging can also be done physical against physical. The ITC describes offsetting a sale of one coffee with the purchase of another. It calls this very difficult, because the physical market is less liquid than futures and a buyer for one particular coffee may be hard to find at short notice.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

For producers, the currency is a further layer. Coffee “C” futures are priced in US cents per lb,<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> so a dollar hedge does not fix a price in local money. The link between exchange rates and producer prices is discussed in [How a coffee price is made](/entries/how-a-coffee-price-is-made/).

## Intermediated price insurance

An international task force on commodity risk management (ITF), working with the World Bank, tested ways to bring options to coffee producers through local intermediaries.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> According to a 2007 working paper, the intermediary – for example a cooperative, trader, exporter or local bank – buys an option from a wholesale provider and sells floor-price insurance to producers.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The same paper lists test cases in El Salvador, Tanzania, Mexico, Uganda and Nicaragua.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The 2002 World Bank paper reports that linking price insurance with credit was the key to success in the early cases.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> An ICO board paper of 2000 mentions a Price Guarantee Contract launched by the PTA Bank, the Eastern and Southern African Trade and Development Bank, for agricultural chains in East Africa.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The published version of the 2007 study estimated the costs of a put-based scheme from historical option data. It found the costs relatively low and outweighed by the benefits for most producers.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The authors of the 2002 World Bank paper add that price risk management is not a tool for the poorest farmers.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Fixed prices bring a risk of their own for cooperatives. If a cooperative sells forward at a fixed price and the market then rises, members may sell to other buyers with ready cash. According to the textbook chapter, such defaults are rare but real.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Fairtrade’s guidance explains that its standard requires a price risk management strategy for fixed-price contracts, and warns that a sudden price spike can leave producer organisations selling at a loss.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Share of hedged coffee.** No source consulted for this entry measured what share of producers’ coffee is hedged, directly or through intermediaries.
- **Cost of margins for origin firms.** The ICO’s 2025 report describes liquidity pressure qualitatively.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No study consulted here measured the funding cost of margin calls for exporters or cooperatives.
- **Benefit for farm income.** Whether intermediated hedging raises or steadies smallholder income over several seasons was not measured in the sources consulted; the 2007 study infers benefits from field evidence.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
