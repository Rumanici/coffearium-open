## What food safety means for coffee

Coffee passes through many hands before it reaches the cup. At several points, unwanted substances can enter it or form in it. Food law calls these contaminants and sets limits for some of them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

For coffee, the hazards fall into a few groups. Mould toxins can form on the farm and in storage.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Pesticides are used while the crop grows.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Roasting forms compounds such as acrylamide.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Machines can release metals, and cold brew can carry microbes.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Fraud adds cheaper material to the product.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

This entry follows these hazards along the chain and gives the limits authorities set. It describes what the rules and studies say; it gives no health advice. What a cup contains in general is covered in what’s in a cup of coffee.

## Mould toxins: ochratoxin A

Ochratoxin A (OTA) is a toxin made by some moulds. The Codex code of practice for coffee calls it the primary food-safety issue in the production of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In coffee, only some *Aspergillus* species produce it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

OTA forms before the coffee is roasted. The risk lies in the time drying coffee spends in the water-activity range where OTA-producing fungi grow, and again when dried coffee gets wet in storage or transport.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The moisture and water activity entry explains the thresholds. Defective beans and husk sometimes contain more OTA than sound beans.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Roasting breaks down much of it: 65–100%, depending on the roasting process.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The EU sets maximum levels for roasted and soluble coffee, but not for green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Swiss law sets the same values for roasted and soluble coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Pesticide residues

Pesticides are used at different stages of coffee cultivation; insecticides are among the controls discussed for the coffee berry borer.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Traces can remain on the green coffee. EU law sets a maximum residue level (MRL) for each pesticide in each product.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Where no specific level is set, a default of 0.01 mg/kg applies.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

The limits for each product are listed in the EU pesticides database.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Roasting and brewing can lower some residues. A 2022 review reports this for one insecticide, dinotefuran, from a single study.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Compounds formed in roasting

Roasting forms process contaminants such as acrylamide. The EU sets benchmark levels for it: 400 µg/kg for roast coffee and 850 µg/kg for instant coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The roasting contaminants entry covers how acrylamide, furans and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons form, and how roast degree shifts them.

## Machines, capsules and metals

Metal parts of coffee machines are in contact with hot water and coffee. The German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) tested eight new machines in 2013. One of three portafilter espresso machines released large amounts of lead, especially after descaling.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

The BfR states that the EU has no legal limit for metals released from metal food contact materials.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> It recommends rinsing machines thoroughly after descaling.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Capsules are another question. In a 2013 study, the French food safety agency ANSES compared capsule and pod coffee with drip-brewed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> It concluded that capsules did not substantially change earlier conclusions on exposure to chemical contaminants from coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The packaging entry covers capsule materials.

## Microbes and cold brew

A 2020 viewpoint article states that the roasting process and fresh, hot brewing usually minimise microbiological risk.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> It calls cold brew one of the few coffee beverages with some microbiological food-safety hazards.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

Cold brew is made without sterilising temperatures and typically has a pH above 4.6.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The cold brew entry covers the hazards and the rules for cafés.

## Fraud and authenticity

Food fraud in coffee takes three main forms. A 2021 review lists mixing beans of two species of different value, adding other substances or foods, and mislabelling.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

Arabica (*C. arabica*) costs more than robusta (*C. canephora*), which creates an incentive to substitute one for the other.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In Brazil, cheaper materials such as corn, barley, soybean or coffee husks have been used as fillers in roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Laboratories detect them with chromatography, spectroscopy and DNA-based methods.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The coffee oil entry explains the marker used for robusta.

## Ochratoxin A in detail

**Where it arises.** The Codex code describes favourable conditions for OTA-producing fungi during drying.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In storage, moisture can come from damp floors and walls; dried coffee must be protected from rewetting.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Temperature changes during transport can cause condensation of the remaining water.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In lower-grade coffee, fruits with black and sour defects contained the highest OTA levels.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The drying, moisture and water activity, microorganisms and hulling and dry milling entries give the control points in detail.

**What authorities say.** In its 2020 opinion, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) states that OTA causes kidney toxicity in several animal species and kidney tumours in rodents.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> OTA is genotoxic in vitro and in vivo, but the mechanisms are unclear.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> For tumours, the margins of exposure would indicate a possible health concern if genotoxicity is direct.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> EFSA describes the uncertainty as high and notes that the risk may be overestimated.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The opinion covers all foods; its abstract does not give coffee’s share of exposure.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Limits.** EU Regulation 2023/915 sets 3.0 µg/kg for roasted beans and ground roasted coffee and 5.0 µg/kg for soluble coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No entry names green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The Swiss ordinance on contaminants (VHK) lists 3 µg/kg for roasted coffee and 5 µg/kg for soluble coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Pesticide residues in detail

**The EU framework.** Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 requires that products in its Annex I do not exceed the MRLs set in Annexes II and III.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Where no specific MRL is set, 0.01 mg/kg applies.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> According to the Finnish Food Authority, MRLs are set by the European Commission based on opinions of EFSA, and are listed in the EU pesticides database.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> No coffee-specific MRLs were looked up in the database for this entry.

**What studies report.** A 2022 narrative review states that studies have found pesticides in commercial coffee after processing.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> It cites a study in which roasting at 230–240 °C for 12–14 min lowered dinotefuran by 62.2–100%.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The same study reports further losses of dinotefuran on brewing.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The review tabulates EU MRL exceedances in green coffee samples from one study, for example diazinon in 11 samples; it gives the size of that sample set inconsistently (118 and 181).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> These are single-study findings, not monitoring results.

No EU-wide monitoring results for coffee were consulted for this entry. Swiss pesticide residue rules were not checked.

## Metals from machines and capsules

**Lead.** In the BfR’s 2013 tests of eight new machines, one of three portafilter espresso machines released large amounts of lead, especially after descaling.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The BfR considers it possible that acidic descalers release lead from metal components, and states that the source of the lead is unknown.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> It refers to a Council of Europe resolution with a specific release limit of 10 µg of lead per kg of food, a recommendation rather than law.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Its advice is to rinse thoroughly after descaling and run water through before preparing coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The BfR does not name products.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Capsules.** The 2013 ANSES study tested ten brands of capsules against drip-brewed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Average levels of cobalt, chromium, tin, nickel, copper, zinc and acrylamide were slightly higher with capsules, but of the same order of magnitude; aluminium was not among them.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In the data table, aluminium ranged from 1 to 156 µg/l across eight brands, against 78 µg/l in the filter-coffee reference.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> No phthalates or bisphenol A were detected.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Furan was detected in all samples, but no conclusion could be drawn.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Microbiological hazards

A 2020 viewpoint article states that roasting and fresh, hot brewing usually minimise microbiological risk in coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Cold brew differs: it is made without sterilising temperatures, and its pH, typically above 4.6, does not effectively suppress microbial growth.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Citing a summary of challenge studies that is based on personal communications with industry, the article reports that *Salmonella*, *Listeria monocytogenes* and *Escherichia coli* may stay viable in cold brew for 7–28 days; the underlying studies are not named.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> It states that ready-to-drink products under anaerobic conditions must be controlled, by pH or thermal processing, to mitigate the risk of *Clostridium botulinum*, and the authors recommend that cold brew be considered specifically in HACCP systems.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> It also expects pesticides or mycotoxins in cold brew when the raw beans are contaminated.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The cold brew entry gives the US rules for cafés.

## Authenticity testing

**Robusta in arabica.** A 2018 study found 16-O-methylcafestol (16-OMC), long used as a robusta marker, in arabica roast coffee for the first time.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The authors note that the presumed absence of 16-OMC from arabica underpins the DIN 10779 method.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> With their method they reached a detection limit of about 1% w/w robusta in arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The coffee oil and green coffee composition entries cover the chemistry.

**Fillers.** A 2018 review of chromatographic methods names corn, soybean, barley, rye, caramel and coffee husks as the most commonly used adulterants in Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> It reports that adulterants are added before or during roasting, in concentrations of up to 40% (w/w) in roasted coffee sold in Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> According to the review, microscopy is the main technique for controlling the quality of Brazilian coffee, and ultra-performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry is a recent trend for confirming fraud.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

**Origin and other methods.** A 2021 review covers chromatography, spectroscopy and single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) methods to tell arabica from robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> It also describes methods to trace geographical origin and the practice of adding pharmacologically active compounds to coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Coffee’s share of OTA exposure.** The EFSA abstract consulted for this entry does not give the contribution of coffee; the full opinion was not read.
- **Pesticide residues in roasted coffee and the cup.** Only one study on one insecticide, reported in a review, was available for this entry; no EU monitoring data for coffee were consulted.
- **Metals from machines.** The BfR tested eight machines in 2013 and did not identify the source of the lead; no larger survey was consulted for this entry.
- **Allergens.** No source consulted for this entry addressed allergens in coffee.
