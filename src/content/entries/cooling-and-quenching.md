## What cooling does

Cooling is the last stage of a roast, after drying and the roast reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The moment the beans leave the roaster is called the drop.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> From then on, the beans must lose their heat quickly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Speed matters because the roast does not stop by itself. Late in the roast, the reactions release heat, and the beans start to heat themselves.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to a roasting textbook chapter, a few seconds then separate the intended roast degree from an over-roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> If the roast runs on uncontrolled, the beans may catch fire.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Two methods are used, alone or together: cold air, and a measured spray of water called water quenching.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) lists cooling as one of the main operations of a roastery, between roasting and grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

This is the seventh and last step in the roast. **Previous step:** second crack.

## Air cooling

In small drum roasters, the beans drop into a cooling tray.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the roasting textbook chapter, industrial drum roasters most often use a round-bed cooler, a round sieve with a slowly turning stirrer.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Paddle roasters may instead use a rectangular cooling sieve without any stirrer.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Cold air is drawn through the layer of beans. In drum roasters it may flow from below or from above; in paddle roasters it usually flows upwards.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Fluid-bed roasters move the beans into a separate cooling section.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Coolers also release dust. The EPA lists them among the sources of particulate matter, which is typically led to cyclones, separators that spin the dust out of the air.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> After cooling, the beans run through a destoner, an air classifier that removes stones, metal fragments and other waste.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Air cooling leaves the coffee dry. According to a Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) handbook and a textbook chapter, unquenched roasted coffee holds about 1–2% water (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A 2020 multi-study paper gives 1–2.5% moisture for roasted beans in general.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Water quenching

Most medium- to large-scale roasters have a water quenching device.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> When the beans reach their final temperature, it sprays a set amount of cold water onto them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The water evaporates on the hot bean surface, and the evaporation takes the heat away.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Quenching is a precooling step: air cooling still follows.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the textbook chapter, it helps to reach the same roast degree batch after batch.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In one industrial roaster described in a doctoral thesis, water was sprayed first in the roasting chamber and again in the cooling chamber.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

A 2008 doctoral thesis, following earlier work, describes three ways of applying the water.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In spray quenching, fine droplets evaporate on the hot surface. In film quenching, water is poured over the beans, and in immersion quenching the beans are dipped in water.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The thesis judges spray quenching the most efficient and widely used method.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

High-speed video in the same thesis shows how it works. The first droplets evaporate on impact; as the surface cools, later droplets condense on the bean instead.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

Ideally, the water evaporates fully rather than soaking into the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Beans may absorb part of it, and the amount of quench water can be used to set the final moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In one study on a production roaster, different amounts of quench water gave coffees with 2.3–8.8 g water per 100 g (wet basis).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> On a 320 kg bowl roaster, 5 L of water per batch left 2.4 g and 38 L left 5.3 g water per 100 g (wet basis).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## What extra water changes

According to a textbook chapter on grinding, quench water does not spread through the bean at once. It first wets the surface and then moves inwards by diffusion.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> That is why the chapter recommends a rest between roasting and grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Grinding.** In a 2008 study, coffees with more water gave coarser particles on grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> They ground better after a period of equilibration, while dry coffees did not need one.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Above 6 g water per 100 g (wet basis), grinding stayed unacceptable even after 24 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

**Brewing.** In the same study, coffees with less water were extracted more effectively on an espresso machine than wetter ones.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Gas and freshness.** In a 2007 study, quenched coffees with more water lost their carbon dioxide faster.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A fine water spray that left the coffee almost as dry as air cooling made no such difference.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Most aroma compounds were about as stable as in air-cooled coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> One sulphur compound behaved differently, which the authors link to a faster loss of freshness.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the textbook chapter, a high water activity in roasted beans speeds up staling and can shorten shelf life.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Rules and standards

Legal limits apply to the total water in roasted coffee, including any left by quenching. Swiss law allows roasted coffee to contain no more than 5% water by mass.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The Austrian Food Codex sets the same maximum of 5% by weight.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

For quality grading, quenching is excluded. Under SCA 102-2024, the SCA's standard for cupping samples, the roasted sample is air-cooled at once, without water quenching.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Once it reaches room temperature (about 20 °C), the standard recommends airtight storage and a rest of 8–24 hours after roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Physics of cooling

**Why the roast runs on.** According to the roasting textbook chapter, the roast becomes self-accelerating late on, as the beans generate heat of their own; its final stages approach the conditions of a combustion process.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The chapter therefore calls for an abrupt stop with an efficient precooling or cooling step.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A doctoral thesis on hot-air roasting describes cooling as excess cold air, a precise amount of water sprayed onto the hot beans, or both.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Two ways to take heat out.** Cold air carries heat away from the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> According to the 2008 thesis, air cooling takes several minutes and is relatively slow, while water quenching is faster because evaporating water absorbs heat.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In the laboratory trials, passive cooling in a container took 45 minutes and the coffee came out slightly darker than air-cooled coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Quench water removes heat as it evaporates on the bean surface.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The thesis states the aim as full evaporation on the surface rather than a large rise in bean water content.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> According to the roasting textbook chapter, excessive quench water is absorbed and raises the final moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**The bean keeps losing mass.** Cooling is not only a loss of heat. A fluid-bed study estimated what left the beans in the first 20 s after the roast, by extending the measured release curves.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> After a fast roast, 1.6 g water per 100 g green coffee left during cooling; after a slow roast to the same colour, only 0.1 g.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In the fast roast, less water had evaporated in the roaster (10.2 g against 11.4 g per 100 g green coffee), and more left during cooling.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The roast loss entry gives the full mass balance.

## Water, structure and storage

**Redistribution.** Quench water first wets the surface and needs time to spread through the bean by diffusion.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Coffee oil pushed to the surface in dark and fast roasts also needs time to move back inside, according to the grinding chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In the 2008 trials, coffees were ground right after cooling and again after 6 and 24 hours of equilibration.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> High-moisture coffees needed the equilibration; low-moisture coffees did not.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Grinding and extraction.** Coffees with a high water content gave coarser particles.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Coffees with a low water content were extracted more effectively, and percolation through them was slower.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The grinding entry covers how moisture and bean temperature change the particle size distribution.

**Degassing.** The 2007 study compared air cooling and water quench cooling on a 200 g laboratory fluid-bed roaster and on a 320 kg production roaster with a rotating bowl.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> For safety reasons, all production batches had to be water-cooled, so only the amount of water was varied.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Coffees with more water degassed faster; their pore size distribution did not explain it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The difference showed mainly early on: after one week of storage, degassing rates no longer differed markedly.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Air-cooled coffee re-moistened to 5 g per 100 g degassed like film-quenched coffee, so the water content, not the cooling method, made the difference.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The authors suggest that bean firmness, which rises with moisture, may play a part.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the thesis, firmness rose almost linearly with water content.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> A 2018 degassing study cites this result: the cooling mode, air or water, affects how whole beans degas.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Aroma.** In storage at 25 °C, most aroma compounds were stable to a similar degree in air-cooled and quenched coffees.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Dimethyl trisulfide evolved differently in the wetter coffees, which the authors read as faster oxidation of thiols and a faster loss of freshness.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the 2008 trials, hexanal and sulphides were highly sensitive to water content in both open and closed storage; other aroma compounds changed only in closed storage, where more water harmed stability.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The thesis summarises it: with more water, dimethyl sulfide, 3-mercapto-3-methylbutyl formate and N-methylpyrrole were lost faster in open storage and dimethyl trisulfide built up faster; under protective atmosphere, Strecker aldehydes and α-diketones also degraded faster.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Measuring moisture after cooling

Swiss and Austrian rules set the 5% maximum on the roasted coffee itself.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> According to the SCA handbook, the value of about 1–2% for unquenched coffee stays stable if the coffee is stored in protective packaging.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Timing matters for any reading. Quench water sits at the surface first, so a sample taken straight after cooling is not yet uniform.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry compared moisture meters on freshly quenched coffee.

## Risks and defects

- **Overshoot.** A roast that is not stopped quickly runs past its intended degree; uncontrolled, the beans can catch fire.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Over-quenching.** Excessive quench water is absorbed, raises the final moisture and speeds up staling.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It can also push coffee towards the 5% legal maximum in Switzerland and Austria.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Grinding too early.** Freshly quenched, wetter coffee grinds coarser until the water has equilibrated.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Dust and emissions.** Coolers emit particulate matter, which is usually collected in cyclones.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **How much water is moderate.** The industrial trials show how the amount of quench water sets the water content<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>, but no study consulted for this entry gives a water content at which flavour starts to suffer in a sensory test.
- **Cooling speed and flavour.** No peer-reviewed study comparing air-cooling speeds and their effect on flavour was found.
- **Moisture meters.** How well rapid meters read freshly quenched coffee was not found in the sources consulted.

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Baggenstoss et al., 2007 – Water quench cooling, degassing and aroma.</strong> Compared air cooling with water quench cooling on a 200 g laboratory fluid-bed roaster and a 320 kg production bowl roaster, then followed carbon dioxide release and aroma compounds in storage at 25 °C. Wetter coffees degassed faster, and one sulphur compound pointed to faster staling.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 55(16), 6685–6691 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf070338d">doi:10.1021/jf070338d</a></p>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Baggenstoss et al., 2008 – Water content after quenching.</strong> Quenched industrial roasts to 2.3–8.8 g water per 100 g and ground them at once, after 6 h and after 24 h. Wetter coffees ground coarser and extracted less effectively, and their aroma was less stable in storage.</p>
<p class="meta">European Food Research and Technology 227(5), 1357–1365 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00217-008-0852-8">doi:10.1007/s00217-008-0852-8</a></p>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Baggenstoss, 2008 – Roasting and quenching technology (doctoral thesis).</strong> Brings together the quenching, moisture and grinding studies with laboratory and industrial roasts, and adds high-speed video of water droplets hitting hot beans. Concludes that the effects of quenching follow the water left in the bean, and recommends storing coffee at low moisture for a longer shelf life.</p>
<p class="meta">ETH Zürich, Diss. ETH No. 17696 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3929/ethz-a-005666902">doi:10.3929/ethz-a-005666902</a></p>
