## Three ways to count a year

Coffee is picked in different months in each origin, so no single calendar fits every producer. Coffee statistics therefore use several kinds of year. Each dataset names its year in its own way, and the labels look alike.

- **Coffee year.** The International Coffee Agreement 2007 defines the coffee year as the period from 1 October to 30 September.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The International Coffee Organization (ICO) uses it for the world supply and demand balance.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Coffee year 2022/23, for example, began on 1 October 2022 and ended on 30 September 2023.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Crop year.** The ICO defines a crop year as the 12 months from one harvest to the next.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The crop years it uses start on 1 April, 1 July or 1 October.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Marketing year.** The US Department of Agriculture (USDA) reports each producing country in its local marketing year. It starts in October, April or July, depending on the country.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Countries that do not produce coffee are on an October–September year.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Calendar year.** FAOSTAT, the statistics database of the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), reports crop production by calendar year.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

A year label such as “2025/26” therefore means different months in different tables. The table below sums up the frames.

| Frame | Who uses it | Months | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coffee year | ICO: supply and demand balance, exports, prices | 1 October–30 September | <sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> |
| Crop year | ICO: production of each exporting country | From 1 April, 1 July or 1 October | <sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> |
| Local marketing year | USDA: production and trade of each producing country | April–March, July–June or October–September | <sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> |
| Marketing year, non-producers | USDA: importing countries | October–September | <sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> |
| Calendar year | FAOSTAT | January–December | <sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> |

## Crop years and marketing years by country

A country’s year usually starts near the beginning of its harvest. Brazil picks robusta from April and arabica from May, and the harvest ends by about September.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The ICO counts Brazil’s crop year from 1 April.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The USDA uses a July–June marketing year for Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Peru harvests from April to September.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The USDA uses an April–March marketing year for Peru<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>, and an ICO market report states that Peru’s crop year begins in April.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Colombia’s main harvest runs from October to December, and its USDA marketing year from October to September.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Vietnam and Ethiopia are also on October–September marketing years in USDA reports.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Harvest months for each origin are listed in Harvest calendars.

## One harvest, several year labels

Take the coffee Brazil picked from April to September 2025.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The USDA counts it in marketing year 2025/26, which runs from July 2025 to June 2026.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In the ICO’s system it belongs to Brazil’s crop year 2025/26, from April 2025 to March 2026.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> That crop year overlaps two coffee years: 2024/25, which ends on 30 September 2025, and 2025/26, which starts on 1 October 2025.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The ICO says so itself. For producers whose crop year starts on 1 April, crop-year production falls across two coffee years.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> To build a coffee-year total, the ICO takes a portion of each of two crop years (see the deep dive).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Labels from national bodies can differ again. A 2025 USDA report quotes Brazil’s National Supply Company (Conab): 55.7 million bags of production “in 2025”.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The USDA’s own forecast for marketing year 2025/26 is 65 million bags of 60 kg.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The report says that Conab uses different methods from the USDA’s Brasília office and has historically given lower estimates; it does not discuss the year labels.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Why it matters for comparisons

Two figures with the same label can cover different harvests. A USDA “2025/26” figure for Brazil and an ICO coffee year 2025/26 figure do not cover the same months.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A comparison of USDA, ICO and FAOSTAT world totals is given in The coffee belt.

- **Production.** Compare figures from one dataset, with the year frame named. Production by country is set out in Coffee production by country.
- **Exports.** The ICO reports exports by coffee year in its monthly market reports.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2014 ICO report gave Brazil’s exports counted from April, the start of its crop year.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Prices.** The ICO uses the coffee year for prices as well, so supply, demand and prices refer to the same period.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The weights of its composite indicator price are set per coffee year.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> How prices form is explained in How a coffee price is made.

## How the ICO converts crop years to coffee years

The ICO calls its coffee year a marketing year: the year commencing on 1 October.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> To keep the world balance consistent, it converts production from a crop-year basis to this basis, depending on each country’s harvest months.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The ICO gives Brazil as its example. Brazil’s crop year 2022/23 ran from 1 April 2022 to 31 March 2023 and covered the first half of coffee year 2022/23.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Crop year 2023/24 ran from 1 April 2023 to 31 March 2024, so it overlaps the second half of coffee year 2022/23 (our reading of the dates; the ICO note says “latter half of coffee year 2023/24” here, which does not fit its own dates or its next sentence, which allocates part of the 2023/24 crop year to coffee year 2022/23).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Coffee year 2022/23 production therefore takes a portion of the 2022/23 and a portion of the 2023/24 crop year.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The tool for this is the crop percentage distribution. The ICO’s Statistics Committee describes it as the availability of the crop in each quarter, used to convert supply data from a crop-year basis to a coffee-year or calendar-year basis.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The resulting figures serve a consistent aggregate balance for analysis. The ICO states that they do not represent the production occurring on the ground within individual countries.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

A hypothetical example shows the arithmetic (our illustration, not ICO data). A country with an April–March crop year harvests 50 million bags in crop year 2025/26. If 40% of that crop is available from October 2025 to March 2026, 20 million bags count in coffee year 2025/26 and 30 million in coffee year 2024/25. Coffee year 2025/26 then also receives a share of the next crop year, 2026/27.

## USDA marketing years and FAOSTAT calendar years

The USDA circular sorts producing countries into three marketing years, beginning in October (Colombia), April (Indonesia) or July (Brazil); non-producing countries use October.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Country reports state the year in their production figures, for example “MY 2025/2026 (April–March)” for Peru and “MY 2022/23 (Oct-Sep)” for Ethiopia.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The circular does not describe a conversion of these local years to a common year; we found none in the document.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A USDA world total for “2025/26” is therefore read here as a sum of local marketing years (our reading).

FAOSTAT’s crop production dataset is described with the temporal keyword “calendar year”.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to World Bank notes on FAO cereal data, the FAO allocates production to the calendar year in which the bulk of the harvest took place.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No FAO document consulted for this entry states this rule for coffee. For a harvest that spans the turn of the year, such as Vietnam’s from late October to January<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>, the months alone do not show which calendar year receives the crop.

## Comparing figures across datasets

The differences in year frame suggest a few checks before two figures are compared (our guidance, drawn from the definitions above).

1. Name the dataset and its year frame: ICO coffee year, ICO crop year, USDA marketing year or FAOSTAT calendar year.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
2. Find the country’s start month in that dataset; for Brazil it is April in ICO crop years and July in USDA marketing years.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
3. Check whether the figure is converted. ICO coffee-year production is an analytical conversion, not a count of one harvest.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
4. Compare exports and prices in the same frame. The ICO reports both by coffee year.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
5. Keep methods apart from timing. Differences between Conab and USDA estimates for Brazil are put down to method by the USDA.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **ICO crop year of each country.** The ICO documents consulted for this entry name the three crop-year start dates and give Brazil and Peru as April countries.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> None of them lists the crop year of every exporting country.
- **Crop percentage distribution values.** The ICO defines the concept<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>, but no document consulted here publishes the quarterly shares by country.
- **FAOSTAT and coffee harvests across the new year.** The FAO rule we found is stated for cereals.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
