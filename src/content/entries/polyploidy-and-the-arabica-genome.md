## Four sets of chromosomes

Most coffee species carry two sets of chromosomes, one from each parent. Biologists call such plants diploid. In coffee, one set holds 11 chromosomes, so a diploid plant has 22.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

*Coffea arabica* is different. It carries four sets, 44 chromosomes in all. A plant with more than two sets is called polyploid; with four sets, tetraploid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a 2003 study of 15 African coffee species and six further taxa, all were diploid with 22 chromosomes; arabica was named as the one exception.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Two parents in one genome

Arabica’s four sets come from two different species. Two sets descend from *C. canephora*, the species grown as robusta. The other two descend from *C. eugenioides*, a wild coffee species.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A polyploid built from two species is called an allotetraploid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The second parent is described in [Coffea eugenioides](/entries/coffea-eugenioides/).

A 1999 study first showed this parentage. It compared genetic markers and labelled arabica chromosomes with DNA of the candidate parents.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Genome assemblies of arabica and of both parents confirmed it in 2024.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Each parental half of the genome is called a subgenome. The 2024 study names them subCC, from the canephora side, and subEE, from the eugenioides side.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The plastid genome of arabica, the small genome inside its chloroplasts, comes from *C. eugenioides*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Why arabica pollinates itself

Arabica mainly fertilises itself; pollinators may occasionally carry pollen between plants.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Most diploid coffee species cannot do this. They reject their own pollen through a mechanism called gametophytic self-incompatibility.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

A 2011 study of the genes behind this mechanism links the difference to polyploidy. It states that arabica’s self-compatibility is likely the result of its allotetraploid origin, given the strong link between polyploidy and the breakdown of self-incompatibility in plants.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Two diploid species, *C. heterocalyx* and *C. anthonyi*, are also self-compatible; the cause in those is unknown.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> How coffee flowers are pollinated is covered in [Flowering and pollination](/entries/flowering-and-pollination/).

## How large the genome is

The arabica genome is close to the size of its two parents’ genomes together. A 2024 genome study projects 1,281 Mb (million base pairs) for arabica, 705 Mb for *C. canephora* and 682 Mb for *C. eugenioides*; the two parents add up to 1,387 Mb by our calculation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2014 study gave about 710 Mb for *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A second 2024 study estimated 1.33 Gb for arabica by flow cytometry, which measures the DNA in cell nuclei.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Subgenomes and meiosis

*C. arabica* (2n = 4x = 44) is an allopolyploid whose subgenomes derive from *C. canephora* and *C. eugenioides*.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The 1999 study used restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) markers and genomic in situ hybridisation (GISH); it concluded that arabica is an amphidiploid formed from these two species or closely related ecotypes.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It also found low divergence between each subgenome and its parent species, which pointed to a relatively recent origin.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to a 2017 textbook chapter, this low divergence supports the hypothesis of a very recent speciation.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Arabica generally shows bivalent pairing of homologous chromosomes and disomic inheritance.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Each chromosome pairs with its partner from the same subgenome, not with the matching (homoeologous) chromosome of the other. A 2024 study cites cytogenetic evidence that irregularities in this disomic behaviour are ordinary in arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The genome structure has changed little since the hybridisation. The 2024 Nature Genetics study found largely conserved structures between the diploid parents and the descendant subgenomes, with no obvious global dominance of either subgenome.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Genes were lost in similar proportions from both subgenomes, and the polyploidy event seemingly did not affect the rate of this gene loss (fractionation).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> *C. canephora* itself shows no sign of a whole-genome polyploidisation in its lineage since the ancient gamma triplication.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Exchanges and chromosomal changes

Segments can move between the subgenomes. In a homoeologous exchange, a stretch of one subgenome replaces the corresponding stretch of the other. A 2012 study characterised a 50 kb region of the eugenioides-derived subgenome that had replaced its canephora-derived counterpart.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The 2024 Nature Genetics study detected a bias in such exchanges in favour of subCC.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The 2024 Nature Communications study, based on a chromosome-scale assembly of the variety Bourbon, found exchanges on chromosomes 2, 7 and 10 that occurred very early in the arabica lineage and are fixed in all accessions analysed.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It also found many recent chromosomal aberrations, including aneuploidies, deletions, duplications and exchanges, that remain polymorphic in the germplasm.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Four accessions showed whole-chromosome aneuploidies.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors propose such variants as a source of variation in a species with otherwise low diversity.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The same study found evidence of cryptic robusta introgression, via derivatives of the Timor Hybrid, in arabica-like germplasm.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The Timor Hybrid, a natural cross of arabica and *C. canephora*, is covered in [The Timor Hybrid](/entries/the-timor-hybrid/).

## Dating the hybridisation

The subgenomes have diverged little from their parent species.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Published datings span two orders of magnitude.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

A 2012 study dated its homoeologous exchange to 10,000–50,000 years before present, from nucleotide substitution frequency, and called this the most recent estimate of arabica’s origin.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2017 textbook chapter, citing that study, gives the speciation as 10,000–50,000 years BC.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A 2020 study summarised the literature as a broad interval of 10,000 or 665,000 years before present.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The 2024 Nature Genetics study considers 350,000–610,000 years a likely range for the founding polyploidy event.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It notes that earlier datings ran from 10,000 to 1 million years ago and that more recent timings, such as 20,000 years, could be underestimates.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same study times the bottlenecks that followed with a generation time of 21 years.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2024 Nature Communications study gives no date of its own and speaks of a very recent origin.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Where the hybridisation took place is also open. The 1999 study noted that it is difficult to locate, because the extent of tropical forest in Central Africa changed considerably during the late Quaternary.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Age of the species.** Datings range from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand years, depending on the method and on how bottlenecks are treated (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Place of origin.** Shifts in Central African forest during the late Quaternary make the site of the hybridisation hard to locate.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Cause of self-compatibility.** The 2011 study links it to polyploidy and found three putative self-incompatibility RNase alleles in arabica, one possibly a pseudogene; the mechanism of the breakdown was not shown.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Meiotic stability.** How often irregular pairing produces aneuploid or rearranged plants, and what that means for breeding, is not established in the studies consulted for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
