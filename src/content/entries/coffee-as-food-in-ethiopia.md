## More than a brewed bean

Coffee in Ethiopia is not only a drink made from roasted seeds. Sources describe dried coffee berries cooked with butter, infusions of the leaves and drinks made from the dried husk of the cherry.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The husk is the dried outer part of the cherry around the seeds; a 2022 review counts skin, pulp and parchment as husk.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

“Food” is an outside label. In a 2018 study among the Boorana Oromo, people did not consider buna qalaa, the butter-cooked coffee, a food; they eat it as a symbol.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> This entry follows the sources and names each use as the people who practise it describe it, where a source allows. The brewed coffee and its ceremony are covered in [The Ethiopian coffee ceremony](/entries/the-ethiopian-coffee-ceremony/).

The coffee plants behind these uses grow in Ethiopia’s forests, semi-forests, gardens and plantations.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> How that diversity is grown and conserved is told in [Ethiopian landraces](/entries/ethiopian-landraces/).

## Buna qalaa: coffee cooked in butter

Buna qalaa means “slaughtered coffee” in Afaan Oromoo, the Oromo language: *buna* is coffee and *qaluu* is slaughtering.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Cutting open the coffee bean with the teeth is compared to cutting an animal’s neck.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The preparation starts with dried coffee berries, washed and cracked open with the teeth.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> They are toasted in a pot over the fire, and fresh butter is added while they are still on the fire.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the 2018 study the cracked berries are also cooked in milk, so that milk and butter soak into them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Sources differ in detail: one starts from green or dried berries, another speaks of raw coffee beans fried in clarified butter.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Buna qalaa belongs to occasions. A 2011 article from Wallaga, western Ethiopia, names community meetings, weddings and fundraising events.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Among the Boorana Oromo it accompanies rituals such as weddings and *gubbisa*, the naming of the eldest son.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Coffee as provisions for a journey

A travel account by James Bruce, an 18th-century traveller, describes coffee carried as food.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In his account, Oromo warriors on long journeys carried roasted, ground coffee berries mixed with butter and rolled into balls. One ball was said to last a whole day.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> We know this account only as it is retold in the 2011 article, which takes it from a later book.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It is a traveller’s report, not an observation by the people concerned.

## Leaf infusions

Coffee leaves are made into tea-like drinks. A 2020 review names Ethiopia, South Sudan, Jamaica, India, Java and West Sumatra as places where the infusion is a traditional food.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In Ethiopia it is called kuti, jeno or jenuai.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The same review says arabica leaves have probably been used in Ethiopia, and robusta leaves in India and West Sumatra.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The notification that led to the EU authorisation (below) was reviewed in 2019 by an advisory committee of the United Kingdom’s food regulator, then part of the EU process; its paper lists Ethiopia, South Sudan, Liberia, Indonesia and Jamaica.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> According to the applicant, the drink is made like tea from fresh, dried or roasted leaves, and spices, herbs or milk are sometimes added.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The committee noted that further details of traditional use were not provided.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Husk drinks: hashara and qishr

The dried husk of the cherry is brewed as well. Drinks from coffee cherries are called hashara in Ethiopia and qishr in Yemen.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Elsewhere the dried husk is sold as cascara. A 2022 review notes that its use as a hot-water drink is rather unknown in the Western world.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Husk drinks also stand near the start of written coffee history. According to a coffee history chapter, the first recorded coffee drinkers in Arabia used an infusion of dried coffee husks. The Ethiopians called it *buno*, the Sufis *qishr*.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> That chapter dates the first records to the 15th century.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> How coffee moved on from there is told in Coffeehouses from Mecca to Europe.

## Buna qalaa as ritual

The 2018 study worked with Boorana Oromo informants through interviews, focus groups and observation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It reads buna qalaa as a ritual that “accompanies all ritual performances of the Oromo”, from the Gadaa system to reconciliation and weddings.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Married women slaughter the coffee; girls who have not reached fertility do not take part.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Order and blessing matter. Elders sit at the front and eat first, then juniors follow; the head of the household tastes only symbolically before the elders.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A blessing comes before the ritual tasting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Coffee is not normally slaughtered at night, with exceptions such as weddings and the day a *qaalluu*, a ritual leader, has died.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The preparation has its own vocabulary. When the cracked berries go into the milk, the sound is called *tooruu*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Cracking the husk lets milk and butter enter the bean and stops it jumping out of the pan.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Oil replaces butter when butter runs short, and a salt bar, largely replaced by sugar, sweetens the mixture.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Origin stories.** Oral traditions recorded in the 2018 study say the first coffee plant sprouted from the tears of Waaqa or, in a Boorana myth, on the grave of Kuma.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> These are myths, given here as the study records them. The 2011 article notes a belief that the Oromo were the first to recognise coffee’s stimulating effect, and adds that nothing substantiates it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry dates the beginning of buna qalaa.

## Leaf and husk drinks in EU food law

Under EU novel food law, Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, a food with a history of safe use outside the EU can be notified as a traditional food from a third country.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Two coffee products have passed this route.

**Coffee-leaf infusion (2020).** A company, AM Breweries, notified the infusion on 27 November 2018.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The Commission authorised it in July 2020, citing a history of safe food use in Africa, Asia and North America.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The authorisation covers the infusion as a herbal infusion only; the request to use it as an ingredient in other beverages was not considered valid.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The specification sets at most 20 g of dried leaves per litre of hot water, pasteurisation of at least 71 °C for 15 s, and limits for caffeine, chlorogenic acid and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Dried coffee cherry pulp (2022).** EFSA reported that the pulp has been consumed as infusions, plain or spiced, for more than 25 years in Yemen, Ethiopia and Bolivia.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> It raised no safety objections.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Regulation (EU) 2022/47 of 13 January 2022 then authorised dried coffee cherry pulp and its infusion.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> It covers the dried, unroasted pulp of arabica and/or robusta cherries, separated from the beans and also known as cascara; a typical infusion uses up to 6 g of pulp per 100 mL of hot water above 75 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Above 150 mg/L of caffeine, the label must carry a high-caffeine warning.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Caffeine in leaf and husk drinks

No study consulted for this entry analysed a leaf or husk drink prepared in an Ethiopian household. The numbers below come from EU assessments, two reviews and one coffee study, and they differ in kind: assessment values, legal limits, a proposed product range and one measured brew.

For the 2020 notification, EFSA used 7 mg/g of caffeine in the dried leaves and 9.9–10.9 mg/L in a drink made with 20 g/L of leaves, as reported in a 2020 review.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The UK committee paper states that the caffeine level of the notified product is consistent with a literature range of 8–12 mg/g.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The EU specification caps the leaf infusion at below 80 mg/L caffeine, below 100 mg/L chlorogenic acid (5-CQA) and below 700 mg/L EGCG.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> EGCG is limited because its presence in arabica leaf infusion cannot be excluded.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

No measured value for hashara was found. For a juice made from coffee fruit extract, a 2022 risk assessment proposes standardising the extract so that the drink contains 70–300 mg of caffeine per litre; this is a recommended product range, not a measurement.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> For comparison, one study measured 840–1,060 mg/L of caffeine in hot French-press coffee brewed at 35 g in 350 mL with water at 98 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

How caffeine acts in the body is covered in [Caffeine](/entries/caffeine/).

## Open questions

- **Composition of household drinks.** No study consulted for this entry measured caffeine or phenolic compounds in kuti or hashara as prepared in Ethiopian homes; the numbers above come from EU notifications and reviews.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Where and how leaf drinks are used.** The UK committee noted that the history-of-use dossier lacked detail, and the 2020 review gives names but no regions within Ethiopia.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Age of the practices.** The sources record buna qalaa and coffee-butter balls, but none consulted for this entry dates their beginnings; origin stories are myths.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
