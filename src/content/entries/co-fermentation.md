## Two meanings of co-fermentation

After harvest, microbes on the fruit and in the processing environment ferment the coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s24">24</a></sup> Producers who want more control add something to this fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> This entry uses the word “co-fermentation” for two different additions.

1. **Starter cultures.** A selected strain of yeast or lactic acid bacteria is added to the cherries or the fermentation tank. Researchers call this inoculation.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> When yeasts and bacteria are added together, studies speak of co-inoculation.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
2. **Added ingredients.** Fruit, spices, juices or other materials are fermented together with the coffee. This entry calls such lots “infused”. In one Colombian trial, crushed pineapple went into the tank together with a yeast starter.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

The two practices can be combined, as in that trial. That makes it hard to say which addition caused a change in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> How spontaneous fermentation works is covered under fermentation; the microbes themselves under microorganisms.

## What a starter culture is meant to do

A starter culture is a large dose of one or more chosen strains. In one laboratory trial, the added yeasts grew to about 100 times the yeast population of a spontaneous fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The aim is that this strain leads the fermentation instead of whatever microbes happen to be present.

Three goals recur in the studies:

- **Faster, more complete mucilage breakdown.** Many yeasts and bacteria make enzymes that break down pectin, the gel that holds the mucilage together.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Aroma.** Starter yeasts raised compounds such as ethyl acetate and isoamyl acetate, which smell fruity.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In one trial, only the inoculated coffee showed a caramel flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Protection.** Some yeasts slow the growth of moulds that produce ochratoxin A, a toxin.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Starters have been tested in natural, semi-dry (pulped natural) and washed coffee, and in closed-tank fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup>

## How a strain is chosen

Many candidate strains come from coffee itself. Researchers isolate yeasts from coffee fermentations and test them one by one.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2021 review lists the main criteria: pectin-degrading enzymes, production of aroma compounds, the chemical changes the strain causes, and no unwanted metabolites.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Laboratory screens come first. In a 2019 study, eight of 28 yeast isolates showed pectin-degrading activity on test plates.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a 2017 study, 32 wild yeasts were grown next to toxin-producing moulds to see which held them back.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A strain that passes is then tried on coffee. There it must survive and stay present until drying; trials check this with DNA-based methods.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Fermenting with fruit, spices and other ingredients

In the second meaning, producers add other materials to the fermentation. For this practice, the peer-reviewed record is thin. No study consulted for this entry compares the same coffee fermented with and without added fruit alone.

What is known comes from two directions. First, laboratory work shows that aroma compounds dissolved in the fermentation liquid can move into depulped seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The parchment slows this down, and some compounds are broken down on the way.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Second, the one trial with added fruit also used a yeast starter and other ingredients, so their effects cannot be separated.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## What the rules allow

The World Brewers Cup draws its line at the green coffee stage. Its 2026 rules allow no additives of any kind once coffee has become green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> Additions during fermentation, before that stage, are therefore not excluded by this wording.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> One Cup of Excellence programme set a stricter line: its 2023 Experimentals category in Brazil admitted no additives other than water.<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup>

## Inoculation in practice

**On cherries for natural and semi-dry coffee.** A Brazilian research group tested two methods. In the direct method, a yeast suspension was sprayed onto cherries already spread on raised drying terraces. In the bucket method, the suspension was mixed with the cherries in a bucket and left for 16 hours before drying.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Cells from a 500 mL suspension were applied to 10 kg of coffee, giving 10⁷ cells/g.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The bucket method favoured the persistence of the starters, especially *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, in semi-dry coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**In wet and closed-tank fermentation.** In an on-farm wet trial, *Pichia fermentans* YC5.2 was inoculated with or without 2% (w/v) sucrose<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>; a 2024 review gives the inoculum as 10⁷ cells/mL.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In self-induced anaerobic fermentation (SIAF) in 50 L closed bioreactors, three yeasts were added at 10⁷–10⁸ cells/mL.<sup><a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup>

**Checking that the starter took hold.** Persistence has been followed by DGGE fingerprinting and by quantitative PCR (qPCR).<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In the semi-dry trial, the inoculated yeasts were present throughout fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Whether a starter displaces the resident bacteria differs between studies; this is covered under microorganisms.

## Selecting strains: the evidence

**Pectin-degrading enzymes.** Pectinolytic activity has been found in coffee isolates of the bacteria *Klebsiella*, *Erwinia*, *Bacillus* and *Lactobacillus*, and of the yeasts *Pichia*, *Saccharomyces* and *Candida*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a 2019 screen, 28 yeasts were isolated and eight showed pectin-degrading activity; their pectin degradation index ranged from 110 to 178%, highest in *Saccharomycopsis fibuligera* KNU18Y4.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The same study measured polygalacturonase, pectin lyase and pectin methylesterase; *Wickerhamomyces anomalus* KNU18Y3 reached 16.96 U/mL pectin lyase.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Aroma and absence of off-products.** A 2021 review names volatile production and the lack of undesirable metabolites as selection criteria, beside pectin lyase secretion.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the semi-dry trial, butyric and propionic acids were absent from all treatments.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> These acids can give an onion flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Adaptation.** The same review describes *S. cerevisiae* strains as showing good adaptability to the fermentation process.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry tested candidate strains for tolerance of temperature, pH or ethanol before field use; stress tolerance is therefore not documented here as a measured criterion.

**Antagonism against ochratoxin A moulds.** In a 2017 laboratory study, 32 wild yeasts from coffee and cocoa fermentations were co-cultured with *Aspergillus carbonarius* and *A. ochraceus* at 28 °C for seven days.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> *Pichia anomala* CCMA0148 and *S. cerevisiae* CCMA0159 inhibited growth of all fungal strains most.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The abstract reports that *Rhodotorula mucilaginosa* inhibited ochratoxin A production by all three isolates, but the results also list *R. mucilaginosa* isolates that stimulated it in some tests.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> These were culture-media tests, and the authors call for tests on coffee fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2023 Indonesian study went further: three *Candida krusei* isolates inhibited *A. ochraceus*, grew on wet- and semi-wet-processed beans, and gave higher total cupping scores than uninoculated beans and beans treated with a commercial lactic acid bacteria inoculant.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## What trials found: chemistry and the cup

**Acids and volatiles.** In natural coffee, all directly inoculated treatments had high citric and malic acid, and 217 volatile compounds were identified.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> After roasting, caramel-related volatiles appeared with *S. cerevisiae* and fruity (apple, cherry) compounds with *C. parapsilosis*.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In wet fermentation, *P. fermentans* raised ethanol, acetaldehyde, ethyl acetate and isoamyl acetate and lowered lactic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In semi-dry coffee, acids, pyrazines and pyridines were the main volatiles in green and roasted beans.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Strain, method and variety interact.** *S. cerevisiae* CCMA 0543 scored highest when inoculated in buckets; *C. parapsilosis* did better sprayed directly; *T. delbrueckii* did well with both methods.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In a 2020 trial with four starters, two varieties and two processing methods, the perceived fruity, nutty and cocoa attributes varied with variety, processing and inoculum.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A strain that works for one coffee and method cannot be assumed to work for another.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Co-inoculation.** Combining *P. fermentans* with the lactic acid bacterium *Pediococcus acidilactici* on ripe cherries raised sugar consumption and the production of lactic acid, ethanol and ethyl acetate; the authors suggest a synergy between the two.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> With immature cherries, only *P. fermentans* alone improved metabolite formation, and the combined treatment had no significant effect.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Cup scores.** Results are mixed (see Contested). In closed-tank trials, *T. delbrueckii* gave 86.50 points for pulped coffee and *C. parapsilosis* 85.90 for natural coffee, against 84.25 and 83.75 for conventional coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup> These figures compare inoculated closed-tank coffee with conventional coffee, so the vessel and the yeast changed together.<sup><a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup> In a laboratory-scale wet trial, inoculated coffees scored higher for flavour, aroma and acidity, but differences in sweetness, balance, clean cup, uniformity, aftertaste and overall score were reported as not statistically significant.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The same paper marks the highest overall score (82.0, mixed yeasts) with p < 0.05, so its reporting of the overall score is not consistent.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A 2024 review notes a yeast trial in which every sample, including the uninoculated control, scored above 85 points.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**The Colombian starter-plus-fruit trial.** In a 2026 study of Castillo coffee, the inoculated wet fermentation outperformed spontaneous fermentation in all sensory attributes by quantitative descriptive analysis, with over a hundred differential metabolites.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Inoculated coffee had more sucrose, mannitol, methyl phenylacetate, malic, citric and quinic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In the preprint, the starter was a commercial yeast cocktail with flour and a sugar source, added with crushed pineapple; the strains were confidential.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## How compounds reach the seed

Microbial metabolites made in the mucilage – lactic acid, acetic acid and mannitol – have been found in the endosperm of spontaneously fermented coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s24">24</a></sup> A French research group measured the transfer directly, using labelled compounds in simulated wet processing.

**Volatiles.** Labelled butanal, 2-phenylethanol and isoamyl acetate were added to the liquid at 25 °C and sampled from 0 to 48 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> After 12 hours in the medium with depulped beans and yeast, the beans held 0.2 ± 0.03 µg/g butanal, 11.2 ± 0.66 µg/g 2-phenylethanol and 1.3 ± 0.04 µg/g isoamyl acetate.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The parchment slowed 2-phenylethanol significantly; butanal and isoamyl acetate were partly metabolised; and the yeast reduced butanal transfer.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

**Non-volatile precursors.** Lactic acid, alanine and glutamic acid also moved into the beans; parchment reduced the transfer of these precursors by up to 60%.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> In real fermentations with two *S. cerevisiae* strains, lactic acid and alanine were produced and entered the beans after a 12-hour lag.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Fructose underwent germination-related metabolic reactions inside the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

**Models.** A 2023 model described 2-phenylethanol transfer well with a resistance that changes over time (R² = 0.98); butanal and isoamyl acetate fitted better with two parallel first-order reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

These studies used single compounds in solution, not fruit. They show that dissolved aroma compounds can enter the seed. They do not show how much of a fruit's aroma survives roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a>,<a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

## Fermenting green coffee

A third practice ferments the green coffee itself, after drying and hulling. In solid-state fermentation of green canephora with *Aspergillus* and *Mucor* species, free sugars, free amino acids and caffeoylquinic acids changed before roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> After roasting, *A. oryzae* raised pyrazines and *Mucor plumbeus* raised furans.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> This treatment falls after the green coffee stage, where the 2026 World Brewers Cup rules allow no additives.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup>

## Competition rules and disclosure

The 2026 World Brewers Cup rules define green coffee as seeds of the genus *Coffea*, dried in post-harvest processing and free of all pericarp layers.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> After that stage, no additives of any kind may be added, including exposure to aromatic substances, flavourings, perfumes, liquids or powders.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> The brewed beverage may contain no additives beyond whole-bean coffee, brew water, brewing devices and service vessels.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup>

The Cup of Excellence set a narrower boundary in Brazil in 2023: its Experimentals category included “both wet and dry processes with unique treatments”, with no additives other than water.<sup><a href="#src-s22">22</a></sup>

No standard consulted for this entry requires a producer or seller to declare added fruit, spices or starter cultures on a green or roasted coffee lot.

## Risks and limits

- **Confounded comparisons.** Several trials change more than one thing at once: the vessel and the yeast,<sup><a href="#src-s23">23</a></sup> or the yeast, flour, sugar and fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The effect of the starter alone is then unknown.
- **Non-significant score gains.** In some trials, higher mean scores were not statistically significant.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Fruit quality matters.** Mixed cultures that helped ripe cherries did not help immature ones.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Laboratory versus farm.** Antagonism against ochratoxin A moulds was shown mainly on culture media; evidence on coffee seeds is limited to one study consulted for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Unknown strains.** Commercial cocktails may not name their strains, which prevents replication.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Fruit volatiles in the cup.** No study consulted for this entry traced compounds from added fruit or spices into roasted coffee.
- **Detection.** No validated method for telling an infused lot from an uninfused one was found among the sources of this entry.
- **Food labelling.** No food-law source consulted for this entry addresses co-fermented coffee; its labelling status is not established here.
- **Stress tolerance.** Tolerance of heat, acidity and ethanol is not reported as a screening step in the studies consulted.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Which microbes do the work.** The authors of the 2026 Colombian study call for separating the contributions of each microbe and pathway.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
