## Mostly water

A cup of black coffee is water with a small amount of coffee dissolved in it. Brewers measure that amount as total dissolved solids (TDS), the share of the drink by weight that is coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The Golden Cup range for filter coffee is 1.15–1.35% TDS.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The rest, 98.65–98.85%, is water (our calculation).

Espresso is stronger. In one study, three espresso recipes held 3.32%, 5.20% and 8.44% TDS, against 1.35–1.55% for filter and immersion brews of the same coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Even the strongest espresso was still about 92% water (our calculation).

Strength and serving size work together. A 150 g cup of filter coffee in the Golden Cup range holds about 1.7–2.0 g of dissolved coffee (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a laboratory study, a 40 g espresso brewed from 20 g of coffee held 3.88 g.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In a comparison of nine brew methods, an espresso and a lungo held about the same total solids per cup.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How strength and extraction are measured is explained in *Extraction yield and TDS*.

## What the dissolved part is made of

The dissolved part is a mixture of many compound classes. A 2014 review names volatile aroma compounds, alkaloids such as caffeine and trigonelline, chlorogenic acids, diterpenes and melanoidins among the key compounds of the beverage.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Melanoidins are brown, high-molecular-weight products of roasting. The same review puts them at around 29% of the dry matter of coffee brew.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Coffee brews also contain carbohydrates that the body does not digest. They are mainly galactomannans and arabinogalactans from the bean’s cell walls.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Acids, sugars and the other soluble compounds are covered in their own entries: *Acids in coffee*, *Carbohydrates and body*, *Melanoidins* and *Chlorogenic acids*.

How much of each reaches the cup depends on the coffee, the roast and the brew. The same 2014 review notes that “a cup of coffee” is not a well-defined measurement.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> All per-cup values below are ranges from different studies, not fixed contents.

## Minerals and vitamins

A 2014 review reports that coffee brew contains several micronutrients, including magnesium, potassium and vitamins B3 and E.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It gives 7–24 mg of magnesium and 34–116 mg of potassium per cup.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Vitamin B3 forms during roasting, when part of the trigonelline breaks down to nicotinic acid, also called niacin.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The review cites reports of 1–3 mg of nicotinic acid per cup.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

A 2021 review of studies published between 2000 and 2020 looked at five minerals in coffee brews. It found that coffee is not a significant source of sodium.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Calcium, phosphorus and sodium per portion each stayed at or below 2.2% of the daily requirement.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Compared with the EU reference intakes used on food labels, one cup holds roughly 2–6% of the magnesium and potassium and 6–19% of the niacin reference values (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The Deep dive shows how these figures were worked out.

Everything in a filter cup that is not water weighs about 2 g (our calculation from the strength above).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No food composition database could be accessed for this entry, so energy values for black coffee and milk drinks are not given here.

## Compounds with effects in the body

Several of the dissolved compounds are studied for their effects in the body. Their amounts per cup vary widely:

- **Caffeine.** Espressos bought in 20 cafés held 51–322 mg each.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) considers habitual intake up to 400 mg a day of no safety concern for non-pregnant adults.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Details are in *Caffeine*.
- **Chlorogenic acids.** A single espresso supplies 24–423 mg, according to the 2014 review.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Roasting breaks them down; see *Chlorogenic acids*.
- **Trigonelline.** Amounts in brews appear to range between 40 and 110 mg per serving.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Diterpenes (cafestol and kahweol).** Boiled coffee, French press and Turkish coffee contain 6–12 mg per cup; filter and instant coffee less than 0.6 mg.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> See *Coffee oil*.
- **Melanoidins.** Around 29% of the dry matter of the brew.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2011 estimate put melanoidin intake from coffee in the order of 1 g a day, reaching about 2 g for heavy drinkers.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

What these compounds do in the body is covered in *Bioactive compounds*. What large observational studies have found about coffee drinkers is covered in *Coffee and long-term health*. Unwanted compounds such as acrylamide are covered in *Contaminants and food safety*.

## Coffee and fluid balance

Coffee is mostly water, and caffeine increases urine output a little. A 2015 meta-analysis pooled 16 studies of caffeine in healthy adults, with a median dose of 300 mg.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> It found a minor diuretic effect, which exercise cancelled out.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Women showed a larger effect than men, and the effect was larger at rest.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The studies tested caffeine, not coffee in particular.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

Health authorities count beverages in total water intake. EFSA’s reference values for water include water from drinking water, beverages of all kinds and food moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The 2024 Swiss dietary recommendations state that caffeinated drinks such as coffee and black tea can contribute to fluid intake. They recommend moderate consumption and name water first, followed by herbal and fruit teas, among the unsweetened drinks to prefer.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Per cup, per serving, per 100 mL

Published values come in three bases, and they are not interchangeable. Concentrations per 100 mL describe the brew; amounts per cup also depend on how big the cup is. The 2014 review notes that “a cup of coffee” is not a well-defined measurement.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The 2021 mineral review assumed 150 mL for brewed coffee and 30 mL for espresso to estimate one portion.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Strong brews in small cups and weak brews in large cups can therefore give similar amounts. In the nine-method comparison, an espresso and a lungo held about the same total solids per cup.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 40 g espresso held 3.88 g of dissolved solids<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>, about twice the 1.7–2.0 g of a 150 g filter cup in the Golden Cup range (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The 2021 review also examined the factors that influence the mineral content of brews, including the brew method.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> How brewing water is specified is covered in *Water for coffee*.

## Reference intakes, our calculation

The EU sets reference intakes for adults in Annex XIII of Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. They are used for nutrition labelling: 375 mg for magnesium, 2,000 mg for potassium and 16 mg for niacin.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Dividing the per-cup values of the 2014 review by these figures gives:

| Nutrient | Per cup (review) | EU reference intake | Share per cup (our calculation) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnesium | 7–24 mg<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> | 375 mg<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> | 1.9–6.4% |
| Potassium | 34–116 mg<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> | 2,000 mg<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> | 1.7–5.8% |
| Niacin (nicotinic acid) | 1–3 mg<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> | 16 mg<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> | 6.3–18.8% |

The review itself puts the niacin figure at 6–18% of a recommended allowance of 16 mg a day for adult men.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The 2021 mineral review used its own reference values, including 3,500 mg a day for potassium. With those, it calculated that one portion covers 7.5% (women) or 6.4% (men) of the magnesium requirement and 6.6% of the potassium requirement.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

For labelling, EU law treats 7.5% of the reference value per 100 mL as a “significant amount” in a beverage.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The potassium range of 37.21–154.07 mg per 100 mL in the 2021 review corresponds to 1.9–7.7% of 2,000 mg per 100 mL (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> This is a labelling yardstick, not a judgement about health.

## Caffeine and urine output

The 2015 meta-analysis included 16 studies in healthy adults, giving 28 effect sizes, analysed with a random-effects model.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Across all of them, caffeine increased urine volume by 109 ± 195 mL compared with conditions without caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The effect was larger at rest than during exercise, and larger in women than in men.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The pooled studies tested caffeine at a median dose of 300 mg, not coffee in particular.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

EFSA’s adequate intakes for water are 2.0 L a day for women and 2.5 L a day for men, from all sources and under moderate temperature and activity.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry gives a figure for how much of a cup of coffee counts towards that total.

## Open questions

- **What “a cup” is.** Reviews report per-cup values without a common cup volume, so they cannot be compared directly.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Origin of the mineral range.** The 2014 review gives 7–24 mg of magnesium and 34–116 mg of potassium per cup without naming the underlying studies or cup sizes in that passage.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Melanoidin quantity.** Isolation methods differ, and published amounts per 100 mL range widely.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Food composition data.** No national food composition database could be accessed for this entry. Energy and macronutrient values for black coffee and milk drinks therefore remain to be added.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Ludwig et al., 2014 – Coffee: biochemistry and potential impact on health.</strong> A review of the compounds in green and roasted coffee and in the brew, and of their absorption and metabolism. It collects per-cup ranges for caffeine, chlorogenic acids, trigonelline, diterpenes, minerals and nicotinic acid, and notes that “a cup” is not a defined measure.</p>
<p class="meta">Food & Function 5(8), 1695–1717 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1039/C4FO00042K">doi:10.1039/C4FO00042K</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Olechno et al., 2021 – Coffee brews as a source of macroelements.</strong> A review of studies from 2000 to 2020 on sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium and phosphorus in coffee brews made by different methods. Assuming 150 mL for brewed coffee and 30 mL for espresso, the authors estimate that one portion covers 6.4–7.5% of the daily magnesium requirement and 6.6% of the potassium requirement.</p>
<p class="meta">Foods 10(6), 1328 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/foods10061328">doi:10.3390/foods10061328</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Zhang et al., 2015 – Caffeine and diuresis.</strong> A meta-analysis of 16 studies in healthy adults with a median caffeine dose of 300 mg. Caffeine had a minor diuretic effect that exercise cancelled out; women and people at rest showed larger effects.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport 18(5), 569–574 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2014.07.017">doi:10.1016/j.jsams.2014.07.017</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Gloess et al., 2013 – Nine extraction methods compared.</strong> Brewed one coffee by nine common methods and compared the brews by instrumental and sensory analysis. Espressos held more than 4% total solids and filter coffee about 1%, yet an espresso and a lungo held similar total solids per cup.</p>
<p class="meta">European Food Research and Technology 236, 607–627 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00217-013-1917-x">doi:10.1007/s00217-013-1917-x</a></p>
</div>
