## What coffee leaf rust is

Coffee leaf rust is a disease of the coffee leaf caused by the fungus *Hemileia vastatrix*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its signs are orange spore masses on the underside of the leaf.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The American Phytopathological Society calls it the most economically important coffee disease in the world.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2017 review puts its losses at one to two billion US dollars a year.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The fungus is known only from coffee plants (*Coffea* species). It reproduces almost entirely through one kind of spore, the urediniospore, which infects new leaves again and again.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No alternate host for its rare sexual spores has been identified.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For *C. arabica*, the review names rust as one of the main limiting factors.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Several resistance genes that breeders have moved into arabica come from robusta (*C. canephora*).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## How the fungus infects a leaf

A spore needs liquid water on the leaf to germinate. High humidity alone is not enough; rain or heavy dew is.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> On a wet leaf, temperature decides how fast it happens.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Infection runs between a minimum of about 15 °C and a maximum of about 28 °C, with an optimum of 22 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The germinating spore grows towards a stoma, one of the breathing pores on the underside of the leaf. It enters only there.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The whole infection needs about 24–48 hours of continuous wetness.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Inside, the fungus grows into the leaf. After 10–14 days, new spore pustules break out through the stomata.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## What it does to the tree and the harvest

Badly infected leaves fall early. The tree loses part of its capacity to photosynthesise and becomes weaker.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Severe attacks can kill twigs and even whole trees.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

The damage reaches into the following year. Next season’s cherries grow on this season’s new shoots, so rust this year reduces next year’s crop.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A review of rust in Brazil describes the same effect: lower production in the year after an epidemic.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In Brazil, the review reports losses of 30–50%.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Rust is usually milder at altitudes above 1,200 m in Brazil, where conditions favour it less.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2017 review notes that it has been reported increasingly at higher altitudes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Trees carrying a heavy crop are more susceptible.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## From Ceylon to Hawaii

The disease was first recorded in 1861 near Lake Victoria in East Africa, on wild coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Planters in Ceylon (Sri Lanka) reported a “coffee leaf disease” in 1867. The fungus was described as *Hemileia vastatrix* in 1869.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Soon after, it wiped out coffee growing in Ceylon, and British planters turned to tea.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

The rust then crossed the coffee lands of the Indian Ocean and the Pacific, from 1870 to 1920.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Estates in Asia were replanted with robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In the late nineteenth century, liberica coffee stood second only to arabica in the coffee trade.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The rust reached the Atlantic coast of Africa in the 1950s and 1960s.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The Americas stayed free until 1970.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In January 1970 rust was found on arabica in southern Bahia, Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It spread through South and Central America in the 1970s and 1980s.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A US Department of Agriculture datasheet of 2015 named Hawaii as the exception among coffee-growing regions.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Hawaii found it in October 2020, on Hawaii Island and Maui; by July 2021 it was on all the main islands.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Since 2008, a series of outbreaks across the Americas has become known as the “Big Rust”.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In Colombia and Central America, the epidemic of 2008–2013 cut production and hit the livelihoods of thousands of smallholders and harvesters.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## How producers control it

Producers have managed rust in four main ways: finding resistant plants, breeding resistant varieties, chemical control and agroecological control.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Resistant varieties are considered the most effective and durable option.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Resistant varieties.** Most rust-resistant arabica varieties descend from the Timor Hybrid, a natural cross of *C. arabica* and *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Their resistance is being lost in several of them, as new forms of the fungus appear.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The varieties themselves are covered in the entry on arabica varieties.

**Fungicides.** Copper fungicides protect the leaf but must be on it before infection.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Systemic fungicides, such as triazoles and strobilurins, can also stop infections that have already started.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Sprays are aimed at the underside of the leaves, where infection happens.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Farm management.** Trees with a heavy crop are more susceptible.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a controlled study, low nitrogen fertilisation raised sporulation, most of all under high light.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Low profitability led to poorer farm management before the 2008–2013 epidemic.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Shade has two sides: one study proposes it as part of rust control, while a review found that medium shade favours the rust.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## The life cycle in detail

**Spores and germination.** Urediniospores are dikaryotic, with two nuclei in each cell, and form the repeating asexual cycle.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Germination requires free water; humidity alone does not trigger it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Germination is optimal at about 24 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Below 15 °C spores do not germinate.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A US Department of Agriculture datasheet gives 15–30 °C for germination and the other infection steps.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> For infection as a whole, one review gives 15 °C minimum, 22 °C optimum and 28 °C maximum.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Penetration.** The germ tube forms an appressorium over a stoma on the lower leaf surface. A penetration hypha then grows into the substomatal chamber.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Infection needs about 24–48 hours of continuous free moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Latent period.** The time from infection to new spores is the latent period. Reported values are 10–14 days<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> and about three weeks.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Temperature controls its length; one review summarises models that predict it from daily maximum and minimum temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Sporulation.** One lesion produces four to six crops of spores, about 300,000 urediniospores, over 3–5 months.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Rain splash spreads spores within a plantation; long-range spread is mainly by wind.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Sexual stage.** Teliospores occur rarely and germinate in place to form four basidiospores. Basidiospores cannot infect coffee, and no other host plant has been identified.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The fungus has long been treated as asexual. Population studies have nevertheless found signs of random mating.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Weather, altitude and epidemics

On a wet leaf, temperature is the most important environmental factor for spore germination.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Rust intensity is lower at higher altitudes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In Brazil the disease is usually less severe above 1,200 m.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Long-term Cenicafé observations in Colombia found more rust at high altitude in warmer El Niño years than in La Niña years.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

A 2015 review of the Colombian and Central American crises names two main drivers: economics and weather.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Low profitability led to reduced management, and weather conditions consistent with climate change enhanced the epidemics.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2017 review describes the same convergence of agronomic, climatic and economic factors.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Races, SH genes and the gene-for-gene model

Nine resistance factors have been identified in coffee, named SH1 to SH9.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> SH1, SH2, SH4 and SH5 come from *C. arabica*. SH3 was introgressed into arabica from *C. liberica*. SH6 to SH9 come from robusta and occur in the Timor Hybrid and other interspecific hybrids.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The fungus carries matching virulence genes, v1 to v9.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Coffee and rust interact according to Flor’s gene-for-gene model, in which each SH gene has a matching virulence gene (v1 for SH1, and so on).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In this model, a resistance gene in the plant works only against rust that lacks the matching virulence gene. A plant with SH3 resists every race without v3. A race that carries v3 overcomes SH3.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A plant with several SH genes can be infected only by a race that carries all the matching v genes. In 1989 a review noted that virulence to SH3 is more complex than this simple matching predicts.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

A physiological race is a group of isolates with a distinct combination of virulence genes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Races are identified by inoculating a set of coffee “differentials”, plants with known resistance genes. In 1989 there were more than 30 known races.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> By 2017, more than 50 races and 23 coffee differentials had been identified.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Timor Hybrid derivatives and lost resistance

The Timor Hybrid populations derive from a plant found on Timor in 1927. In the 1950s they were shown to be natural hybrids of *C. arabica* and *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Breeders chose the Timor Hybrid as their main source of resistance genes, because it crosses readily with arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Crosses made at the coffee rust research centre CIFC in Portugal gave the Catimor and Sarchimor populations. Similar populations were made in Colombia and Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Together they are the source of most rust-resistant varieties grown as of 2017.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Resistance in several Timor Hybrid derivatives is being lost, as new races appear that can overcome it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Brazil, few varieties are completely resistant; most show various levels of partial resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> F1 hybrids showed lower sporulation than an inbred line in a controlled study, possibly through hybrid vigour.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Host factors: fruit load, light and nitrogen

**Fruit load.** Susceptibility increases with berry yield. When immature berries were removed, disease severity fell to almost half.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Overbearing can make rust worse.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Light and shade.** Leaves exposed to high light intensity generally become more susceptible.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Bright sunlight may also kill the fungus in the leaves.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The 1989 review concluded that medium levels of shade favour rust on susceptible varieties most.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a 2017 controlled-environment study, high light combined with low nitrogen gave heavy sporulation, and a warmer temperature regime (27–22 °C) gave about 2,000 times more sporulation than a cooler one (23–18 °C), as the abstract puts it; the results section reports about 10,000 times more spores per infected leaf area in the inbred line Caturra.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The authors proposed vigorous varieties, shade and suitable nitrogen fertilisation as part of an agroecological approach.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Plant vigour.** The 2017 study found that measurements of photosynthesis (chlorophyll fluorescence) helped predict how much the rust would sporulate.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Chemical control

**Copper.** Copper fungicides are preventive.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> They must be on the leaves before infection occurs.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Copper builds up in soil over years of use.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Systemic fungicides.** Curative treatments use systemic fungicides.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Brazil, rust is managed with protectant and systemic fungicides, including copper, triazoles and strobilurins, applied by decision rules that vary with the risk.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The combined or alternate use of copper and systemic products is advised.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> One practice is copper early in the season, then one or occasionally two systemic sprays later in the season.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Application.** Sprays are directed upward to reach the lower leaf surface, and intervals between sprays are kept under 21 days.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The guidance consulted here describes foliar sprays.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In a Brazilian field trial from 2010 to 2012, a triazole applied to the soil, followed by foliar sprays of a triazole and a strobilurin, also controlled rust and raised yields.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

In the greenhouse part of that study, single fungicides reduced rust severity by between 8.9% and 98.5% against untreated plants.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Forecasting.** Infection depends on leaf wetness and temperature, and models predict the latent period from daily maximum and minimum temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The 2015 review of the Central American crisis proposes early warning systems as one of its solutions.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Biological control

Spores of *H. vastatrix* are parasitised by another fungus, *Lecanicillium lecanii*.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It cannot control the rust effectively, but it can reduce spore viability and disease severity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same fungus mainly attacks the green coffee scale, *Coccus viridis*, an insect.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No biological product consulted for this entry has been shown to control rust on its own in the field.

## The 2008–2013 epidemic in numbers

The 2015 review gives these production losses:<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

- **Colombia:** production fell by 31% on average in the epidemic years, compared with 2007.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Central America:** production fell by 16% in 2013 compared with 2011–12, and by 10% in 2013–14 compared with 2012–13.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Employment** in Central American coffee fell by 16% in 2012–13 and by 7% in 2013–14 because of the epidemic.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

A 2017 review states that the disease can cause yield losses of up to 35%.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The figures measure different things: national production against a reference year, and yield losses.

## Cup and supply

According to a 2017 textbook chapter, there is no evidence that diseases or pests change the synthesis of specific compounds in the bean directly.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Their effect on quality runs through the plant. Strong defoliation from rust weakens trees, and weak plants give lower yields and can give lower quality.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

The indirect effects are larger. According to the same book, resistant varieties from Timor Hybrid crosses raised productivity, but as of 2017 their cup quality was debated in some cases, for example Costa Rica 95.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Breeding showed that introgressed varieties with good quality are possible when quality is a main selection criterion.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> For buyers, an epidemic means lower volumes, in the epidemic year and in the year after.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Light during germination.** A need for darkness during germination is often stated, but none of the sources consulted for this entry gives one, so it is left out.
- **Why resistance breaks down.** How new races arise in a fungus thought to be mainly asexual is not settled; signs of recombination have been found.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Shade.** Whether shade raises or lowers rust depends on the balance between less plant stress and more leaf wetness; studies consulted here point both ways (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Mycoparasites in practice.** *Lecanicillium* lowers spore viability, but its value as a control tool on farms is not established.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Current spread.** No global map of rust presence updated in 2026 was consulted for this entry (as of October 2026); the last dated addition found is Hawaii (2020).<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
