## Two species behind one drink

Almost all coffee comes from two species. *Coffea arabica* gives arabica coffee. *Coffea canephora* gives the coffee traded as robusta. This entry sets the two side by side; each species has its own entry with the detail.

The deepest difference is in the chromosomes. Arabica has 44, in four sets, because it arose as a hybrid of two species.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Canephora has 22, in two sets, and it is one of arabica’s two parents.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How that hybrid arose is told in Polyploidy and the arabica genome.

The two also reproduce differently. Arabica flowers mainly pollinate themselves; insects only occasionally bring pollen from other plants.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Canephora is outcrossing.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to a coffee science textbook chapter, it is strictly self-incompatible, needing pollen from another plant, so breeders select clones or cross chosen parents.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## How the plants differ

Botanists describe the two plants in the Flora of Tropical East Africa, quoted by Kew’s Plants of the World Online. Arabica is a bush or tree 2–7 m tall. Canephora is a tree or bush 3.5–8.5 m tall.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Other descriptions give other ranges: Kew’s own plant page gives 2–8 m for arabica,<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> and the Singapore flora gives up to 12 m for canephora.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> Heights in this compendium are therefore given with the flora they come from; see also Branching and fruiting wood.

The leaves tell them apart more clearly. Arabica leaf blades are 7–18 cm long and 3–7.5 cm wide.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Canephora blades are 12–31.5 cm long, sometimes up to 40 cm, and 4.5–12 cm wide.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Flowers grow in clusters where a leaf meets the branch, the leaf axil. The flora counts 2–20 flowers per axil in arabica. In canephora it counts 8–30, sometimes up to 48.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Both have white flowers and red fruit; cultivated arabica can also bear yellow or purple fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> How flowering and fruit set work is covered in Flowering and pollination.

The fruit takes longer to ripen in canephora, at least in the studies consulted for this entry. A 2025 review gives an average of 220–243 days from flowering to ripe fruit for arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 2025 Brazilian trial recorded 252–308 days from flowering to harvest for conilon, a form of canephora.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The sites and methods differ, so the numbers are not a direct comparison.

## Where each grows best

Arabica is a highland plant and robusta a plant of warmer lowlands, in the classic view. A 2006 review gives an optimum mean annual temperature of 18–21 °C for arabica and 22–26 °C for robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The same review states that robusta tolerates cold much less well than arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

That warm optimum for robusta has been questioned. A 2020 study of yields on 798 farms in South-East Asia found robusta did best below 20.5 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The two findings are set side by side in Contested.

Rain matters too. The 2006 review gives an optimum of 1,200–1,800 mm a year for arabica. It says robusta adapts better than arabica to rainfall above 2,000 mm.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> How growers match a species to a site is covered in Site selection and climate.

## The seeds side by side

The seeds, the coffee beans, differ in shape. One 2022 study measured roasted beans from 21 arabica and 14 robusta samples with a camera system, in dishes of 20 beans: 105 dishes of arabica and 70 of robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The arabica beans were longer on average, 10.95 mm against 10.46 mm. The robusta beans were slightly wider, 8.42 mm against 8.25 mm.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

So robusta beans look rounder and arabica beans more oval. The width-to-length ratio was 0.81 in robusta and 0.76 in arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The drawing shows both means at the same scale.

The study did not test shape alone. Its 100% correct classification came from a model that combined spectral, shape and colour features and sorted dishes of 20 beans, not single beans.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Bean shapes within one species, such as peaberries, are covered in Peaberries and bean shapes.

## What the seeds hold

The seeds contain the same classes of compounds in different amounts. Robusta holds more caffeine: a 2021 review gives 0.9–1.5% of dry weight for arabica and 1.2–2.4% for robusta (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Arabica holds more sucrose. In a 2004 study of wild trees, arabica held 7.99–11.0% sucrose and canephora 5.94–6.47%, on a dry-matter basis.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

Lipids are higher in arabica, about 15% on average against about 10% in robusta (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Chlorogenic acids, a family of phenolic compounds, are usually reported higher in robusta, but a meta-analysis disagrees (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

These differences carry into the cup, according to a roasting chemistry chapter. It links arabica’s higher sucrose to more caramel-like and buttery aroma compounds after roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> It describes robusta’s aroma profile as smoky, earthy, roasty and phenolic.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The whole picture of green coffee chemistry is in Green coffee composition.

## Chemistry behind the differences

**Sucrose and trigonelline.** The 2004 study measured 14 wild species and six further taxa, four trees each.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Arabica had one of the highest sucrose contents in the set, 9.3% on a dry-matter basis. Canephora averaged 6.10%.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Across all species, sucrose ranged from 3.8% to 10.7% and trigonelline from 0.39% to 1.77%.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The authors call both compounds aroma precursors and suggest canephora could be improved by crosses with *C. eugenioides*.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Caffeine and chlorogenic acids.** The caffeine ranges of the two species overlap at 1.2–1.5%, so on these ranges caffeine alone would not separate every sample.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The chlorogenic acid ranges of a 2006 review overlap between 7% and 8.4%.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A 2021 meta-analysis in a master’s thesis found the chlorogenic acid profiles of the two species extremely similar; roast level affected the amounts more than species did, except for total feruloylquinic and dicaffeoylquinic acids.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Chlorogenic acids treats the dispute in full.

**Lipids and diterpenes.** Arabica lipids average about 15%, robusta about 10%.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The diterpene 16-O-methylcafestol (16-OMC) is the species-specific one: a 2006 review reports its absence from arabica as confirmed.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The review calls it the ideal characteristic for detecting robusta in arabica blends.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The diterpenes as a group are covered in Coffee oil.

**Aroma.** According to the roasting chemistry chapter, robusta’s higher amino acids lead to more pyrazines, and its tryptophan to animal-smelling skatole.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The same chapter attributes arabica’s higher diketones, furfurals and furaneol to its higher sucrose.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> No sensory study consulted for this entry compared the two species under one protocol; cup character here rests on that chapter alone.

## Telling them apart in the lab

A buyer may want to know whether green coffee, a roast coffee or a blend contains robusta. Three kinds of method appear in the sources consulted for this entry.

**16-OMC by chromatography.** EN 18003:2024 specifies a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method for 16-OMC in green and roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> It is suitable for 40–1,600 mg/kg; its collaborative study showed that 20–40 mg/kg can also be analysed, depending on the laboratory equipment.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The standard leaves compliance assessment out of scope.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> In Germany, DIN EN 18003:2025-01 replaced DIN 10779:2011-03, a method for roasted coffee only.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> A 2006 review states that the older DIN method detects robusta at less than 2% of a blend.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**16-OMC by NMR.** A 2018 study used low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) on roast coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> It found 16-OMC and 16-O-methylkahweol in arabica for the first time, at an estimated combined level of at least 10 mg/kg in one sample.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> It detected robusta in arabica at about 1–2% w/w. The trace amounts in arabica set a floor near 1%.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> For comparison, the same study cites 600–1,800 mg/kg and 1,204–2,236 mg/kg of 16-OMC in roast robustas from earlier work.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**DNA.** A 2018 method from IRD, the French research institute for development, uses high-resolution melting analysis of DNA from green and roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> It targets differences in chloroplast DNA rather than nuclear DNA, to make detection more sensitive. It identifies the species and, in a blend, estimates the percentage of robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

**Imaging.** The 2022 imaging study combined spectral, shape and colour features of roasted beans, averaged over dishes of 20 beans. Its model classified every dish in the test set correctly and predicted the robusta share in mixtures.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The authors present it as a rapid screening tool.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Sensory comparison under one protocol.** No study consulted for this entry compared arabica and robusta cups with one sensory method; the cup descriptions rest on a textbook chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **Bean shape across origins.** The bean measurements come from one study of roasted beans; how far shape separates the species across origins, grades and green coffee is not established in the sources consulted here.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Detection floor for robusta.** If arabica holds traces of 16-OMC, chemical tests cannot detect robusta much below about 1%; DNA methods avoid that marker, but their limit was not given in the abstract read for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>
- **Robusta’s temperature optimum outside South-East Asia.** The 2020 optimum comes from farms in one region (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

<p class="meta">Food Control 88, 198–206 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodcont.2018.01.014">doi:10.1016/j.foodcont.2018.01.014</a></p>

<p><strong>Kath et al., 2020 – Not so robust.</strong> Modelled yields from 798 robusta farms in South-East Asia against temperature. Found an optimum below 20.5 °C, lower than earlier estimates, which questions the view of robusta as a warm-lowland crop.</p>
