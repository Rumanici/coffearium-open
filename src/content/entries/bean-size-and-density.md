## Two properties of green coffee

Roasters look at two physical properties of green coffee before they roast it: how large the beans are and how dense they are. A roasting textbook chapter names green bean density, moisture content, screen size and shape as factors in the roast plan.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Size** is graded on screens. Each screen number gives the hole size in 64ths of an inch, so screen 18 has holes of 18/64 in (7.14 mm).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How screens and sorting machines work is described under grading and sorting.

**Density** is mass divided by volume. The difficulty is which volume. A heap of beans fills a container with beans and with air between them. One bean alone has its own volume, which includes small pores inside it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> These two volumes give two different densities.

## How size and density are measured

**Screen size.** A weighed sample is shaken through a nest of screens, and the share left on each screen is weighed. ISO 4150 describes this method for green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The details, sample sizes and grade names are in green coffee grading standards.

**Bulk density.** Beans fall freely into a container of known volume, the surface is levelled, and the beans are weighed.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> ISO 6669 standardises this routine method for whole green and roasted beans.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Studies also report bulk density read by grain meters.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Practitioners describe a simple version of the same principle: a graduated cylinder filled to a mark and weighed.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Bean density.** Here the volume of the beans themselves is measured. A weighed sample is dipped into oil, and the oil it displaces gives its volume.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The reading is taken quickly, so that oil does not enter the pores.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Studies call this value bean density or, in some cases, true density.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Always check which method a number comes from. In a 2010 study, coffee seeds without parchment had a bulk density of 393–534 kg/m³ but an oil-displacement density of 1,154–1,235 kg/m³ during drying.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## What makes beans larger or denser

According to ISO 6669, botanical, horticultural, processing, storage and handling factors, including age, all influence the bulk density of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The value also depends on the mass, size and shape of the beans and, less, on their moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Altitude.** Two field studies link higher plantings to heavier or denser beans. On a mountain in Mozambique, beans grown at 935 m had higher mass and density than beans from 650 m.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> They also tended to sit more often on screens 18–20.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Across Ethiopian samples, hundred-bean weight rose with elevation.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The Mozambique study covered one cultivar over a narrow altitude band.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry tested altitude as a predictor of density across many origins.

**Moisture.** Water changes both values. During drying, the oil-displacement density of seeds fell as their moisture rose.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Practitioners report that denser seeds also hold more water.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> In one study of six green coffees, the denser ones were indeed the moister ones.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Moisture is covered in moisture and water activity.

**Defects.** Defective beans differ from sound beans in their physical properties. A 2005 study measured bean and bulk density of defective beans and could tell defect types apart by physical and chemical properties.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Dry mills remove light beans on gravity tables, as described under grading and sorting.

## Size, density and the roast

Practitioners give dense coffees more heat. One importer’s roaster charges with a hot drum but a low flame, and reports that dense coffees tend to stall later in the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> A trade magazine describes higher charge temperatures and more aggressive early heat for dense, high-grown coffees.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> These are practice reports. No peer-reviewed study consulted for this entry tested them.

**What studies show.** In a 2023 calorimetry study of six green coffees, the authors suggest that denser coffee needs more heat in roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In those samples, the denser coffees were also the moister ones (Deep dive).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2025 modelling study uses a drying equation from earlier work in which smaller beans lose moisture faster.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Mixed sizes.** A 2013 review says that green coffee must be even in size for roasting, so that smaller beans do not burn.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Size grading at origin serves this purpose. How heat travels from the surface to the core of a bean is explained under heat transfer.

## Density falls during roasting

Roasting turns a dense seed into a light, porous bean. The beans gain about 50% to almost 100% in volume, while they lose weight.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to a roasting textbook chapter, bean density decreases continuously as the roast goes on.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The weight loss is described under roast loss.

Roast speed matters as well as roast degree. At the same colour, fast-roasted beans have greater volume and porosity and lower density than slow-roasted beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In one study, one arabica roasted to the same colour in four ways ended between 0.548 and 0.592 g/cm³.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

Density therefore says something about a roast, but not on its own, because it falls with roast degree and with roast speed.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> One doctoral thesis monitored its roasts by weight, colour, density, firmness, porosity and degassing together.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Roasted coffee also fills more space: an industrial study measured 335–358 g/L, against 775–880 g/L for green coffee in other studies.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> What this means for dosing by volume is shown under caffeine and roast degree.

## Three densities and the space between beans

Three quantities share the name density. **Bulk density** counts the volume a heap of beans fills, including the gaps.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> **Bean density** counts the volume inside each bean’s outer surface, including its internal pores; it is measured by liquid displacement.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Some studies call the displacement value true density.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Others report an “apparent density” without naming the method.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> A third value, the density of the solid matter without pores, is not reported for coffee in any study consulted for this entry.

The two measured densities give the share of volume between the beans, the intergranular porosity: ε = 1 − ρ<sub>bulk</sub> ÷ ρ<sub>bean</sub>.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> As an illustration only (our calculation, with values from two different studies), a green bulk density of 0.83 g/mL and a bean density of 1.2 g/cm³ give ε ≈ 0.31.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Packing depends on bean shape, size spread and filling, which is why ISO 6669 fixes the free-flow filling and the levelling.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Roasting changes the inside of the bean as well. A 2018 study cites a porosity of about 0.5 in dark-roasted beans.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> How the pores form is the subject of porosity and cell structure; why the cell walls can stretch at all is explained under glass–rubber transition.

## Why density falls

Density falls because volume rises while mass falls. A 2025 modelling study states that volume expansion increases surface area and decreases the specific weight of the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> In a 2018 study in a drum at five temperatures from 200 to 280 °C, bean density varied linearly with moisture content and dropped more at higher roasting temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Expansion was isotropic above 220 °C, and final volume reached up to 1.8 times the green volume.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

According to the roasting textbook chapter, bean density falls continuously, but swelling levels off at some point.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the chapter, faster roasts form gas faster and expand faster, giving lower density at the same colour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2008 study is consistent with this: its fastest roast (160 s) had the lowest density, the production drum roast (1,170 s) the highest.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to the same thesis, light roasts from moister green coffee ended denser, while in dark roasts these differences disappeared.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Size, heat and evenness: the evidence

**Geometry.** For beans of the same shape, surface area per unit volume is inversely proportional to size (our calculation). A screen-18 bean is 18/15 = 1.2 times as wide as a screen-15 bean, so it has about 17% less surface per unit volume (1 ÷ 1.2 ≈ 0.83). How heat moves into the bean is covered under heat transfer. No study consulted for this entry measured how much later the core of a larger bean heats in a roaster.

**Drying rate.** A 2025 modelling study uses a drying-rate equation, taken from earlier work, that depends on bean size and is higher in smaller beans.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The study modelled single beans above screen 18; it did not compare sizes itself.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Heat demand.** The 2023 calorimetry study suggests that denser green coffee needs more heat; its conclusion names density and moisture together.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Its two densest arabicas (831 and 834 g/L) were also the moistest (11.7 and 11.6%, basis not stated), and the lightest (775 g/L) the driest (8.6%).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Water takes heat to warm and evaporate, so with six samples the effect of density cannot be separated from that of moisture (our reading). Calorimetry was done on 10 mg cross-sections, not on whole beans in a roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Practice.** In roasting practice, dense coffees are given a hot charge or more early heat, and they are said to stall before first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a>,<a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The importer’s roaster states that it has no solid research on density and sensory quality.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The magazine article warns that elevation alone should not dictate roasting decisions.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> How charge temperature works is covered under preheating and charge, and the early phase under drying phase.

## Measuring size and density

- **Report the method.** State bulk or bean density, the instrument or container, the unit, and the moisture of the sample (with its basis).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Bulk density.** ISO 6669 is a routine method for whole beans that flow freely; accuracy depends on correct levelling.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It covers whole beans only.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Bean density.** Weigh a sample of about 30 g, immerse it in oil of known density in a wire basket, and read the weight of oil displaced.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Shake out air bubbles, but read quickly so that oil does not enter the pores.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Screen size.** Sieve a 300 g sample as in ISO 4150 and report the mass share on each screen.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Meters.** Grain meters that report bulk density are used in research; their model is named in the studies.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Defective beans.** Black and sour beans roasted to a lesser degree than other beans under the same conditions in a 2005 study.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Pale beans after roasting (quakers) are described under roast defects.
- **Uneven size.** Small beans in a mixed lot can burn, according to a 2013 review.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Misleading numbers.** A green coffee “density” given without method may be a bulk value near 0.8 g/mL or a bean value above 1 g/cm³.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Quenched coffee.** Industrial bulk density was measured on coffees quenched with different amounts of water.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Added water changes both mass and the reading, so compare roasted bulk densities only at known moisture.

## Open questions

- **Density as a roasting guide.** No peer-reviewed study consulted for this entry roasted coffees that differ only in density at equal moisture and size, and measured roast course or flavour.
- **Size and evenness.** The colour spread within a batch of mixed screen sizes has not been measured in the sources consulted.
- **Altitude and density.** The two field studies consulted cover one Mozambican slope and Ethiopian samples.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Whether altitude predicts density across origins, once cultivar and processing are fixed, is not established.
- **Solid density.** No value for the density of coffee solids without pores was found in the sources consulted.
- **Density and cup quality.** Whether density predicts cup quality better than screen size is open, as noted under grading and sorting.

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Baggenstoss et al., 2008 – Roasting speed and bean properties.</strong> Roasts one washed Sumatran arabica to the same colour fast and slow in a laboratory fluid bed, with a temperature profile, and in a production drum. Faster, hotter roasting gave lower bean density, higher volume and lower roast loss; the drum roast was the densest.</p>

<p><strong>Bustos-Vanegas et al., 2018 – Physical properties during roasting.</strong> Follows arabica beans roasted in a drum at five temperatures with X-ray microtomography. Volume reached up to 1.8 times the green volume, and bean density fell linearly with moisture, faster at higher temperatures.</p>
