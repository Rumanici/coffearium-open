brief:
  - The skin and pulp are removed by a pulper, and the seed is dried with some or all of its mucilage still attached, instead of having it washed off.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
  - The amount of mucilage left on is set by the pulping or demucilaging machine, or by a shortened fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

## How it works

Honey processing removes the skin and pulp in a pulper but leaves the mucilage – wholly or in part – on the parchment while the coffee dries.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> There is no fermentation tank and no wash: the mucilage stays on the seed and dries with it.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> After drying, the parchment is removed in a huller, as for washed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

It therefore sits between the two classic families. Washed coffee loses skin, pulp and mucilage before drying; natural coffee keeps all three; honey coffee keeps only the mucilage, or part of it.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Setting the amount of mucilage

The point of the method is to choose how much mucilage dries on the seed. Two ways are reported:

- **Machine settings.** A 2023 review describes the depulping step as adjusted to keep a specific amount of material around the seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> A green-coffee importer reports that higher water pressure and faster rotor speeds remove more mucilage, and that the coffee then dries faster.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **A shortened fermentation.** Cenicafé made honey coffees by stopping tank fermentation early, at about 25%, 50% and 75% mucilage breakdown, and compared them with coffee not degraded at all.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

The review literature describes the result as anything from about 20% to 80% of the mucilage kept.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> In one review, black honey usually keeps all of it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## The colour grades

White, yellow, red and black honey are producer conventions, not defined grades. The two sources that give figures disagree:

- A 2016 review gives about 25% for yellow and about 50% for red, with black usually fully covered.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- A 2023 review, citing a study of smallholders, gives black, red, yellow and white honey as 80% down to 20% of the pulp material left on the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

The 2016 review also ties the colour to drying, not only to the amount kept. Yellow honey dries with the least shade or cloud cover in about 8 days; red honey takes about 12 days under cloud or shade; black honey dries over about 30 days.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> An importer likewise links lighter honeys to quick drying in hot, dry weather, and darker ones to cool, humid conditions.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A "yellow honey" from one farm may therefore not match a "yellow honey" from another.

## Drying

Sticky mucilage changes how the coffee dries. Honey coffee dries faster than natural coffee, but it needs strict control of layer thickness, frequent turning and good ventilation to prevent clumping and local fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Drying time can be steered by the depth of the bed and how often it is turned.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Published drying times vary widely:

- About 8, 12 and 30 days for yellow, red and black honey in one review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- 12 to 30 days in another review, without a split by grade.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>
- 3–6 days in a thin layer in the sun, from 53% to 10–12% moisture (wet basis), in the Cenicafé trial.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

The review that gives the drying times calls further drying of pulped natural coffee a highly sensitive step that must be supervised constantly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Names and history

"Honey" is a name from the coffee trade. According to two green-coffee importers, it was popularised in Costa Rica – as *miel* – for what was already done in Brazil under the name pulped natural.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A 2023 review names Costa Rica and El Salvador among the countries that produce it.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> In Brazil the method is called *cereja descascado* (CD): only the skin is removed, and the fruit is dried with the mucilage attached to the parchment.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Brazilian research also calls it semi-dry<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>: the pulp is removed, there is no fermentation, and the mucilage dries on the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

The names do not line up exactly:

- **Honey = pulped natural = semi-dry.** A 2026 drying review, a 2022 study and an importer all treat them as one method.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Pulped natural as a separate route.** A 2016 review describes pulped natural as removing the pulp with pressure-washing machines and lists it apart from honey.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **"Semi-dry" for fermented but unwashed coffee.** A Colombian study uses semi-dry for beans that are fermented but not washed.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>
- **"Semi-washed" has three meanings.** The Codex code of practice, whose definitions follow ISO 3509, calls pulped coffee dried directly in its mucilaginous parchment and then hulled "semi-washed" green coffee – that is, honey-type coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> An FAO manual for Lao PDR uses "semi-washed" for coffee whose mucilage is removed by a demucilager straight after pulping, without fermentation<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>; that coffee dries without mucilage and belongs with washed coffee. In the trade, an importer lists "semi-washed" as another name for Indonesian wet-hulled coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup> Check which of the three an author means by "semi-washed".

When comparing studies, check what the authors actually did, not which name they used.

## Drying physics with a mucilage layer

Mucilage is mostly water – 85–91% (w/w) in a compilation cited by one study – with 6.2–7.4% sugars.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Keeping more mucilage therefore means more of this watery, sugary layer on the parchment to dry.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

Two effects follow in the sources:

- **Stickiness.** Residual mucilage makes the coffee sticky, which can hinder airflow through the mass.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Wet beans cluster; continuous agitation in rotary dryers reduces local over- or under-drying and limits microbial activity by preventing this clustering.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Speed against shade.** The review that links colour to drying uses shade and cloud cover to slow drying for the darker grades, and full sun to speed it up for yellow.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> An importer reports the same direction: more mucilage removed, faster drying.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

None of the sources consulted for this entry gives drying curves for different retention levels under the same conditions. How much each extra 10% of mucilage adds to drying time is not known from published data.

## Microbiology on drying honey coffee

Without a tank, microbes still act on the mucilage of pulped coffee: researchers study "semi-dry" fermentation and the communities on honey coffees directly.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

- **Communities shift with the amount of mucilage.** In a 2025 Arabica study, bacteria were more active at low mucilage retention and fungi at high retention.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The study found seven dominant bacterial and four dominant fungal genera, and fungal diversity was higher than bacterial diversity across treatments.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Semi-dry Arabica.** Bacteria in a semi-dry trial with starter yeasts included *Weissella*, *Leuconostoc*, *Gluconobacter*, *Pseudomonas*, *Pantoea*, *Erwinia* and *Klebsiella*; other yeasts detected included *Debaryomyces hansenii*.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Inoculated yeasts persisted throughout the fermentation, and the inoculated coffee showed a caramel flavour missing from the control.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **Robusta.** In a 2023 study, honey Robusta was fermented for 192 hours against 72 hours for washed coffee, with effects on carbohydrates, organic acids and flavour precursors.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Chemistry and cup

**Composition.** Seventeen Brazilian Arabica cultivars processed wet had more chlorogenic acids and trigonelline and less sucrose than the same cultivars processed semi-dry; caffeine did not differ.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A parallel comparison of wet and dry processing of Arabica found sucrose not significantly affected, while glucose and fructose were significantly lower in washed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> A Colombian study of Castillo coffee found differences in colour, pH and caffeine between its wet and "semi-dry" (fermented, unwashed) coffees, but none in titratable acidity, chlorogenic acid or cup score.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Aroma.** In a 2025 Arabica study, the treatment that kept 75–80% of the mucilage had the highest relative content of volatile compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A Yunnan study compared seven processing methods and found that its three honey methods enhanced caramel, roasted and buttery notes.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In a 2023 Robusta study, honey coffee showed more pleasant, sweet, tea-rose and chocolate notes than washed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Cup scores.** Honey does not automatically score between washed and natural. In a Peruvian trial with Catimor, honey scored 82.88, below washed (84.00) and natural (85.75); of the sensory attributes, only fragrance and body differed significantly between methods.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In the Cenicafé trial, cup scores did not differ statistically between treatments.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> For *C. canephora* in Ecuador, the Conilon type had its best sensory attributes at high altitude with honey processing.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Defective cups from partial removal.** In the Cenicafé trial, making honey coffees by stopping fermentation early carried a 22–77% probability of cups with sensory defects.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Clumping and local fermentation.** Thick layers, rare turning or poor ventilation let sticky beans clump and ferment in place.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Uneven drying.** Clusters of wet beans dry unevenly; agitation reduces local over- and under-drying.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Fungal load.** Fungi are more active at high mucilage retention.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The sources here do not link specific grades to mould defects or ochratoxin A.
- **Supervision.** Drying pulped natural coffee is a sensitive step that needs constant supervision.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Measuring the process

Moisture is tracked from the start of drying to the end point – in the Cenicafé trial from 53% down to 10–12% (wet basis).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **No standard.** Colour names mean different amounts of mucilage in different sources, and one ties them to drying conditions as well.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>
- **Colour: mucilage or drying?** Whether the colour of the dried parchment comes mainly from the amount kept or from drying speed has not been tested head-to-head in the sources consulted here.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Sugars.** Semi-dry coffee held more sucrose than wet in one study<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>; how glucose and fructose in honey coffee compare with washed and natural coffee is not measured in the sources here.
- **Microbial succession.** The trials report communities at selected points; how they change day by day across drying for each grade is still open.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
