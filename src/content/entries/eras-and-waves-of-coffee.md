## What the wave model says

Coffee people often tell recent coffee history as a sequence of “waves”. A coffee historian sums up the story as a journey in three steps.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> First came commodity coffee for the mass market. Then came gourmet espresso drinks in coffee-shop chains. Then came single-origin coffees in small artisan cafés, roasted and brewed with care to show their flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Each wave is linked to typical products. A coffee trade textbook chapter ties the first wave to vacuum-packed and soluble (instant) coffee, sold as an undifferentiated commodity.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The second wave added grades, some traceability and roasting styles.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2017 discussion paper sees the second wave at its peak in the spread of Starbucks.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The third wave is described as an independent subculture of coffeehouses, roasters and home hobbyists.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It values craft, expertise and direct contact between barista and customer.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Its buyers want to know where and how a coffee was grown and processed.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The specialty movement promotes the idea that specialty coffee expresses its quality through a distinctive flavour in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A lot is a batch from one origin and process, traded as a unit. According to the 2017 discussion paper, almost all third-wave coffees are washed arabicas from high altitudes, sold as micro-lots from single estates.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The model is a story the trade tells about itself, not a research finding. The 2015 study that describes it says this history might be revisionist, imprecise and focused on North America.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Where the terms came from

The phrase “third wave” is credited to Trish Rothgeb, an American roaster who published it as Trish Skeie.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> She used it in The Flamekeeper, the newsletter of the Roasters Guild, after seeing how coffee quality was pursued in Norway.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A coffee historian dates the article to spring 2003; a 2018 peer-reviewed study gives 2002 (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The original newsletter could not be accessed for this entry.

According to the historian, Rothgeb described the third wave as a reaction to those who wanted to automate or homogenise specialty coffee – the second-wave chains.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The term was invented to contrast artisanal roasters and coffeehouses with the forms of coffee drinking before them.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The first and second waves serve as that contrast.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The older term “specialty coffee” is traced to the coffee importer Erna Knutsen in the 1970s.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> She used it for coffees of the best flavour from special microclimates.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The sources disagree on the exact year (see Contested).

## Before the waves: older eras

The three-wave story starts late. A coffee historian notes that it squeezes six centuries of recorded coffee history into a single first phase.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> He reports a five-wave proposal that splits the first wave into a “pioneering” period and an “industrial” period, the latter beginning at the end of the 19th century.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In the pioneering phase, coffee spread from Arabia through the Ottoman Empire to Europe, and the first coffeehouses appeared. That history has its own entry: Coffeehouses from Mecca to Europe. For most of this time, consumers bought green coffee and roasted it themselves.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The industrial era moved that work to the roaster. According to the historian, roasting became a stand-alone business in the late 19th century.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Packaged, branded, pre-ground coffee followed, then soluble coffee and ready-to-drink products after the Second World War.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Cheapness, consistency and convenience were what the mass market demanded.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Espresso belongs to this era too. In 1905 Desiderio Pavoni put the first espresso machine into industrial production, the La Pavoni Ideale.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Achille Gaggia introduced the spring-lever machine in 1948.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Both are described in Espresso machine.

## Institutions and milestones

The specialty trade built its own institutions alongside the wave story. The Specialty Coffee Association of America (SCAA) was founded in 1982 and the Specialty Coffee Association of Europe (SCAE) in 1998.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In 2017 the two unified as the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA).<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Competitions followed. The first World Barista Championship was held in 2000.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to the SCA’s magazine, it took place in Monte Carlo and was won by the Norwegian barista Robert William Thoresen.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The Cup of Excellence, a competition and online auction for top coffees from one producing country, began in Brazil in 1999, according to its organiser; a 2013 conference paper places its origin in the late 1990s.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

Shared tasting systems came too. An SCAA education foundation became the Coffee Quality Institute (CQI), which developed the Q Coffee System for cupping and grading on the SCAA protocol.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> As of October 2026, CQI states that it has ceased to administer this programme.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> How the SCA’s cupping form developed is told in Cupping.

## Other coffee cultures, other paths

A coffee historian argues that the wave story fits the Anglo-American markets but not mature coffee cultures elsewhere.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Italy.** Espresso, rather than espresso-based drinks, has stayed central.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Coffee-shop chains did not develop there, partly because maximum prices for coffee at the bar kept margins low.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Nordic countries.** The third wave was first named after a visit to Norway, before coffee-shop chains reached the region.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Light roasts predominate, and Nordic barista contests set the precedent for the World Barista Championship.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Japan.** The café in Japan dates from the late 19th century, according to a university-press history.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The historian notes that Japanese café culture shaped many practices later called third wave, and that third-wave cafés adopted Japanese drippers such as the V60.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Australia.** A 2010 study rejects the wave analogy for Australia.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It questions the idea that post-war Italian migrants brought coffee drinking to Australia. It dates espresso as known in 2010 to late in the 20th century.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Coffee-drinking customs around the world are compared in Coffee drinks around the world.

The 2017 discussion paper adds its own dating of the second wave: Peet’s and Zabar’s as pioneers, with Starbucks as the peak.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry gives a definition of the fourth wave that others share.

**History and geography.** A coffee historian calls the narrative “somewhat reductive” and says it can be challenged on both geographical and historical grounds.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Within the United States, the specialty movement began with small roasters who sold whole beans and roasted dark to contrast with mass-market blends, according to the historian.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> He reads the American third wave as a return to the spirit of these pioneers, and he warns against seeing it through a national lens, because it has become a transnational community of enthusiasts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Imprecision.** The 2015 study uses the first-to-third-wave sequence only as its participants’ own account. Its author says the account might be revisionist, imprecise, North-American-focused and generally unsatisfactory as history.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> He notes that coffee and coffeehouses existed for centuries in many countries before the American 1950s.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Value and power.** The 2017 discussion paper studies the third-wave market in the United States. It argues that roasters, baristas and marketers have built a new vocabulary of quality around provenance and exclusivity, and that this vocabulary creates much of the value added in the trade.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Smallholder producers own land and trees but lack the cultural capital to capture much of that value; the author sees older patterns of dependency reproduced.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Certification schemes and other claims about how coffee is produced are covered in Certifications and labels.

**National paths.** A 2010 study of Australia dismisses the wave analogy as an explanation of the country’s coffee culture.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It describes Australians as predominantly tea drinkers across all classes until the Second World War, Greek and Italian roasters setting up in the post-war decades, and espresso as known in 2010 established only late in the 20th century.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Waves and the coffee price

The wave story also describes a change in how coffee is priced. A coffee trade textbook chapter argues that in the first and second waves, quality was handled as a premium on top of the exchange reference price.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In the third wave, it says, new business models of roasters have challenged how coffee is referenced to the exchange price.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> According to the same chapter, buyers of second- and third-wave roasters seek coffee from exporters or cooperatives, and third-wave buyers often call their purchasing direct trade.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> How exchange prices and premiums combine is explained in Differentials.

The capsule market shows a different path. The historian reports that capsule systems share the convenience and consistency of industrial-era coffee but sell at a premium, and that some present themselves as a route into connoisseurship in ways that parallel the third wave.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Switzerland’s role in capsule exports is covered in Switzerland and the coffee trade.

## Open questions

- **Origin of the term.** The 2002 and 2003 dates both rest on secondary citations of The Flamekeeper.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> No archived copy of the newsletter could be accessed for this entry, and an earlier use of “third wave” claimed by others was not checked against a primary text.
- **A fourth wave.** No source consulted for this entry gives a definition of the fourth wave that other sources share.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Producing countries.** A coffee historian names rising coffee consumption within producing countries as the most significant development for the global market.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry tests whether the wave model describes coffee cultures in producing countries.
- **Measuring the waves.** No study consulted for this entry measured market shares of first-, second- and third-wave coffee with a shared definition.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Morris, 2017 – We consumers: tastes, rituals and waves.</strong> A historian traces coffee drinking from Arabian coffeehouses through industrial taste communities in the United States, Europe and Italy to the specialty “quality turn”. He treats the three waves as an internal narrative of the specialty movement and shows where it fails outside Anglo-American markets.</p>
<p class="meta">In: Folmer (ed.), The Craft and Science of Coffee, ch. 19 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-803520-7.00019-0">doi:10.1016/B978-0-12-803520-7.00019-0</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Manzo, 2015 – Third-wave coffeehouses as venues for sociality.</strong> An ethnomethodological study of independent coffeehouses and their baristas. It finds face-to-face sociality thriving there and records how members define the three waves, while calling that history imprecise and North American.</p>
<p class="meta">The Qualitative Report 20(6), 746–761 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.46743/2160-3715/2015.2141">doi:10.46743/2160-3715/2015.2141</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Fischer, 2017 – Quality and inequality.</strong> A case study of the high-end third-wave market in the United States. It argues that control over the language of quality, held by roasters, baristas and marketers, captures much of the value, while smallholder producers carry the risk.</p>
<p class="meta">MPIfG Discussion Paper 17/4 · <a href="https://ideas.repec.org/p/zbw/mpifgd/174.html">RePEc:zbw:mpifgd:174</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Adams, 2010 – Thoroughly modern coffee.</strong> Examines how Australians have related to coffee across the 20th century and dismisses the wave analogy as an explanation of Australian coffee culture. It also questions the story that post-war Italian migrants brought coffee drinking to Australia.</p>
<p class="meta">TEXT, special issue ‘Rewriting the menu’ · <a href="https://textjournal.scholasticahq.com/article/31514.pdf">full text (PDF)</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Boaventura et al., 2018 – Value co-creation and the third-wave movement.</strong> A study of value co-creation along the specialty coffee chain in Brazil. Its background section summarises the three waves and dates the naming of the third wave to 2002.</p>
<p class="meta">Revista de Administração de Empresas 58(3), 254–266 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1590/S0034-759020180306">doi:10.1590/S0034-759020180306</a></p>
