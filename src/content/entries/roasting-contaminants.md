## What roasting contaminants are

Roasting builds flavour, colour and aroma through chains of chemical reactions. A few products of the same reactions are unwanted. The EU treats acrylamide as a contaminant that forms in food from precursors in the raw material.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2023 review calls such compounds thermal contaminants induced by roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The roasting chemistry chapter of a 2017 textbook names acrylamide and furan as Maillard-type products to mitigate.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

The best-studied ones in coffee are acrylamide and a group of furans: furan itself and the methylfurans.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> A 2023 review also lists polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), 4-methylimidazole and several other groups.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

This entry covers what forms in the coffee itself. Smoke and gases that leave through the stack are covered in roaster emissions. The entry describes what authorities state and require; it gives no health advice.

## Acrylamide: made early, broken down later

Acrylamide forms from sugars and amino acids, mainly asparagine, through the Maillard reaction.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Green coffee contains both precursors. In one 2021 study, green coffee had no measurable acrylamide; it appeared only on roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Acrylamide does not simply build up as the roast goes on. It peaks in the lighter roast degrees and then falls as roasting continues.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In a 2020 industrial roasting study, it peaked after 10 min at 175–177 °C. In the finished coffee it was close to 300 µg/kg, well below the peak.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Robusta (*C. canephora*) and arabica (*C. arabica*) coffee differ. The EU regulation on acrylamide states that robusta-based products tend to have higher levels.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Studies mostly agree, though one industrial study found the opposite at the peak.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Furans: rising with roast degree

Furan and its methylated relatives behave differently. They are volatile compounds, and their sum was higher the darker and the longer the roast in a 2023 study.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In a 2011 study, furan in espresso brews rose about sixfold from the lightest to the darkest roast.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

2-Methylfuran is the most abundant of them, followed by furan.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Furan is volatile, and part of it can evaporate during roasting and brewing.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In a Canadian survey, instant coffee and coffee beverages had relatively low levels of furans compared with other coffee products.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) names coffee as the main source of furan in the diet of adults.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Other compounds: PAHs, 4-methylimidazole and ochratoxin A

**Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons** are compounds that form in high-temperature processes such as roasting, smoking and barbecuing.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In a 2019 study, a mild roast lowered PAHs compared with the green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> A 2023 study found benzo[a]pyrene, a marker PAH, only in two dark-roasted samples.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**4-Methylimidazole** forms from sugars reacting with ammonia. A 2023 review reports higher average levels in robusta than in arabica coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Ochratoxin A** is not formed by roasting. It is a mould toxin that can form during drying and storage of green coffee, as the Codex code of practice describes; moisture and water activity covers it.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The Codex code of practice states that roasting reduces it by 65–100%, depending on the process.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

## What a roaster can and cannot change

The EU regulation asks food business operators to identify the roast conditions that keep acrylamide low within the target flavour profile.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It also asks them to consider the share of robusta in a blend and the use of asparaginase.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Asparaginase is an enzyme that breaks down asparagine before roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

These levers pull in different directions. A darker or longer roast lowers acrylamide but raises furans.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A UK coffee trade code warns that roasting darker cannot be considered a universal option.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> A 2023 study concluded that lowering both at once by roasting changes seems not to be possible.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Some things lie outside the roaster's hands. Acrylamide in roasted coffee falls during storage, depending on time and temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Part of the furan evaporates during brewing, in the cup or the pot.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Acrylamide chemistry

**Precursors.** Acrylamide forms from reducing sugars and amino acids, mainly asparagine, in the Maillard reaction.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The 2023 batch study found acrylamide strongly related to free asparagine, and furans to sucrose more than to the other sugars.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In the 2021 study, green robusta contained more asparagine than arabica (800 ± 50 versus 540 ± 40 mg/kg) and less reducing sugar.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Sucrose must first split into glucose and fructose before it can react; the Maillard reaction entry covers that step.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

**Formation and elimination.** In the 2020 industrial study, asparagine fell quickly, while glucose and fructose peaked at 12 min and then degraded fast.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The authors attribute the sugar rise mainly to sucrose hydrolysis.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Acrylamide peaked before the sugars did and fell below 400 µg/kg four minutes later.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> An early oven and laboratory-roaster study reported that beans roasted longer, or at higher temperatures, had less acrylamide.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2023 review places the formation peak in the intermediate stage of roasting, at 160–180 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Rate at the same colour.** A UK trade code, based on a European industry toolbox, states that roasting faster gives higher acrylamide at the same roast colour.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured acrylamide at one colour across several roast durations with numbers in an accessible text.

**After roasting.** Acrylamide in roasted coffee decreases during storage, depending on time and temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> In a radiotracer study, close to 90% of the label in spent grounds was firmly bound to the coffee matrix, and furanthiols did not explain the loss.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> According to a decaffeination textbook chapter, acrylamide is formed in roasting and its quantity is not changed by decaffeination or steaming.<sup><a href="#src-s21">21</a></sup>

## Furan chemistry

**Precursors.** EFSA lists ascorbic acid, amino acids and carbohydrates among the precursors of furans, and notes that furan and methylfurans can occur together.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In coffee, furan levels correlated with sucrose.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The roasting chemistry chapter warns that model systems may not predict how furan forms in a roasting bean.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

**Roast degree and rate.** In the 2011 study, furan in espresso brews rose from 22 ± 2 to 138 ± 8 ng/ml as L* fell from 47.4 to 23.16.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> At one colour, a slow roast at 140 °C for 20 min gave the lowest level (64 ± 5 ng/ml); faster, hotter roasts gave 89 and 138 ng/ml.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The authors suggest more furan evaporated during the long roast.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In the 2023 roasting study, steam-treated and water-cooled samples were lower in furans.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Losses before the cup.** Furan is volatile.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In the 2011 study, espresso brews held 32–85% more furan than drip brews from the same coffees, which the authors link to shorter contact and higher pressure.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured the furan lost on grinding.

## PAHs and 4-methylimidazole

**PAHs.** In the 2019 study, pyrolytic reactions begin above 170 °C; some PAHs form above 220 °C and heavier ones from 250–260 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Its mild roast lowered total PAHs, which the authors attribute to the volatility of light PAHs.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The authors name drying and roasting as probably the main sources of PAHs in coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In the 2023 retail study, chrysene and pyrene dominated, which the authors read as a probable sign of roasting at 280 °C or more.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> A 2023 review states that longer roasting raises high-molecular-weight PAHs.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry compared PAHs in coffee from direct-fired and indirectly heated roasters.

**4-Methylimidazole.** The 2023 review describes its formation from sugars and ammonia and higher average levels in robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry reported its levels in roasted coffee with accessible numbers.

## Authorities and regulation

Rules and assessments as consulted on 20 October 2026. The legal text in force applies.

**EU acrylamide regulation.** Regulation (EU) 2017/2158 defines benchmark levels as performance indicators used to verify the effectiveness of the mitigation measures.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> They are based on experience and occurrence for broad food categories.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For coffee, the regulation requires operators to identify critical roast conditions and to control them in a prerequisite programme under good manufacturing practice.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It asks them to consider asparaginase treatment where possible and effective, and to take robusta's higher levels into account in blend risk assessment.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its recitals describe acrylamide as a contaminant and a chemical hazard in the food chain, formed in foods whose raw materials include coffee beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**EFSA on acrylamide (2015).** EFSA found the highest acrylamide levels in solid coffee substitutes and coffee, and in fried potato products.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It found that epidemiological associations had not shown acrylamide to be a human carcinogen, but that the margins of exposure indicate a concern for neoplastic effects based on animal evidence.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified acrylamide in 1994 as probably carcinogenic to humans (Group 2A).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**EFSA on furan and methylfurans (2017).** In adults and older people, coffee is the main contributor to dietary exposure to furan.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> EFSA concluded that the calculated margins of exposure indicate a health concern.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) describes furan as possibly carcinogenic to humans, based on high-dose animal tests.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The CFIA reports that maximum levels for furans have not yet been established in Canada; Health Canada did not expect the levels in the survey to pose a health concern.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**PAHs and 4-methylimidazole.** A 2023 review states that no maximum limits for PAHs are set for coffee, and a 2023 analytical paper says the same for coffee beverages, as of their writing.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The review reports that IARC classifies 4-methylimidazole as possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B).<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Mitigation options

- **Roast degree and time.** Longer and darker roasting lowers acrylamide.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> It raises furans.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Blend.** A lower robusta share is one factor the EU regulation asks operators to consider.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Asparaginase.** A 2023 review cites studies reporting a 60% reduction of asparagine in arabica and 35% in robusta, and a 59% reduction of acrylamide in arabica, from enzyme treatment.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The UK trade code gives the treatment potential relevance for only a very modest fraction of coffee production.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>
- **Special processes.** The 2023 review lists vacuum and superheated-steam roasting, supercritical CO₂ extraction and acrylamide-degrading enzymes, and notes that cost, scale-up and sensory effects are often unstudied.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In one 2023 study, double roasting cut acrylamide in light roasts by up to 36%.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Cooling.** In the same study, water quenching of a light robusta roast gave 543 µg/kg acrylamide against 371 µg/kg with air cooling.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The cooling and quenching entry covers the methods.

## Measurement

The 2023 roasting study measured acrylamide by GC-MS/MS after derivatisation (DIN EN ISO 18862), with acrylamide-d3 as internal standard.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Furans were measured by headspace GC-MS with deuterated standards.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Because furans are volatile, the 2011 study stored samples sealed at 4 °C and brews in vials without headspace.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Acrylamide and furans together.** One 2023 study found no roasting change that lowered both.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Whether a profile exists that lowers both within a given flavour target was not found in the sources consulted.
- **Roast rate at one colour.** Numeric data on acrylamide for fast and slow roasts to the same colour were not found in an accessible text for this entry.
- **Shop-scale roasters.** The studies consulted used pilot, laboratory or industrial roasters; none measured contaminants on roasters of a few kilograms per batch.
- **Heating type and PAHs.** No study consulted for this entry compared direct-fired and indirect roasters.
- **4-Methylimidazole levels.** No accessible measurements in roasted coffee were found for this entry.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Schouten et al., 2021 – Acrylamide across five roast degrees.</strong> Roasted Brazilian arabica and Indian robusta coffee from light to dark on a pilot drum roaster and followed acrylamide and antioxidant activity. Acrylamide peaked at the light-medium degree, higher in robusta, and fell by more than 80% to the dark roast.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry 343, 128514 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2020.128514">doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2020.128514</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bertuzzi et al., 2020 – Acrylamide in an industrial roaster.</strong> Sampled arabica and robusta coffee during industrial roasting and measured acrylamide, asparagine and sugars. Acrylamide peaked at 10 min, at 175–177 °C, and the finished coffee held about 300 µg/kg.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry, 125372 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2019.125372">doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2019.125372</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Alsafra et al., 2023 – Precursors behind the contaminants.</strong> Roasted 64 batches of green coffee to one profile and related acrylamide and four furans to sugars and amino acids by multivariate statistics. Acrylamide followed free asparagine; furans followed sucrose.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry 427, 136655 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2023.136655">doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2023.136655</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Engelhardt et al., 2023 – Acrylamide and furans move in opposite directions.</strong> Roasted robusta and arabica coffee on tangential, drum and hot-air roasters with several profiles and pretreatments. Acrylamide was highest in light roasts and furans in dark and long roasts; the authors concluded that lowering both at once seems not to be possible.</p>
<p class="meta">Beverage Plant Research 3, 8 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.48130/BPR-2023-0008">doi:10.48130/BPR-2023-0008</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Altaki et al., 2011 – Furan from roaster to cup.</strong> Measured furan in coffees roasted to different degrees and rates and in brews from espresso machines, drip makers, capsules and instant coffee. Furan rose with roast degree, was lowest after a slow roast, and was higher in espresso than in drip brews.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry 126(4), 1527–1532 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2010.11.134">doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2010.11.134</a></p>
