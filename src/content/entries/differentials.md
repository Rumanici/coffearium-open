## What a differential is

Most green coffee is priced in two parts. The first is a futures price, set on an exchange and published every trading day. The second is the differential: a premium or discount that belongs to one particular coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The price of the physical coffee is the futures price plus the differential.

For arabica the reference is the Coffee “C” contract in New York, quoted in US cents per pound (lb).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> For robusta it is the London robusta contract, quoted in US dollars per tonne.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A differential is always stated against one of these, and against a named delivery month (see Coffee futures).

The trade also calls the same gap the “basis”.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the general futures definition, the basis is the cash price minus the price of the nearest futures contract. It may reflect differences in time, product form, grade and location.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Who sets it

Commercial differentials are not published as a price reference. Seller and buyer negotiate one for each lot: a batch of coffee from one origin and process, traded as a unit.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to a 2017 textbook chapter, this is most of the time a buyer’s market, in which a few large buyers dominate the trade.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In a typical chain, a cooperative or exporter sells to an importer or roaster. They agree quantity, quality, shipment month and the differential, and leave the futures part open.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> That futures part is fixed later against the futures month named in the contract.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How such contracts are written is explained in Green coffee contracts.

## What moves a differential

A differential packs several things into one number. According to the textbook chapter, it blends quality, origin and supply and demand in the producing country.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The definition of the basis adds time and location.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In practice the main drivers are these:

- **Origin.** Buyers pay more for some growths than others. The exchange’s own schedule shows this: Colombian coffee is delivered at a premium, Brazilian at a discount.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Quality and bean size.** Cleaner coffee and larger screen sizes earn more. The robusta contract prices this directly, with a premium for its best class and discounts for weaker ones.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Certification.** Certified coffee can carry extra money. Fairtrade adds a Premium and an organic differential to its minimum or the market price.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Shipment period.** A differential is stated against a named futures month. Futures months differ from each other by the spread.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Location and logistics.** A price at the port of export (free on board, FOB) differs from a price in a European warehouse. Freight and other costs lie between them.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Local supply and demand.** When one origin has a short crop, its differential can rise.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Exchange differentials and commercial differentials

The word “differential” has two meanings in coffee. The first is set by the exchange. A futures contract names a standard quality, the basis grade. Coffee better or worse than that standard is delivered at a fixed premium or discount.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The Coffee “C” rule is short: coffees judged better are at a premium; those judged inferior are at a discount.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

As of October 2026, Colombia, Costa Rica and Kenya are delivered at a premium of 10 US cents per lb, and Guatemala at 5 cents. Brazil is delivered at a discount of 6 cents.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> From the May 2027 contract, Vietnam can also be delivered, at a discount of 6 cents.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The second meaning is the commercial differential, agreed in a real trade. It is not published.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The two are linked. In 2024 ICE raised the premiums for Colombia, Kenya, Costa Rica and Guatemala. It said the change would better reflect current commercial origin differentials.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The exchange schedule changes only by rule amendment. Commercial differentials, in the words of the ICE filing, are constantly subject to change, both within and across crop years.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Why a hedge does not cover it

A trader who holds coffee can protect it against falling prices by selling futures (see Coffee futures). If prices fall, the futures gain makes up for the loss on the coffee. This works only for the futures part. If the differential changes between the day the hedge is placed and the day it is lifted, that change stays with the trader. Regulators call this basis risk.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The following example uses round, invented numbers. **They are example values, not market quotes.**

| Step (example) | Physical coffee | Futures |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | An importer buys a lot at 340 ¢/lb: futures 300 + differential 40 | Sells futures at 300 ¢/lb |
| Day 2 | Sells the lot at 290 ¢/lb: futures 260 + differential 30 | Buys the futures back at 260 ¢/lb |
| Result | Loss of 50 ¢/lb | Gain of 40 ¢/lb |
| Net | | −10 ¢/lb: the change in the differential (40 → 30) |

The hedge removed the effect of the 40-cent fall in futures. What remained was the 10-cent fall in the differential (our arithmetic). Had the differential risen to 50, the importer would have gained 10 cents instead.

## Definitions: basis, differential and basis risk

The US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) separates the terms clearly. The basis is the difference between the spot or cash price and the price of the nearest futures contract, usually calculated as cash minus futures; it may reflect different time periods, product forms, grades or locations.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Differentials, in the CFTC’s use, are the discounts or premiums allowed for grades or locations below or above the par grade of a futures contract.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Basis risk is the risk of an unexpected widening or narrowing of the basis between the time a hedge is placed and the time it is lifted.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The coffee trade uses “differential” for both: the exchange’s fixed allowances and the negotiated cash-minus-futures gap.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> This entry calls them exchange differentials and commercial differentials. The 2017 textbook chapter also names the gap between two futures months, the spread, as a separate variable of economic importance.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

A commercial differential has no meaning without the futures month it refers to. A lot shipped in October may be priced as “December plus 40”; if the same lot were priced against March, the differential would differ by the December–March spread. This follows from the definition of the basis against a named contract (our reading of the CFTC definition).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Tight markets, short crops and price spikes

A short crop at one origin can push its differential up. In crop year 2008/09, Colombian production fell by 24%, according to an ICO market report; the ICO attributed the tight supply of Colombian Milds to climatic problems and a tree replanting programme.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The ICO’s annual review gives a larger fall, around 30.7% (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The ICO described the result as an unprecedented increase in the premium for this origin.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The Colombian Milds premium over New York averaged 5.42 US cents per lb in 2007/08 and 42.26 in 2008/09, a rise of 680%.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The 2017 textbook chapter describes the same episode from an exporter’s side: Colombian differentials rose within a few weeks and pulled the differentials of other origins up behind them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The spike did not last. In the ICO’s monthly series the premium fell from 80.62 cents in May 2009 to 46.12 cents in September 2009.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The ICO expected Colombian supply to return to normal in crop year 2009/10.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

A forward seller is exposed to such moves. The textbook chapter describes an export manager who sold much of the expected crop forward at the old differentials; when differentials jumped, the cooperative’s members believed they could have earned much more.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same chapter notes that a cooperative bound to a fixed price can lose its members’ coffee to other buyers when market prices rise, and may then default.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Differentials and futures do not always move together. In September 2026 the New York average fell by 10.4% on the month, while the Colombian Milds gap over it barely changed (74.26 to 75.47 cents; our arithmetic).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Two months are not a trend, and no source consulted for this entry measured how commercial differentials behave across price spikes in general (see Open questions).

## Specialty coffee and outright prices

Some coffee is not priced against the exchange at all. Second- and third-wave roasters often buy FOB from exporters or cooperatives, or directly from producers, and organise freight and insurance themselves.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Contracts can fix one complete price at once (an outright price) or use futures plus a differential (see How a coffee price is made). The Specialty Coffee Transaction Guide, which collects FOB contract prices, lists fixed versus differential contracting as one of the questions its data can answer.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

Auctions set prices by bidding. In the Cup of Excellence auctions studied in a 2007 university working paper, coffees were sold online to the highest bidder, and prices rose with the competition’s quality score.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> According to that paper, the winning Brazilian coffee of 2005 sold for many times the ICO price for Brazilian Naturals in the same month.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

Certification minimums interact with both models. A data brief of the Specialty Coffee Transaction Guide, built on FOB contract prices, notes that a mandated minimum does not oblige producers to price all certified coffee at the same floor.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Fairtrade’s organic differential is added to the minimum or to the market reference price, whichever is higher.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Quality and price links between coffees

How closely the prices of two coffees move together depends partly on how similar the coffees are. A 2018 peer-reviewed study tested long-run relationships between pairs of coffee prices. It found that coffees with chemical similarity have more similar prices, are more likely to keep a stable long-run relationship and return faster after a shock.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> In differential terms, a coffee close to the exchange’s basis quality should keep a steadier differential than one far from it; that is our reading, and the study did not measure FOB differentials.

## Open questions

- **Volatility of commercial differentials.** A 2017 textbook chapter describes differentials as usually less volatile than the futures price, but not always.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2024 ICE filing describes commercial differentials as constantly subject to change, within and across crop years.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry gives a long, public series of FOB differentials by origin; the ICO group gaps are averages in consuming markets.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **Differentials in a broad price spike.** The 2008/09 case concerned one origin’s short crop.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> How differentials of all origins respond when futures themselves spike was not measured in a study consulted here.
- **Buyer power.** The textbook calls the differential market mostly a buyer’s market<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>, but no study consulted here measured how the negotiated differential is split between exporter and importer.

<p><strong>International Coffee Organization, 2010 – Annual Review 2008/09.</strong> The review reports coffee-year averages of the gaps between the ICO group indicators and between Colombian Milds and New York futures.
