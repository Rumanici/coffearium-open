brief:
  - In roasting practice and in one drum study, the drying phase runs from charge until the beans visibly change colour, a point roasters call yellowing.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>
  - The early roast is mainly endothermic because the water in the bean must be heated, which demands a high energy input.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
  - Water does not stop leaving at yellowing; in a fluid-bed study, water formed by reactions made up 36–41% of all water evaporated.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
  - One single-bean model predicts a drying front moving inward from the surface, with a ridge of high water-vapour pressure near it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
  - No study consulted for this entry tested drying-phase length alone for flavour; at constant colour, time to first crack mattered less than development time.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## What the drying phase is

The drying phase is the first stage of a roast. It begins at the charge, when green coffee enters the preheated roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In roasting practice it ends when the beans begin to change colour, usually described as "yellowing".<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> A 2024 drum study used the same marker and called the phase "pre-colour change".<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Researchers divide the roast into drying, flavour and colour development, and cooling.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> One modelling study calls drying generally the longest phase of the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In the 2024 study, it lasted 4.5–8.5 min of 16 min roasts, depending on the profile.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Previous step: preheating and charge. Next step: browning phase. It is step 2 of 6 in Coffearium's roast steps; the curve as a whole is explained under roast profiles.

## Heat in, water out

Green coffee still holds water when it enters the roaster. Studies cited here used green coffee at 8.3% and 10.5% moisture (wet basis, wb), and at 10% (basis not stated).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Moisture content and water activity are explained in their own entry.

Heat reaches the bean from hot air (convection) and from hot surfaces such as the drum wall (conduction).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At first, much of that heat goes into warming the water in the bean. The early roast is therefore mainly endothermic: it takes in a lot of energy.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Evaporation also cools the bean, and a 2025 single-bean model places the start of that cooling at 100 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Inside the bean, water turns to vapour as the temperature rises. According to a Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) handbook, the vapour builds up pressure inside the dense green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A 2017 model predicts a drying front: a boundary between a drying outer layer and a moist core, moving inward.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Near this front, the model shows a ridge of high water-vapour pressure.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

As the water leaves, the bean changes. Vapour release begins the expansion of the inner tissue.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Above 100 °C, browning reactions start.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The colour moves from greenish grey-blue towards yellow.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## When drying ends

Yellowing is a visual marker, not a physical boundary. A practitioner course notes that it has no clear definition and is somewhat subjective.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> The same course says beans release moisture steadily until shortly before first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Measurements agree with that second point.

In a laboratory fluid-bed study, beans lost water throughout the roast, not only at the start.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The green coffee's own water left first. In the slow roast, its evaporation had become almost negligible after about 300 s of 720 s.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> After that, most of the water leaving came from chemical reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Over the whole roast, reaction water made up 41% of the water evaporated in a fast roast and 36% in a slow one.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Scientists also define the end of drying in different ways. A chemistry chapter describes a drying phase up to about 100 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> A single-bean model defines it as the bean being below 160 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The chart shows these and other early-roast events on one temperature scale.

A roaster's bean probe shows yet another number. According to a roasting textbook chapter, it reads a mix of bean surface and hot air, specific to each machine.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The chapter therefore calls comparing such temperatures between roasting systems problematic.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## What shapes the drying phase

**The heat applied.** According to a roasting textbook chapter, faster heating makes dehydration proceed faster.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the fluid-bed study, the peak evaporation rate was higher in the fast roast, while more water evaporated in total in the slow roast.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The same chapter says the profile decides how much water leaves in the first stages.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Airflow.** More air per kilogram of coffee raises the air speed at the bean surface. According to the same chapter, this speeds up the evaporation of water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> An airflow change acts on convection almost at once, whereas a flame change takes longer.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Drum speed.** The chapter describes faster drum speeds as tossing the beans more, so they spend less time on the hot wall.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Too fast a drum presses beans against the wall and can scorch their flat sides.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured drum speed and drying directly.

**Batch size.** A 2025 study on a small spouted-bed roaster varied batches from 200 to 500 g and the airflow.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> This gave more varied time–temperature curves than changing the inlet air temperature, especially early in the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**The green coffee.** Wetter beans lose more water in the first phase and end at a similar final moisture, according to the textbook chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Practitioners also adjust the early roast for bean density; this is roasting practice, not a tested finding (see Deep dive).<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>

## Water inside the bean

**Drying front.** A 2017 study modelled one bean as a 4 mm sphere roasted at 200 °C, with an initial moisture of 12%.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Its new first-principles model (Model 2) shows a drying front that moves to the centre, with a ridge of high water-vapour pressure near it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A model adapted from earlier work (Model 1) predicts temperature and moisture nearly uniform after the first minute. The authors judge their new model's drying front more realistic, because evaporation is a local process; in its simplified form, the front is much slower than in Model 1.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The model was fitted to published moisture data; the authors name the lack of physical data as its main drawback.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Gradients.** In a 2013 computational model of a single bean in hot air, validated by experiment, the core was 16 K cooler than the surface after 180 s in one simulation with air at 230 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> That model assumes water diffuses to the surface and evaporates only there.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The two models therefore place evaporation differently: at an internal front or at the surface. Neither has been checked by measuring moisture inside a roasting bean.

**Pressure and expansion.** In the 2017 model, water evaporating inside the cells produces high gas pressure, which makes the cells expand.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2025 study describes the same: vapour release during drying starts the expansion of the inner matrix.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> An SCA handbook adds that this vapour pressure is only released once the temperature is high enough.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Later, carbon dioxide from reactions adds to the gas pressure.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Energy.** Evaporating water absorbs heat: one model uses a latent heat of 2.3 × 10⁶ J/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In a 2025 lumped single-bean model, leaving out evaporation made the model overestimate bean temperature during the exothermic reaction period, and evaporation had a greater effect on the estimate than heat released by reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> This is why the early roast needs a high energy input.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## How much water leaves, and when

**Two kinds of water.** The water evaporated in a roast is partly the green coffee's own and partly water formed by reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2005 fluid-bed study separated them by roasting pre-dried beans (1.1 g water per 100 g, wb) next to normal ones (8.3 g/100 g, wb).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Early in both roasts, only the beans' own water was released.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The reactions that release water and carbon dioxide in relevant amounts started at a bean core temperature of 180–200 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors note that most of the 1.1 g of residual water in the pre-dried beans had to leave before reaction water could be detected.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Shape of the curve.** In that study, moisture fell almost linearly in the fast roast and levelled off only at the end; in the slow roast, the curves were exponential.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A roasting textbook chapter places the highest rate of roast loss in the early stages, mainly from dehydration.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a 350 g gas drum, however, mass was lost at a constant rate for each roasting setting, from 0.6 to 3.0% per min.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In single beans, moisture stayed nearly constant until about 100 °C and then decayed exponentially.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The shape depends on the roaster and the heat applied, so no single curve describes "drying".

**What the water does chemically.** According to a roasting textbook chapter, the speed of key flavour reactions depends on water availability, and some slow down below a critical moisture.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the same chapter, the first roasting stage does not produce large quantities of aroma but may form precursors; most aroma compounds formed fastest at water contents of 7% to 2% (wb).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Keeping colour and time constant, a different early profile can evaporate more or less water and create new combinations of temperature and water activity.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Loss of water also contributes to the brittle texture of the roasted bean.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

## Glass transition and swelling

A glass transition is the change of an amorphous material from rigid ("glassy") to soft ("rubbery"). In green coffee, more water lowered the temperature at which it happens: the transition temperature fell from 48.76 °C to 34.89 °C as water activity rose from 0.155 to 0.512.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> These measurements are of stored coffee, not of beans during a roast.

For the roast itself, the evidence is a textbook account. The cell-wall polysaccharides are described as glassy or rubbery depending on moisture and temperature; in principle the bean passes from glassy to rubbery and back to glassy during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The bean expands while rubbery, so the dehydration kinetics – and thus the profile – help decide how much it swells.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> An SCA handbook places the glass transition around first crack instead.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No measurement of the transition inside a roasting bean was found (see Contested).

## Green coffee moisture and density

**Moisture.** A doctoral thesis on hot-air roasting adjusted green coffee to between 3.2 and 18.2 g/100 g (wb) to study its effect.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> According to a roasting textbook chapter, wetter beans lose more water in the first phase and reach a similar final moisture, so roast loss is higher.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Initial moisture is described as influencing aroma mainly in light roasts; in dark roasts most differences level off.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The authors of a 2025 single-bean model recommend a low initial moisture to make the roast easier to control.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Roast loss as a guide to roast degree is affected too, because green moisture varies from batch to batch.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Density.** The textbook chapter lists green density, moisture, screen size and shape among factors that shape the roast plan.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a trade magazine, practitioners describe giving denser, high-grown coffees a higher charge temperature and more aggressive early heat; one expects denser seeds to hold more water.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> No peer-reviewed study consulted for this entry tested how density changes the drying phase, so these remain practitioner views.

## Drying-phase length: practice and evidence

**What roasters do.** According to a roasting textbook chapter, roasters log the time and probe temperature as the colour changes from green to yellow.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Courses treat the phase from charge to yellowing as a named stage of the profile.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> No peer-reviewed study consulted for this entry set a target length or share of the roast for this phase, so none is given here.

**What studies measured.** In a 2024 study on a 5 kg drum, the drying phase lasted 4.5–5.5 min in six of seven profiles and 8.5 min in the slow-start profile.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> That study measured titratable acidity, not flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**What sensory studies found.** No study consulted for this entry varied drying alone and tested flavour. The closest evidence splits the roast at first crack:

- **Time to first crack.** Across seven sensory studies at constant colour, time to first crack – drying and browning together – significantly affected five of nine attributes; development time affected all nine.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Longer time to first crack went with less acidity, more bitterness and a less clean cup.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Total time.** In the one study that also varied total roast time, total time alone was not correlated with any flavour attribute.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **A slow, cool start.** In a 2019 defect study, the roast called "Underdeveloped" was charged into air at 135 °C instead of 210 °C. It lasted 20:20 instead of 11:25 min, with nearly the same development time (2:30 against 2:40 min).<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The sensory profile did not separate this roast well from the normal roast, though it scored significantly lower in two attributes, caramel and dark berries.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

The studies consulted for this entry therefore give no basis for any particular drying time for flavour. Roast colour remained the stronger predictor of flavour than timing.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Scorching.** According to a roasting textbook chapter, too high a drum speed can press beans against the hot wall and scorch the flat sides.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Steep gradients.** The same chapter describes a possible problem in extremely fast roasting: high heat transfer at the surface may leave the outside over-roasted while the core stays under-roasted, which could give "burnt" and "greenish" notes together.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Batch-to-batch drift.** With fixed air-temperature control, green-coffee moisture, weather and a cold roaster all change how the beans heat.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Varying green moisture also makes roast loss a less reliable guide to roast degree.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Stalling.** A practitioner quoted in a trade magazine warns that the extra water in denser coffee can easily create a stall as the roast approaches first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> No peer-reviewed study of this was found.

## Open questions

- **Measuring moisture inside the bean.** Models disagree on where evaporation happens, and the main model names the lack of physical data as its weakness.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Glass transition during roasting.** Measured only on stored beans; its timing in a roast rests on textbook accounts that differ.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Drying length and flavour.** No sensory study separated the drying phase from the browning phase.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Density.** Its effect on drying is described by practitioners but was not tested in the studies found.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup>
- **What yellowing marks.** Studies use the visible colour change as a boundary<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>, but no study consulted for this entry linked it to a moisture content or a temperature inside the bean.
