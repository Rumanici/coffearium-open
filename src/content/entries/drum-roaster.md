## What a drum roaster is

A drum roaster holds one batch of coffee in a horizontal drum that turns while it is heated. It is the most widespread batch roaster design.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Batch roasters dominate industrial roasting because they are more flexible and easier to control than continuous systems.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The drum does two jobs. It keeps the beans moving so that they heat evenly, and it lets hot air flow through the batch.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Vanes on the inside wall (also called baffles) lift and mix the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Other designs move the beans differently: a fluid-bed roaster keeps them moving with fast air instead of moving parts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> What happens inside the bean during a roast is described in the roasting overview.

**Cooling.** Small drum roasters drop the beans into a cooling tray.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Industrial machines most often use a round-bed cooler with a slowly turning stirrer; the cooling air passes through the bed from below or from above.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Most medium- to large-scale machines also have a water quench. It is optional, helps keep the roast degree the same from batch to batch, and raises bean moisture if overdone.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Its effects on flavour, moisture and staling are covered in the roasting overview.

## How heat reaches the beans

Heat reaches the beans in three ways at once.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Their shares differ from one roaster to another, and they are hard to measure.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

<div data-figure="drum-roaster-heat-paths"></div>
<ol class="fig-legend">
<li data-n="1"><span><b>Burner.</b> In a directly heated drum, the flame heats the drum wall from below.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup></span></li>
<li data-n="2"><span><b>Conduction from the wall.</b> Beans touching the hot wall take up heat directly; with direct heating this share becomes substantial.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Wall-to-bean conduction matters more in drums than in spouted beds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup></span></li>
<li data-n="3"><span><b>Bean bed.</b> The dense bed is carried up the rising side of the drum. In this region, conduction within the bean dominates.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup></span></li>
<li data-n="4"><span><b>Beans in flight.</b> The vanes lift beans, which then fall through the drum.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup></span></li>
<li data-n="5"><span><b>Convection.</b> Hot air flowing through the drum heats the bean surfaces.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For beans in flight, convection dominates.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup></span></li>
<li data-n="6"><span><b>Radiation.</b> Hot surfaces also radiate heat to the beans. A textbook calls this share usually negligible; a 2025 single-bean study in a gas-fired drum concluded that it governs (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup></span></li>
</ol>

- **Conduction.** Heat passes from the hot drum wall to the beans that touch it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Inside the dense bean bed, conduction within the bean dominates.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Convection.** Hot air carries heat to the bean surface.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Beans thrown into the air by the vanes are heated mainly this way.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Radiation.** Hot walls radiate heat to the beans without touching them. How much this adds in a drum is disputed (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

A drum usually works with relatively little air per kilogram of coffee (a low air-to-bean ratio). The upper limit is the air speed at which beans would be blown out of the drum.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A fluid bed relies mainly on convection; a drum may pass a substantial share of the heat by conduction.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Direct and indirect heating

The balance of conduction and convection depends largely on how the drum is heated.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Direct heating.** The furnace is directly under the drum. The wall gets relatively hot, and beans touching it gain a substantial amount of heat by conduction.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Indirect heating.** The drum is insulated, and the hot air does not heat its bottom directly. The air is led to the back of the drum, for more convection inside.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In one multi-study sensory paper, the coffees were roasted on a "flame-on-drum" roaster (1 kg) and, in one study, a "convection-based" roaster (15 kg).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The authors warn that their results may not carry over to other roasters.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Single-wall and double-wall drums.** Roaster makers also describe drums by their wall. Practitioners describe a double-wall drum as two layers of metal with an insulating air layer between them.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> One manufacturer writes that the air layer reduces direct contact heat and the risk of scorching but can slow heat transfer, and puts the emphasis on the quality of the steel.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry compared single-wall and double-wall drums.

## The controls

On small drum roasters, the roaster works mainly with the burner and the trier; drum speed and airflow are adjusted less often.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Burner (gas or power).** It sets the energy going into the drum. Stepless gas controls with a pressure gauge let a roaster note a setting and repeat it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A flame change takes time to change the roasting environment.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In eight sensory studies, mostly on a 1 kg drum, the roasts started with high energy input and only ever reduced it.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Airflow.** It restricts or increases the air speed through the drum and so changes convection almost at once.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Drum speed.** A faster drum tosses the beans up, giving less wall contact and more convection. Too fast a drum presses the beans against the wall instead, which can scorch the flat sides.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Larger machines often run automatically. Conventional control holds a set hot-air temperature by adjusting the burner; more advanced systems follow a stored bean-temperature curve.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> How these choices show up on the roast curve is covered in the roast profile entry.

## Batch size and drum speed

How full the drum is changes how the beans move. In a 2023 study, beans were tracked inside a pilot-scale drum (160 mm across) loaded with 300, 600 or 900 g.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

- **Larger batches** spread the bean bed over a larger area but packed it less densely.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **At a fixed drum speed,** batch size hardly changed the median bean speed, but larger batches made the motion more uniform.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **At high drum speed (78 rpm),** small batches threw beans higher, towards a "cataracting" motion in which beans are flung across the drum.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Conduction dominates in the bed and convection in flight. The authors therefore name batch size and drum speed as two levers for shifting the balance between them.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The study measured bean motion without heating; it did not measure heat transfer or flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## A short history

Two mid-19th-century US patents describe small rotating cylinders for roasting coffee over a stove. A US patent of 1864 shows a hand-turned cylinder over a stove plate. It is perforated, has two inclined vanes that move the beans from end to end, and is emptied by tilting it.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A US patent of 1865 describes a cylinder of wire cloth turned by a clockwork motor over a stove.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Both were small machines for the stove top.

## Heat transfer in a turning drum

**Two regions.** Particle tracking divides the drum into a dense bean bed, with high occupancy and low speed, and a dispersed region of beans in flight.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In flight, convection dominates; in the bed, conduction within the bean dominates.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Beans next to the heated wall also take up heat from the metal, and the authors expect wall-to-bean conduction to matter more in drums than in spouted beds, where the wall is heated only by the air.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Motion regimes.** Granular flow in a rotating drum is described as rolling, cascading or cataracting.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> At 78 rpm, small batches of green and part-roasted coffee moved towards cataracting; for part-roasted coffee, it was also observed in large batches (900 g).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> At 42–56 rpm, batch size did not significantly change how far beans travelled vertically.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> As beans lose density during the roast, the authors suggest that changing drum speed could compensate.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Radiation.** In a 2025 study, single arabica beans were held on a thermocouple at the centre of a gas-fired drum, touching neither wall nor other beans, so conduction was excluded by design.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The calculated convective coefficient was low, 15.91–17.43 W m⁻² °C⁻¹, which the authors attribute to the low air speed created only by the drum's rotation.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The overall coefficient fitted to the measured bean temperatures was 60.20–102.55 W m⁻² °C⁻¹, and the authors concluded that radiation is the dominant mechanism.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A roasting textbook calls radiation in hot-air roasters usually very limited and negligible.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The two settings differ: a lone bean in a drum without forced air flow, and production drums with hot air drawn through a bed of beans.

**Models.** Heat-transfer models of drum roasting simplify the machine in different ways. A 2011 three-dimensional model of a single bean assumed a rotating cylinder roaster under natural convection.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Validated against beans with inserted thermocouples in a drum prototype, it predicted bean-centre temperature with an RMSE of 5.97 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The 2025 single-bean model treats the bean as one temperature and includes evaporative cooling and heat released by reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Neither model follows a full bed of beans in contact with the wall.

## Same bean curve, different machine

When a similar temperature profile was applied on a traditional drum and on a laboratory fluid bed, the coffees had similar physical properties and aroma formation.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In production, the probe reading is a machine-specific mix of bean and air temperature, so the bean curve is not directly visible.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Research drums are sometimes fitted with extra temperature sensors to follow bean temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Process air and off-gas

Smaller roasters usually draw in process air at one end and release the off-gas at the other: an open system.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Because the off-gas is still hot, open systems are not energy efficient.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Most large-scale operations therefore recirculate a major part of the off-gas to the heating unit; the rest leaves the system and may pass an off-gas cleaning step.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Design standards and norms for industrial roasting systems are set out in VDI guideline 3892 of the Association of German Engineers, according to the roasting textbook.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Scorching.** Too high a drum speed can press beans against the hot wall, over-develop their outside and, at worst, scorch the flat sides.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Practitioner names and causes of surface marks are collected in the roast defects entry.
- **Bean breakage.** Any bean movement causes some mechanical stress; a good design keeps breakage low.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Opening the trier.** Each pull of the trier lets cool air into the drum and can change the final stages of the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Over-quenching.** Too much quench water is absorbed, raises the final moisture and speeds up staling.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Carrying air limits.** Above a certain exit air speed, beans are carried away with the air; this caps the air a drum can use.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Share of each heat path in a loaded drum.** No study consulted for this entry measured conduction, convection and radiation separately in a full production batch; the radiation finding comes from a single suspended bean.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Single-wall versus double-wall drums.** Claims come from manufacturers only.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Batch size, drum speed and flavour.** The particle-tracking study measured motion without heat; how batch size and drum speed change heat transfer and the cup is untested in the sources found.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Drum and convection roasters.** The main sensory evidence comes from one flame-on-drum and one convection-based roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Al-Shemmeri et al., 2023 – Bean motion in a rotating drum.</strong> Tracks single beans by positron emission particle tracking in a pilot-scale drum roaster, unheated, at three batch sizes and three drum speeds. Separates a dense bed from beans in flight and shows that batch size and drum speed change the motion regime, which the authors link to the balance of conduction and convection.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Research International 163, 112253 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodres.2022.112253">doi:10.1016/j.foodres.2022.112253</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bustos-Vanegas et al., 2025 – Single-bean heating in a gas-fired drum.</strong> Holds single arabica beans at the centre of a direct gas-fired drum at 200–280 °C and fits a lumped heat-transfer model with evaporation and reaction heat. The fitted overall coefficient far exceeds the calculated convective one, from which the authors conclude that radiation dominates.</p>
<p class="meta">Frontiers in Food Science and Technology 5, 1603783 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/frfst.2025.1603783">doi:10.3389/frfst.2025.1603783</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Fabbri et al., 2011 – A 3D model of the bean in a rotating cylinder.</strong> Builds a finite-element model of heat and moisture transfer in a bean, with a simplified and a 3D-scanned bean shape, for a rotating cylinder roaster under natural convection. Bean-centre temperatures agreed with thermocouple measurements in a drum prototype.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Engineering 105(2), 264–269 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfoodeng.2011.02.030">doi:10.1016/j.jfoodeng.2011.02.030</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Münchow et al., 2020 – Roasting conditions and flavour.</strong> Pools eight sensory studies roasted on a 1 kg flame-on-drum roaster and, once, a 15 kg convection-based roaster. Colour predicted flavour better than timing; the authors caution that results may not transfer to other roasters.</p>
<p class="meta">Beverages 6(2), 29 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/beverages6020029">doi:10.3390/beverages6020029</a></p>
</div>
