## What espresso is

Espresso is brewed by forcing hot water at high pressure through a bed of roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The ground coffee is pressed into a compact cake, the puck.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Water passes through it in well under a minute, and a small, concentrated cup comes out.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to a coffee science textbook, the character of espresso comes from this pressure and from the low ratio of water to coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Several definitions put numbers on this. A definition cited in a 2016 study describes a 25–35 mL beverage made from 7–9 g of coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In it, water at 92–95 °C is forced through at 9–10 atmospheres, and the flow lasts about 20–30 s.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The Italian Espresso National Institute (INEI) sets narrower values for its Certified Italian Espresso. Examples are 7 ± 0.5 g of coffee and 9 ± 1 bar.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2018 specification for traditional Italian espresso, issued with the contact details of COMITCAF (Comitato Italiano del Caffè), Milan, sets limits for espresso made on a professional bar machine from a blend of whole beans.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The definitions do not agree on every value; the Deep dive compares them.

Practice has moved beyond the single 25 mL cup. A textbook chapter notes a parallel trend towards 20 g of coffee for 40 mL of water.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Research shots since 2023 use similar recipes, for example 20 g of coffee to 40 g of beverage.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> How dose, beverage mass, time, temperature and pressure are chosen is covered in espresso recipes and ratios.

## How water passes through the puck

A packed bed of ground coffee resists the flow of water, like a porous filter.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to a coffee science textbook, the pressure on the bed is a balance between the push of the pump and this resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A finer grind leaves smaller gaps between particles, so the pressure rises and the water flows more slowly.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The textbook adds that too tight a bed gives a long shot, which can over-extract.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The bed changes while water flows. In a 2015 study, coffee beds packed down (consolidated) as soon as flow began.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to the textbook, wetted particles swell and rearrange, and the bed's porosity falls during the shot.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A 2024 study found that fine particles, called fines, act mainly by making the bed less permeable.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Even flow matters as much as total flow. In a 2020 study, measured extraction yield fell at very fine grind settings instead of rising.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The authors attribute this to uneven flow through the bed at fine settings.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the textbook, the bed must be even in density and shape to avoid channelling, in which water takes easy paths.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Grinding into the basket, distribution and tamping shape the bed; they are covered in puck preparation. The machine side is in the espresso machine entry.

## What ends up in the cup

Espresso is far stronger than other brews. In a 2019 study of eight methods, two espressos held 5.20% and 8.44% total dissolved solids (TDS).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Filter and immersion brews of the same coffee held 1.35–1.55%.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In a 2023 study, 40 g espressos brewed from 20 g of coffee held 9.68 g of dissolved solids per 100 g, averaged over all its experiments.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The share of the coffee that dissolves is not higher, however. In a 2024 study, a typical espresso dissolved around 20% of the ground coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In a 2013 comparison of nine methods, filter coffee reached 19%.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Espresso is strong mainly because so little water is used per gram of coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The extraction yield and TDS entry explains the arithmetic.

Espresso also carries more than dissolved solids. A 2011 review describes an emulsion of microscopic oil droplets in the liquid, with fragments of cell walls.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> According to the textbook, the pressure pushes oil droplets out of the cells into the cup; paper filters, by contrast, absorb oil efficiently.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> How these phases are felt in the mouth is covered in body and mouthfeel.

## Crema

Crema is the dense brown foam on top of an espresso.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Its gas is mainly carbon dioxide formed during roasting and trapped in the cell structure.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> According to the brewing textbook, pressure is decisive: brew methods without it form no crema.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> One explanation is that the pressure forces carbon dioxide from the grounds into the water.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> When the brew reaches the cup at ambient pressure, the gas comes out of solution as tiny bubbles.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

A review and a textbook chapter state that crema should make up at least 10% of the volume.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> It can last up to 40 minutes.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The COMITCAF specification requires crema that covers the whole surface for at least 120 s.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The textbook chapter calls colour and bubble size only secondary signs of a good extraction, after flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> How crema changes as roasted coffee loses its gas is covered in degassing.

## Origins and milk drinks

According to a coffee historian, the first espresso machines brewed at low pressure, and the coffee tasted like a concentrated drip brew.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> A head of crema came with Achille Gaggia's spring-lever machine, and Gaggia promoted the result as a new beverage.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The espresso machine entry follows this history.

Espresso is also the base of many milk drinks. In early Italian bars, steam wands on the machines warmed milk as well as alcoholic punches, according to the same historian.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The cappuccino grew from this practice into an espresso topped with steamed milk.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> In the United States, the caffè latte developed as an espresso version of café au lait, with textured, steamed milk.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> How much espresso goes into a milk drink varies. In one survey, cappuccinos from three coffee shops held about double the caffeine and caffeoylquinic acids of their espressos.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

The definitions below are reference points, not quality guarantees. The 2013 study that reports the INEI parameters notes that meeting them alone would not fully satisfy quality requirements.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Concentration over the shot

Espresso is not uniform from first drop to last. In the 2023 flow-controlled study, the concentration of each compound fell exponentially as more beverage was collected.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> TDS and trigonelline fell fastest, followed by the chlorogenic acid 5-CQA (5-caffeoylquinic acid) and caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In the 2024 study, shots of under 10–15 s already reached 17–18% extraction, more than 80% of the highest yield measured.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The best flavour for that coffee, judged by one panellist, came at about 30 s and 19–20%.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

So the beverage mass collected dominates. In the 2023 study, flow rate had the strongest effect on the mass of each compound in the cup, but all effects of flow, grind and temperature were minor compared with the effect of beverage mass.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Per cup, an espresso and a lungo held no significantly different total solids in a 2013 comparison.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> What a lungo adds is mostly water.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Physics of the bed

At low flow, a packed bed follows Darcy’s law: flow rate equals permeability × cross-section × pressure drop ÷ (viscosity × bed height), or Q = K·A·ΔP ÷ (μ·L).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The 2015 study applied it in steady state at low particle Reynolds numbers (below 10).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Permeability fitted a Kozeny–Carman equation corrected with a porosity-dependent tortuosity.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Permeability values for packed coffee beds are listed in the espresso machine entry.

Three effects break the simple picture. First, the bed consolidates under flow.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Earlier experiments, cited in the 2015 study, found that raising the pressure above about 5–7 bar lowered the average flow.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Second, the pump does not set the pressure alone: according to the textbook, a pump gives its highest pressure at its lowest flow, and bed permeability decides where on that curve the shot runs.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Third, flow need not be uniform. In the 2020 study, a model that assumed even flow predicted extraction yield falling steadily with coarser grind; measured yields peaked instead, with lower values at both very coarse and very fine settings.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The authors conclude that uneven flow operates at fine settings and wastes coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Pressure also changes what reaches the cup. According to the textbook, aroma compounds cannot evaporate from the bed under pressure and reach the cup in larger amounts; non-volatile compounds are less sensitive to pressure itself.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In the 2016 on-line study, 11 bar raised the extraction of volatile compounds over the whole 25 s shot.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The same study cites earlier work in which higher water temperature gave more total solids, caffeine and lipids in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Crema: formation and stability

A 2011 review describes crema as foam over an oil-in-water emulsion. In it, 90% of the oil droplets are smaller than 10 µm, and cell-wall fragments of 2–5 µm are suspended.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In one study described in the review, foam volume rose from 5.1 to 6.9 mL as water pressure increased.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Water temperature between 88 and 98 °C made no statistical difference to foam volume.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Formation.** One hypothesis, cited in the crema chapter, holds that carbon dioxide dissolved in the hot water under pressure becomes supersaturated in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The 2011 review states that the bubble formation mechanism has not been investigated in detail.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> According to the crema chapter, fine solid particles and cell-wall fragments then act as nucleation sites.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The bubbles rise only a short way in a small cup, which the chapter links to their small size.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The chapter also links crema formation to the bicarbonate in the brew water, whose equilibrium shifts as the pH falls during brewing.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Stabilisation.** The review reports a high correlation between foamability and protein content.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Fractions rich in high-molecular-weight polysaccharides were linked to foam stability, and a melanoidin-proteinaceous fraction to foamability.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> According to the crema chapter, unpublished activity-guided work identified 4-vinylcatechol oligomers as key foam-active compounds in roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> These form from chlorogenic and caffeic acids during roasting, and their level rose with roasting time.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Lipids work the other way. The chapter states that pure arabica espresso contains more lipids than robusta espresso on average, so lipid-induced foam collapse is more likely.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Foam then breaks down by bubble coalescence, gas diffusion from small to large bubbles (Ostwald ripening) and drainage.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Crema and perception.** According to the chapter, crema at first raised the concentration of volatile compounds above the cup compared with the same coffee without crema.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A stable crema, by contrast, held aroma back.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In a consumer study described in the chapter, more crema went with higher expected and perceived smoothness.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The chapter knows of no sensory data on how crema changes the aroma perceived above the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Caffeine.** Not all of the caffeine leaves the bed. In a lab test of double espressos, about 67% of the caffeine reached the cup; over-extraction with more water raised this to over 85%.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Café espressos vary far more than lab shots, because doses and cup sizes vary.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The caffeine entry has a calculator and the health limits.

**Chlorogenic acids.** Espressos had the highest concentrations of caffeine and chlorogenic acids of the eight methods in the 2019 study.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Roast degree also matters: in the lab study, a dark-roast espresso held under a third of the caffeoylquinic acids of a light-roast one.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> A 2013 machine comparison found 38.1–55.6 mg of total chlorogenic acids in a 25 mL cup.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Oil and diterpenes.** Espresso is not filtered through paper, so oil reaches the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Whether this brings many diterpenes is disputed (see Contested). In a 2025 study, four espressos varied from 35.6 to 2,446.7 mg/L cafestol, a variation the authors could not explain.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> A 2020 study found espresso and paper-filtered coffee similar per 50 mL serving and saw no effect of pressure on diterpene extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Small cups mean that concentrations per litre do not translate directly into intake per cup.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **One definition.** INEI, COMITCAF, a review and a cited definition give different temperatures, pressures, times and cup sizes, and research recipes have moved to larger doses.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> No single definition is established.
- **Espresso and sensory maps.** No study consulted for this entry linked espresso strength and extraction yield to sensory preference in the way the brewing control chart does for filter coffee.
- **Uneven extraction.** Uneven flow at fine settings lowered yield and reproducibility in a 2020 study.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry gives a way to measure in a café how evenly a puck was extracted.
- **Crema chemistry.** According to the crema chapter, systematic studies are too scarce to explain crema formation and stability completely.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The key foam-active compounds it names come from unpublished work.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Diterpenes.** Espresso samples ranged widely in cafestol for unexplained reasons, and two studies disagree on how espresso compares with paper-filtered coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Cameron et al., 2020 – Uneven flow in espresso.</strong> Combined a mathematical model of espresso extraction with measured shots. The model, assuming even flow, predicted yield falling with coarser grind; measured yield peaked at an intermediate setting, which the authors attribute to uneven flow at fine settings.</p>
<p class="meta">Matter 2(3), 631–648 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matt.2019.12.019">doi:10.1016/j.matt.2019.12.019</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Smrke et al., 2024 – Fines and extraction dynamics.</strong> Followed extraction over espresso shots of 20 g to 40 g at several grind settings, with and without added fines. Short shots already reached most of the yield, and fines acted mainly on bed permeability.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 14, 5612 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-55831-x">doi:10.1038/s41598-024-55831-x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Schmieder et al., 2023 – Flow, grind and temperature.</strong> Brewed 20 g shots on a flow-controlled machine to 20, 40 and 60 g and measured caffeine, trigonelline, 5-CQA and TDS in fractions. Concentrations fell exponentially through the shot, and beverage mass outweighed flow, grind and temperature.</p>
<p class="meta">Foods 12(15), 2871 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/foods12152871">doi:10.3390/foods12152871</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Angeloni et al., 2019 – Eight methods compared.</strong> Brewed one coffee by eight methods, including two espressos, and measured TDS, viscosity, caffeine and chlorogenic acids. The espressos were the strongest and most viscous and held the highest concentrations of caffeine and chlorogenic acids.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Research International · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodres.2018.10.022">doi:10.1016/j.foodres.2018.10.022</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Illy & Navarini, 2011 – The espresso foam.</strong> Reviewed what is known about crema: its carbon dioxide gas phase, the emulsion and particles below it, and the proteins and polysaccharides linked to its stability. Foam volume rose with brew pressure but not with water temperature in the range studied.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Biophysics 6(3), 335–348 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s11483-011-9220-5">doi:10.1007/s11483-011-9220-5</a></p>
