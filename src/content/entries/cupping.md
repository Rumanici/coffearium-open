brief:
  - Cupping is a way of tasting coffee in which several cups per sample are ground and brewed one by one, so that differences between cups reveal how uniform a lot is.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
  - 'The SCA protocol specifies 8.25 g of coffee per 150 mL of cup capacity, a grind with 70–75% passing an 850 µm sieve, water at 93 ± 3 °C, a crust left for 3–5 minutes, and tasting from about 70 °C as the coffee cools.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>'
  - Since late 2024, the SCA’s official cupping standards are part of the Coffee Value Assessment (CVA). They replace the 2004 cupping protocol and form.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
  - The CVA splits evaluation into four separate assessments – physical, descriptive, affective and extrinsic – so that what a coffee tastes like is recorded apart from how much the taster likes it.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
  - Only the affective assessment produces a score. Eight 9-point ratings are converted to a 100-point scale on which “neither high nor low” in every section gives 79 and “extremely high” gives 100.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## What cupping is and who uses it

Cupping is a method of tasting coffee in which several cups are prepared from each sample. The beans for each cup are weighed, ground and brewed separately.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Each cup therefore holds a different handful of beans. If all cups taste alike, the lot is uniform; if one cup differs, some beans in the lot differ too.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The same logic shows how widespread a defect is: the number of defective cups indicates how common defective beans are in the sample.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Cupping was designed as a simple way to experience a coffee and use what is found to drive purchasing and other decisions.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It is used across the industry to assess the aroma, taste and flavour of brewed coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> For a long time it was largely the work of green coffee traders and roasters.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Today a cupping score features regularly in coffee contracts.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In Cup of Excellence competitions, research cited by the SCA found that higher scores go with higher prices at the green coffee auction.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Q graders are cuppers trained to rate coffee quality with the 100-point cupping scheme.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

In cupping, hot water is poured onto the grounds in each cup and left to infuse<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> – a form of full immersion brewing.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The SCA keeps this traditional set-up partly out of tradition and partly because individual cups are a simple way to test a lot’s uniformity.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Its standard covers detailed cupping for value discovery. Quick cupping of many samples for routine quality control is explicitly outside its scope.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## How a cupping is done

A session usually covers three to six coffees, laid out on one table.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> All coffees on the table are cupped in parallel: step 1 is done for every coffee, then step 2, then step 3.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The SCA protocol specifies:

1. **Sample roast.** Samples are roasted to a “medium” cupping level and air-cooled without water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> They rest for 8–24 hours before cupping.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Roast batches that are underdeveloped, burnt or baked are not used.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
2. **Weighing and grinding.** The dose is 8.25 g of coffee per 150 mL of cup capacity, weighed per cup to within ±0.2 g.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Each cup is ground separately, as close to the cupping as practical.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
3. **Fragrance.** The taster smells the dry grounds of each cup.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
4. **Brewing and aroma.** Water at 93 ± 3 °C is poured to the rim. A crust of grounds forms on top.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The taster smells the undisturbed crust, which stays unbroken for 3–5 minutes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
5. **Breaking the crust.** The crust is stirred three times while the released vapour is smelled. Both smells together make up the aroma.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
6. **Skimming.** Floating grounds, foam and oils are lifted off with one or two spoons and discarded.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> From the break onwards, spoons are rinsed in hot water before touching any cup.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
7. **Liquoring.** Once the brew has cooled to about 70 °C, the taster slurps a spoonful so that it covers the tongue and palate, then spits it out to avoid taking in too much caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> At least three rounds follow as the coffee cools below body temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

During liquoring the taster assesses flavour, aftertaste, acidity, sweetness and mouthfeel, and finally the coffee overall.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Flavour combines taste with retronasal smell – odour that reaches the nose from the back of the mouth.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Fragrance and aroma are orthonasal: smelled through the nose.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## The Coffee Value Assessment

In 2024 the SCA replaced its 2004 cupping protocol and form with the Coffee Value Assessment (CVA).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Three standards were adopted: 102-2024 for sample preparation and tasting mechanics, 103-2024 for the descriptive assessment and 104-2024 for the affective assessment.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The CVA has four separate assessments.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

- **Physical.** A record of the green coffee: colour, physical defects, moisture and bean size.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It uses a 350 g sample and still applies only to washed Arabicas.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It is still in research and development, and its form is an early (alpha) version.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Descriptive.** What the coffee tastes like: the intensity of each section and the characteristics found, recorded as objectively as possible.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Affective.** How much the taster likes the coffee, or how well it fits a known market. This is the only part that produces a score.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Extrinsic.** Information about the coffee that cannot be tasted – origin, producer, processing, trade, certifications – collected only after the other assessments are complete.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The reason for the split is bias. Tasters can be swayed by extrinsic information, so the SCA recommends completing each assessment separately.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Physical results are to be hidden from tasters until the sensory work is done.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Where separate cuppings are not practical, the descriptive and affective assessments may be combined section by section on one form, with some loss of resolution.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The SCA also defines specialty coffee differently now: as a coffee recognised for its distinctive attributes and, because of them, worth significantly more in the marketplace.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Describing and scoring

**Descriptive assessment.** The taster rates the total intensity of seven sections – fragrance, aroma, flavour, aftertaste, acidity, sweetness and mouthfeel – on 15-point scales.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It is the total that is rated, not the strength of a single note.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Characteristics are ticked on check-all-that-apply (CATA) lists. Up to five descriptors are chosen for fragrance and aroma, up to five for flavour and aftertaste, up to two main tastes and up to two mouthfeel terms.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The lists draw on the inner and middle rings of the Coffee Taster’s Flavor Wheel.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Acidity and sweetness are described in free words only.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Before tasting, the roast level is estimated by eye and recorded.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Affective assessment.** The taster rates eight sections – fragrance, aroma, flavour, aftertaste, acidity, sweetness, mouthfeel and overall – on a 9-point “impression of quality” scale.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The scale runs from 1, “extremely low”, through 5, “neither high nor low”, to 9, “extremely high”.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Impression of quality is a judgement of how distinctive and desirable a section is. It may reflect the taster’s own preference or a market preference the taster knows.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Two more fields record problems. **Non-uniform cups** are cups that differ in kind from the others – a distinct characteristic present or absent – not merely stronger or weaker; differences in intensity can come from brewing mistakes and are not penalised.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> **Defective cups** carry one of three named sensory defects: potato, mouldy or phenolic. The cupper must mark both the defective cups and the defect type; if either is missing, the coffee is not counted as defective.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Every defective cup is also marked non-uniform, unless the coffee is evenly defective in all cups.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**The score.** The standard specifies a linear equation. Its main input is the sum of the eight ratings. All sections at 5 give 79; all at 9 give 100.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Two points are deducted for each non-uniform cup and four for each defective cup.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The result is rounded to the nearest 0.25.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The form deliberately has no space to add the score up: the SCA wants to prevent “reverse cupping”, in which a taster picks a total first and fills in the sections to match.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A two-way table or software converts the sum into the score.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## What changed from the 2004 form

The 2004 form mixed different kinds of sensory test – descriptive, affective and simple yes/no tests – on one sheet.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In a survey of the form’s users, about half thought it mainly subjective and half mainly objective.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The CVA separates description from preference.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> For cuppers used to the old form, the SCA lists these changes:<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

- Description is now its own assessment; the notes on the affective form are for affective comments.
- Balance and clean cup are gone. Balance is rated within overall; a lack of cleanliness can be marked down in flavour or other sections.
- Sweetness is rated on a scale.
- Each section uses the 9-point scale instead of the old range of 6–10, which started at “good”.
- The score is no longer calculated on the form.

The CVA also narrows sensory defects to potato, mouldy and phenolic. Some notes once treated as defects, such as overripe notes or isovaleric acid, have become controversial as natural and honey processing and more intense fermentations reached specialty markets.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Sensory science behind the split

Sensory science distinguishes analytical measurements, such as the intensity of a taste, from value judgements, such as preference or acceptability.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The first can be trained against references and is treated as objective; the second is subjective.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The SCA regards separating the two as best practice in sensory and consumer science.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Its CVA work, with World Coffee Research and the Coffee Science Foundation, found in 2021–2022 that cuppers could act as consistent descriptive panelists, but also found two distinct preference clusters among them.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**The descriptive side.** Classic descriptive methods rate the intensity of attributes on 15-point scales; the CVA adopted the same scale for compatibility.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> CATA is a rapid profiling technique: tasters only tick the attributes present and do not rate their intensity, so less training is needed.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The descriptors behind the lists come from the Coffee Taster’s Flavor Wheel, which in turn is built on the World Coffee Research Sensory Lexicon.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> For each attribute, the lexicon gives references that serve as the standard against which it is measured.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The 2016 wheel was designed by having 72 experts sort the lexicon’s attributes, then grouping them with hierarchical clustering and placing the groups with multidimensional scaling.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**The affective side.** The 9-point hedonic scale was developed at the US Quartermaster Food and Container Institute by Peryam and Pilgrim to measure soldiers’ food preferences.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> It is the most common scale for consumer acceptance.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The SCA calls the 9-point scale the most commonly used affective measurement in food science and adopted its format.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Its labels, however, speak of impression of quality rather than liking.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The SCA says a taster may rate for a market they know as well as for themselves – an idea it calls inter-subjectivity.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**The uniformity box.** Uniformity is a small discriminative test inside the affective form. The SCA notes that this is not ideal from a sensory-science point of view, but keeps it because individual cups make it possible.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Formal difference tests are a separate family of methods; the triangle test in ISO 4120, for example, is a forced-choice procedure for deciding whether two products differ perceptibly.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## How reliable is cupping?

Studies point in different directions, depending on what is asked of the cuppers.

- **Description.** In a study of 13 Colombian coffees, four certified cuppers using the SCAA protocol were compared with a six-person trained descriptive panel.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The two methods overlapped little.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Cuppers’ tasting notes agreed poorly on terms, and the authors concluded that cupping cannot replace descriptive analysis for profiling coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A later review, describing the same comparison of 13 Colombian coffees, reads it as showing that cuppers were reasonably consistent in grading quality but highly variable in describing flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Scoring.** A study of Q graders found that the tasters had full capacity of evaluation, although their perceptions of the attributes varied. Tasting in the morning or afternoon did not, on its own, affect the results; combined with interaction between tasters, it produced distortions, and the authors called for better evaluation technique.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Process.** In a 2026 validation study, the authors observed that Q graders usually arrive at an overall score first and then fill in the categories to match.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> They also note the long-recognised weakness of mixing intensity, quality and presence of attributes on one form.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The CVA’s missing score line targets the same habit.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Experts versus consumers.** In a blind test with 205 naïve consumers, preferences were split equally between a high- and a low-graded coffee, and consumers could not identify the more expensive one better than chance.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The authors conclude that industry grading may correlate poorly with how consumers experience coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Calibration or alignment.** In sensory science, calibration means training panelists to use defined references in a specific test.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The SCA argues that the cupping room’s “calibration” is better called alignment.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Descriptive results should align through shared use of scales and descriptors. Affective results may align within a supply chain, but across very different markets the SCA advises agreeing on the description and “agreeing to disagree” on preference.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In its view, a good affective cupper is consistent with their own scores over time, not necessarily close to other cuppers.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## The “80+ = specialty” convention

A score of more than 80 is widely treated as the line for specialty coffee. A 2026 study states the convention plainly.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> An ICO compilation of specialty definitions includes one, attributed to the Coffee Quality Institute, that requires a score of over 80 points, and a Rwandan standard that sets specialty grade for fully washed Arabica at 80–90 points.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The original SCAA protocol was designed to distinguish specialty-grade from commercial coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> None of the SCA documents consulted for this entry, however, states where the 80-point line was first set, and its origin is therefore not given here.

Under the 2004 form most coffees scored between 80 and 88, which the SCA calls too narrow for a 100-point scale.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The CVA formula was designed to use more of the scale.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The SCA itself now defines specialty by attributes and value, not by a single score.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It notes that a 90 still says the assessor appreciated a coffee more than an 80, and expects future contracts to add descriptive and extrinsic assessments to the score.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## A short history

By the SCA’s account, tasting was added to coffee grading in the 19th century, when it showed that some small beans thought inferior had better flavour than larger ones.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> “Cup testing”, as it was first called, predates sensory science.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Ted Lingle’s *Coffee Cupper’s Handbook* later brought sensory-science ideas and a systematic approach into it.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The SCAA cupping form took shape in 1999, inspired by the 100-point scores used for wine, and settled five years later into the 2004 protocol.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> It was designed to separate specialty-grade from commercial coffee and leaned on ideas from green grading.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The SCA began reviewing it in 2020; a survey of more than 1,500 users was part of that review.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Alpha and beta versions of the new system were piloted with groups of cuppers and an early-adopter programme of nearly 800 individuals and businesses.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A provisional version followed in June 2024, and the SCA adopted Standards 102, 103 and 104 later that year.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## The score formula

The equation in Standard 104-2024 is printed as a graphic. Its text states the inputs (the eight section ratings, non-uniform cups and defective cups), the two anchor points, the deductions and the rounding.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A straight line through the two anchors gives:

**S = 52.75 + 0.65625 × Σ − 2u − 4d**, rounded to the nearest 0.25,

where Σ is the sum of the eight ratings (8–72), u the number of non-uniform cups and d the number of defective cups. This is our own derivation from the published anchors. Rounding halves upwards, it reproduces every row of the SCA’s two-way table, from a sum of 8 (58.00) to a sum of 72 (100.00).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Adoption.** The SCA expected the two systems to coexist for a time, and 2004 scores can still appear in contracts.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> No independent survey of how widely the CVA has been adopted was found.
- **Comparability.** The SCA does not expect old and new scores to correlate linearly.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2026 study calls for comparing the CVA with other methods.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Until such data exist, a 2004 score and a CVA score should not be treated as equivalent.
- **Physical assessment.** The SCA says new research is needed on how green-bean defects affect cup quality, and its physical assessment still covers only washed Arabicas.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Sweetness.** The SCA notes that research is under way to define coffee sweetness and to establish whether it is a taste or a cross-modal effect of sweet-smelling compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Other species.** The CVA has not been tested for species other than Arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

<p><strong>Di Donfrancesco et al., 2014 – Cupping versus descriptive analysis.</strong> Thirteen Colombian coffees were profiled by a trained descriptive panel and scored by certified cuppers using the SCAA protocol.
