## What “digestion” covers here

Coffee meets the whole digestive tract on its way through the body. Studies have looked at the oesophagus, the stomach, the gallbladder, the colon and the bacteria living in the gut. This entry sorts what was measured in people from what is only an association or a laboratory mechanism.

The difference matters. An observational study compares people who drink coffee with people who do not; it shows an association, not a cause.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A trial gives coffee or a comparison drink and measures the response.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Cell studies show how a compound can act, not what a cup does in a person.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> How caffeine itself is absorbed and broken down is covered in *Caffeine*; the liver belongs to *Coffee and long-term health*.

## The stomach: acid and gastrin

Coffee makes the stomach produce more acid. In a 1975 study in healthy people, regular coffee and decaffeinated coffee gave similar peak acid outputs.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Pure caffeine, at the amount found in a cup, gave less than half that of regular coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> So caffeine is not the main driver.

Gastrin is a hormone that tells the stomach to release acid. In a 1986 study, both regular and decaffeinated coffee raised gastrin in healthy volunteers.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The authors concluded that decaffeination removes part of this effect, not all of it.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Which compounds are responsible is less clear. In a 2012 study on cultured human stomach cells, caffeine increased signs of acid secretion. N-methylpyridinium, a compound found in larger amounts in a dark roast blend than in a medium roast blend<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>, reduced them.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> That is a laboratory mechanism, not a measurement in people.

## Heartburn and reflux

Heartburn is a burning feeling caused when stomach contents flow back into the oesophagus. Frequent reflux is called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD; GERD in US spelling).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

A 2026 meta-analysis pooled 40 studies with 122,074 people. Reflux disease was reported in 34.9% of coffee drinkers and 30.7% of non-drinkers.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The authors call this a small association of unclear clinical significance. They found no link with Barrett’s oesophagus, a change in the oesophageal lining.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The studies differed a lot, and association is not causation.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The abstract gives no amounts, such as cups a day.

The authors conclude that advising everyone with reflux to avoid coffee needs further evaluation.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Individual triggers are a matter for a doctor.

## The colon and the urge to go

Many people feel an urge to open their bowels after coffee. In a 1990 questionnaire of 99 healthy volunteers aged 17–27, 29% said coffee caused it.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In 14 healthy subjects, pressure sensors then measured the lower colon. Activity rose within four minutes of coffee in those who reported the effect, and not in the others.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Decaf did the same in the responders; hot water did nothing.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

A 1998 study measured colonic activity in 12 healthy people. Caffeinated coffee stimulated it about as much as a 1,000 kcal meal.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The effect was 60% stronger than water and 23% stronger than decaf.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The effect has been tested after abdominal surgery, when the bowel can be slow to restart.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In a 2018 meta-analysis of six randomised trials after caesarean, colorectal or gynaecological cancer surgery, coffee shortened the time to the first bowel movement by about 10 h.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## The gallbladder and gallstones

The gallbladder stores bile and squeezes it into the gut after a meal. In a 1990 study of six healthy coffee drinkers, 165 mL of coffee made it contract by about a third.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Decaf did much the same.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Gallstones are less common among coffee drinkers in observational studies.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Whether coffee prevents them is disputed. Two genetic studies, which test cause more directly, reached opposite conclusions (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Gut microbes and the bowel

The bacteria living in the gut are called the gut microbiota. A 2024 review found that most human studies reported shifts in some bacterial groups with coffee. It also stresses many conflicting results.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

The clearest signal so far comes from a 2024 study of 22,867 people in the US and the UK. One bacterium, *Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus*, was linked to coffee drinking, and the link was highly reproducible across populations.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> What that means for health is not known from the sources consulted.

For irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), a common disorder of bowel habit and pain, evidence is thin and observational. See Deep dive.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## “Low-acid” and “stomach-friendly” coffee

Some coffees are sold as low-acid or gentle on the stomach. Two different things are mixed up in such claims. One is the acidity of the drink, measured as pH or titratable acidity. The other is how much acid the stomach itself makes.

The studies above measured the second. Decaf raised the stomach’s acid output almost as much as regular coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Compounds such as caffeine and N-methylpyridinium acted on stomach cells in the laboratory.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry tested whether a coffee with a higher pH causes less heartburn.

Cold brew is often called low-acid. One study found hot and cold brews of the same light roasts had similar pH, but the hot brews held more titratable acid.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Other studies disagree on pH; see *Cold brew* and *Acids in coffee*.

## Gastric secretion in detail

The 1975 study compared dose responses of caffeine, regular coffee and decaffeinated coffee in normal subjects.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Peak acid output was 20.9 ± 3.6 mEq/h for regular coffee, 16.5 ± 2.6 mEq/h for decaf and 8.4 ± 1.3 mEq/h for caffeine, compared on a cup-equivalent basis.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The abstract does not give the number of subjects.

The same study measured pressure in the lower oesophageal sphincter, the muscle ring that keeps stomach contents down. Caffeine changed it little; regular and decaffeinated coffee both raised it.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The authors warned that advice based on caffeine’s known effects may bear little relation to what coffee actually does.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

In the 1986 gastrin study, 10 healthy volunteers first drank 50 mL of coffee solution, equal to one home-made cup.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the comparison of 100 mL servings, integrated gastrin output was 2.3 times the control after regular coffee and 1.7 times after decaf.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The 2012 cell study used the human gastric cell line HGT-1. It tested six coffee components alone and in mixtures: chlorogenic acid, caffeine, pyrogallol, catechol, N-alkanoyl-5-hydroxytryptamides (C5HTs) and N-methylpyridinium.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> N-methylpyridinium reduced markers of acid secretion, including expression of the proton pump.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Chlorogenic acids and their fate in roasting are covered in *Chlorogenic acids*; the wider set of coffee compounds in *Bioactive compounds*.

## Dark roast claims

Dark roasts are sometimes marketed as easier on the stomach. The main human study behind this is small. In 2014, nine healthy volunteers drank 200 mL of a dark roast blend or a medium roast market blend.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The dark roast held more N-methylpyridinium (87 against 29 mg/L) and less C5HT, chlorogenic acids and trigonelline; caffeine was similar.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Intragastric pH showed less acid secretion after the dark roast.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

A coffee company is among the study’s author affiliations, and the authors themselves call for further studies.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> No independent replication was found for this entry. The claim is therefore not established.

## Reflux evidence in detail

The 2026 meta-analysis included 40 studies: 85,400 coffee drinkers and 36,674 non-drinkers.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The pooled odds ratio for reflux disease was 1.18 (95% CI 1.03–1.36), with high heterogeneity between studies (I² = 89.38%).<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> For Barrett’s oesophagus it was 1.13 (0.79–1.61), not significant.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The studies compared drinkers with non-drinkers, so they show an association, not a cause.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

The 1975 finding that coffee raised sphincter pressure points the other way from a simple “coffee relaxes the sphincter” story.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry settles the mechanism.

## Colonic motility in detail

The 1998 study used ambulatory colonic manometry: a six-sensor probe placed up to the middle of the transverse colon in 12 healthy subjects.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Over 10 h, each subject received 240 mL of three drinks at 45 °C in random order – black Colombian coffee with 150 mg caffeine, decaffeinated coffee and water – and a 1,000 kcal meal.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The response was greater in the transverse and descending colon than in the rectosigmoid colon.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The abstract reports that decaf, like caffeinated coffee and the meal, induced more colonic activity than water, but also that the effects of decaf were not significantly different from those of water or caffeinated coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In the 1990 study, motility rose within 4 min after regular and decaffeinated coffee in eight responders and lasted at least 30 min.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Six non-responders showed no rise.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Both studies are small, in healthy young or adult volunteers.

The surgery meta-analysis pooled six randomised trials with 601 participants.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Coffee shortened time to first defecation by 9.98 h (95% CI 2.99–16.97) and time to first flatus by 7.14 h.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Hospital stay fell by 0.74 days.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> All the trials had a high risk of performance bias, because patients could not be blinded.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The meta-analysis does not report how much coffee was given.

## Gallstones in detail

The 2022 meta-analysis pooled seven longitudinal observational studies from America and Europe with 333,773 participants.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Coffee drinking was associated with a lower risk of gallstone disease (RR 0.86, 0.79–0.93), and each extra cup a day with 0.95 (0.94–0.96).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The association held for caffeinated coffee (0.84) but not decaf (1.00). It was significant in women but not in men, although the two did not differ significantly.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The same paper’s Mendelian randomisation, which uses genes linked to coffee intake as a natural experiment, found no causal association.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

A 2020 study by Danish researchers followed 104,493 people from the general population for a mean of 8 years.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Those drinking more than six cups a day had a 23% lower risk of symptomatic gallstone disease (HR 0.77, 0.61–0.94).<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Its genetic analysis in 114,220 people gave an odds ratio of 0.89 (0.83–0.95) per extra cup a day.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The authors read this as a causal effect; the 2022 analysis did not confirm it.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Microbiota and IBS in detail

The 2024 multi-cohort study combined detailed diet data from 22,867 participants with public data from 54,198 people in 211 cohorts.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The coffee–microbiome link was highly reproducible across populations.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Plasma from 438 samples showed quinic acid and related compounds linked to both coffee and *L. asaccharolyticus*, and coffee stimulated the bacterium’s growth in vitro.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> These are associations and laboratory growth tests, not a trial.

The 2024 review found that most human faecal studies reported more Firmicutes and Actinobacteria, and several more *Bifidobacterium*, with coffee; one study found fewer Firmicutes.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

For IBS, a 2023 meta-analysis of eight studies with 432,022 participants found lower odds of IBS among coffee drinkers (pooled OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.80–0.88; the abstract prints the interval as 0.80 to 0.84).<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Most of the studies were cross-sectional, six from Asia, one from the UK and one from Africa; they compared any coffee intake with none, without a dose–response analysis.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The authors call for prospective cohort studies that track new cases in healthy people.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The 2024 review notes a patient survey linking caffeine with symptoms such as diarrhoea, and concludes that controlled studies are needed.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Reflux mechanism.** Coffee raised sphincter pressure in one 1975 study<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>, yet reflux is slightly more common in drinkers.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> No source consulted here explains the difference.
- **Low-acid labels.** No study consulted for this entry linked the pH or titratable acidity of a coffee to heartburn or gastric acid output in people.
- **Dark roast.** The human evidence is one nine-person study with an industry affiliation.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Gallstones.** Observational studies agree on a lower risk; the genetic studies disagree on cause.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Microbes and health.** Coffee drinkers carry different gut bacteria<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>, but what that does for health is not established in the sources consulted.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Cohen & Booth, 1975 – Coffee, decaf and caffeine in the stomach.</strong> Compared dose responses of caffeine, regular coffee and decaffeinated coffee for gastric acid output and sphincter pressure. Both coffees stimulated more acid than caffeine alone, and both raised sphincter pressure while caffeine barely did.</p>
<p class="meta">New England Journal of Medicine 293(18), 897–899 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM197510302931803">doi:10.1056/NEJM197510302931803</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Rao et al., 1998 – Is coffee a colonic stimulant?</strong> Measured colonic pressure over a day in 12 healthy people given coffee, decaf, water and a large meal. Caffeinated coffee stimulated the colon about as much as the meal and clearly more than water.</p>
<p class="meta">European Journal of Gastroenterology & Hepatology 10(2), 113–118 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1097/00042737-199802000-00003">doi:10.1097/00042737-199802000-00003</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Muftah et al., 2026 – Coffee and reflux disease.</strong> Pooled 40 studies with more than 122,000 people. Reflux disease was slightly more common in coffee drinkers, Barrett’s oesophagus was not, and the authors question routine advice to avoid coffee.</p>
<p class="meta">Clinical and Translational Gastroenterology 17(4), e00996 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.14309/ctg.0000000000000996">doi:10.14309/ctg.0000000000000996</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bai et al., 2022 – Gallstones, association and cause.</strong> Combined a dose–response meta-analysis of cohort studies with a Mendelian randomisation analysis. Coffee drinking was associated with fewer gallstones, but the genetic analysis found no causal link.</p>
<p class="meta">Frontiers in Nutrition 9, 940689 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.940689">doi:10.3389/fnut.2022.940689</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Manghi et al., 2024 – Coffee and one gut bacterium.</strong> Analysed gut metagenomes and diet in tens of thousands of people across many cohorts. Coffee drinking was consistently linked with *Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus*, which coffee also helped grow in the laboratory.</p>
<p class="meta">Nature Microbiology · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-024-01858-9">doi:10.1038/s41564-024-01858-9</a></p>
</div>
