## Two ways to sell by bidding

Much green coffee is priced against a futures market: the futures price plus a premium or discount for the lot (see How a coffee price is made and Differentials). An auction sets the price another way. Buyers bid for each lot, and the highest bid wins.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> A lot is a batch of coffee from one origin and process, traded as a unit.

Two kinds of auction matter in coffee. National auctions sell ordinary export coffee lot by lot, under national law.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Quality competitions select a few dozen top lots by blind cupping and then sell them in one online auction.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## National auctions in East Africa

**Kenya.** The Nairobi Coffee Exchange is a spot market that has existed since the early 1930s, according to the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Under the 2020 regulations, a coffee exchange is licensed by Kenya’s Capital Markets Authority to trade clean coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The exchange keeps a central sample room, and buyers and roasters receive offer samples before each auction.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Each lot is listed in a sales catalogue, and the grower or the grower’s agent sets its reserve price.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Only licensed persons take part on the trading floor: brokers sell for growers, and licensed buyers and roasters buy.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Growers may also sell directly to buyers abroad; those contracts are registered with the Coffee Directorate.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Ethiopia.** The Ethiopia Commodity Exchange (ECX) opened in 2008.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In the same year, trading coffee through the ECX became mandatory for everyone except cooperatives and large plantations.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> One side effect, according to a 2021 policy report, was that the origin of individual lots could no longer be traced.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In 2017, new laws let washing stations, mills and larger farms export directly.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Grading at the ECX is described in Green coffee grading.

**Tanzania.** Under the 2013 regulations, the Tanzania Coffee Board holds coffee auctions at the Moshi Coffee Auction or another place it approves.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Lots are offered in catalogues prepared by the Board, and coffee exporters or their agents attend and bid.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The Board may allow an applicant to export without going through the auction.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Quality competitions

The Cup of Excellence began in Brazil in 1999, according to its organiser, the Alliance for Coffee Excellence (ACE).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The organiser names the International Coffee Organization among its first funders.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A coffee textbook chapter describes a specialty coffee promotion programme of that organisation whose promotion of Brazilian coffees hinged on a cupping competition followed at once by an internet auction.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> The competition is open to every producer in the host country, and the first sample is entered free.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Two juries cup the samples blind, without knowing the producer, farm, variety or place.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A coffee must score 86 points or more out of 100 to advance from round to round.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The top 30 coffees go to the auction if they score 87 or more.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> About six weeks after the international jury meets, the winning lots are sold online on the ACE website.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The rules say that producers receive the majority of the auction proceeds.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Other programmes work in a similar way. As of October 2026, ACE lists competitions for 2026 in Brazil, Costa Rica, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Indonesia, Mexico, Nicaragua, Peru and Thailand.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Its archive also holds results for Ethiopia from 2020 and for a “Best of Taiwan” in 2023.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Best of Panama is run separately by the Specialty Coffee Association of Panama, with its own online auction.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> A textbook chapter credits a Geisha coffee from Panama, sold for record prices in an internet auction, with prompting producers elsewhere to plant the variety.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

## What the prices show

Competition prices are far above those of commodity coffee, and they vary widely within one auction.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the Honduras Cup of Excellence of 2005, the top lot sold for USD 17.60 per lb and the median lot for USD 1.60.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In 2025, the top lot reached USD 88.40 per lb and the median lot USD 21.60.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> All prices here are in nominal US dollars per pound of green coffee, as the organiser lists them.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The top lot says little about the typical lot. In Brazil in 2023, the highest price was USD 130.30 per lb, about USD 287 per kg (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The median of the 30 Cup of Excellence lots was about USD 12.30 per lb (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In the Brazil Pulped Naturals auction of 2005, the winner sold for USD 49.75 per lb and the second lot for USD 7.80.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Record prices for single lots at Best of Panama are widely reported in the press. No results page of the organiser could be accessed for this entry, so no figure is given here.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

## Ethiopia: from the ECX mandate to the 2017 reform

According to a 2021 report of the International Institute for Environment and Development (IIED), the ECX was launched to improve agricultural marketing.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> When coffee trading through the exchange became mandatory is dated differently (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The IIED report names the loss of traceability of individual lots as a side effect of the mandate.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The same report finds that the cooperatives’ share of coffee exports did not rise much after the mandate.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The 2017 reform was enacted through Proclamations 1050/2017 and 1051/2017.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Proclamation 1051/2017 was published in the Federal Negarit Gazette on 5 June 2017.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A USDA report of May 2018 states that it allowed washing stations, mills and farmers with more than 2 ha of coffee (at least 1 t/ha) to export directly without the ECX.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Before that change, commercial farms and cooperative unions could already export directly.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The IIED report describes the result as traceability restored through “vertical integration”.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> According to the FAOLEX record, buying or selling coffee outside the ECX or other transaction centres set up by the authority remains an offence under Proclamation 1051/2017; the record lists the law as not repealed.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Later amendments, if any, were not consulted for this entry.

Whether the exchange linked Ethiopian prices more closely to world prices is not settled. According to a 2015 conference paper, not all coffee regions had become more connected to world markets in the first years after the mandate.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Cup of Excellence rules in detail

The rules page describes six rounds of cupping.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Samples with major defects are eliminated at once.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

| Round | Jury | Rule as published | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (pre-selection) | National Jury | Each sample cupped once; 86+ advance, at most 150 samples | <sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| After round 1 | – | Whole lot moved to a bonded warehouse under an independent auditing company | <sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| 2 | National Jury | 86+ again, at most 90 samples advance | <sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| 3 | National Jury | At most 40 samples advance | <sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| 4 | International Jury | All samples passed forward are cupped | <sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| 5 | International Jury | Up to 30 samples scoring over 87 are cupped again | <sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| 6 | International Jury | Top ten cupped again for final score and ranking | <sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Awards | – | Over 90: Presidential Award; top three split into lots a and b if 90+ | <sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Auction | – | Online on the ACE website, about 6 weeks after the International Jury week | <sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| National Winner | – | National Jury 86+, International Jury below 87; programme since 2016 | <sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> |

The page also keeps an older wording. It speaks of up to 35 judges and of a minimum of 87 points in the final round.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The thresholds were not always this high. In the Honduras auctions of 2005 and 2008, lots that scored below 85 were sold, and in 2012, 2015 and 2018 lots below 87.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> When each threshold changed was not stated on the pages consulted.

## What studies find about quality premiums

A peer-reviewed analysis of Cup of Excellence e-auctions in Central and South America found that clearing prices depend on sensory traits, a third-party quality ranking, country of origin, variety and lot size, and that they were well above commodity prices.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In its working-paper version, larger lots sold for less per pound, first place carried the largest premium, and specialty prices moved with the commodity price.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The size of these premiums is not given here: the journal text was not open for this entry, and the working paper alone carries no number.

A 2018 peer-reviewed study of the same programme separated “material” attributes, such as cupping notes, from “symbolic” ones, such as origin, variety, processing method and certification. Symbolic attributes explained more of the scores than material ones, and prices were mainly determined by symbolic attributes. Prices also depended on auction conditions such as the number of coffees offered, and first place carried the largest price premium.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> A later working paper by one of its authors found that, for some ranks, a higher score did not raise the price significantly; it ties the rank premium to how buyers read rankings.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

No study consulted for this entry measured prices on the Nairobi or Moshi auctions against quality, or how much of an auction price reaches the producer in Kenya or Tanzania. Shares of value along the chain are treated in Who earns what. No study by Teuber on competition prices was consulted for this entry.

## Open questions

- **Spill-over to non-winning lots.** No study consulted here measured whether a competition raises prices for a region’s ordinary lots in later years.
- **Real price trend.** The Honduras series is nominal; an inflation-adjusted series, or one set against the futures price at each auction date, was not found in the sources consulted.
- **Payment speed and producer share.** The rules give payment deadlines but not the share of the final price the grower receives at each national auction.
- **ECX after 2017.** Later amendments to Proclamation 1051/2017 and the volume still traded through the ECX were not covered by the sources consulted.

<p><strong>Donnet, Weatherspoon and Hoehn, 2008 – Price determinants in e-auctioned specialty coffees.</strong> A hedonic price analysis of Cup of Excellence e-auctions in Central and South America. Clearing prices depended on sensory traits and on reputation, such as the competition rank, and were far above commodity prices.</p>
<p class="meta">Agricultural Economics 38(3), 267–276 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1574-0862.2008.00298.x">doi:10.1111/j.1574-0862.2008.00298.x</a></p>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Traore, Wilson and Fields, 2018 – Quality scores and prices in the Cup of Excellence.</strong> Asked which attributes explain both the jury score and the auction price. Symbolic attributes, such as origin and variety, carried more weight than material ones, and the number of coffees in the auction mattered; cuppers and buyers preferred fruity, floral, sweet, spice and sour-acid notes.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Applied Economics 50(3), 349–368 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1017/aae.2018.5">doi:10.1017/aae.2018.5</a></p>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Wilson, undated working paper – When higher quality does not raise the price.</strong> Used Cup of Excellence auction lots from several countries. Rank had a larger effect on price than score, and within some ranks the score had no significant effect.</p>
