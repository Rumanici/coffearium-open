## What sets these roasters apart

Every roaster must keep the beans moving, so that heat from the hot air reaches them evenly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The most widespread batch design is a horizontal drum that turns.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A fluid-bed roaster has no moving parts and lifts the beans with fast air instead.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Tangential and centrifugal roasters are two other answers to the same task. A roasting textbook chapter lists them, with the drum and the fluid bed, under industrial roasting equipment.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2000 doctoral thesis names the same four designs as the systems most commonly used.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

In every hot-air roaster, heat travels by convection, conduction and radiation at once.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Convection carries heat from the air to the bean surface. Conduction carries it from hot walls to the beans touching them.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The designs differ in how much air they can use, and so in the balance between the two.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Tangential (paddle) roasters

The chamber stays still. Inside, a rotating shaft with paddles stirs the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The thesis calls this design a vertical fixed drum with rotating mixing elements.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Hot air enters in the lower part of the chamber. It often comes in tangentially, along the curve of the half-cylindrical wall.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The air then rises through the beans into a wide expansion chamber above.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> There it slows down so much that no beans are carried away, even with a lot of air.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Because the paddles move the beans, their movement depends little on the air flow.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A trade-press account of the design's origin gives this independence from the air flow as its original aim.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> At the end of the roast, a gate at the bottom opens and the beans fall into the cooler.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Centrifugal (rotating-bowl) roasters

In this design a bowl turns about a vertical axis. Centrifugal force pushes the beans to the rim.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> At the rim, fixed guide baffles send them back to the centre in a spiral path.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Hot air flows down a vertical shaft along the axis of rotation. It enters at the bottom of the bowl, turns upwards through the beans and leaves on top.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> To empty it, the bowl moves down and opens a gap at its edge; the beans pass over the rim into the cooler.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Where they are used

Both designs appear in the sources as industrial roasters, run in batches.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the 2017 textbook chapter, continuous roasters were popular some decades earlier but had become nearly extinct; batch roasters predominate.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry describes a continuous bowl roaster.

In the 2000 thesis, one paddle roaster and one rotating-bowl roaster were used for industrial-scale roasting trials.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry gives market shares or typical installations for either design.

## Air-to-bean ratio and heat transfer

The air-to-bean ratio is the mass of process air per mass of green coffee in a roast.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same heat can be delivered by less air at a higher temperature or by more air at a lower one.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the textbook chapter, the ratio plays a key role in how heat divides between convection and conduction; a precise calculation or measurement of that split is difficult.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The designs set different limits on the ratio. In a drum, the air flow is capped by the exit velocity at which beans would be blown out, so drums usually run at a relatively low ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a paddle roaster, the expansion chamber keeps the beans in even at high air flow, and the paddles move the beans largely independently of it. The chapter therefore describes a wide usable range of ratios and a variable conduction-to-convection balance.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For the bowl roaster, it describes "a certain range" of ratios.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The thesis frames the same point more broadly. Roasters use all three heat-transfer modes, and since industrial coffee is roasted exclusively with hot air, it sorts systems into conduction-dominated and convection-dominated ones.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> For the paddle roaster in its trials, it states that the paddles were there to increase the rate of heat transfer.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Why roast times differ

According to the textbook chapter, the range of roasting times a paddle roaster can run reaches shorter times than a drum's typical range, and the bowl roaster's typical range is also shorter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> We do not give the chapter's minute values: they rest on that one Tier B source.

The sources do not explain the difference in roast times, and no study consulted for this entry compared the designs at equal batch size, air temperature and roast degree (see Open questions).

In a 2020 analysis of eight sensory studies, roast colour was the stronger predictor of flavour; at constant colour, development time had a larger effect than the time to first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A flavour comparison between machine types would therefore need matched colour and time profiles; none was found.

## Risks and limits

Any mechanical movement stresses the beans. According to the textbook chapter, a well-designed roaster avoids and minimises bean breakage.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry compares breakage between paddle, bowl and drum roasters.

According to the textbook chapter, most large-scale operations lead a major part of the hot off-gas back to the burner to save energy; another part must leave the system, so that gas concentrations with the potential for explosion do not build up.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The thesis reports partial recirculation for the bowl roaster in its trials.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Heat-transfer split by design.** No measurement of the conduction-to-convection ratio in a paddle or bowl roaster was found; the textbook chapter calls it difficult to calculate or measure in any roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Same coffee, same colour, different machine.** No peer-reviewed study consulted for this entry roasts one coffee to one colour in a paddle, a bowl and a drum roaster and compares chemistry or flavour.
- **Origin of the paddle design.** A trade magazine traces the paddle design to a patent filed by an equipment manufacturer.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> We could not locate or open that patent, so no date is given here.
- **Air flow and roast time.** Whether the wider usable air-to-bean ratio of paddle roasters explains the shorter roasts they can run has not been tested in any study consulted for this entry.
- **Continuous centrifugal roasting.** Whether rotating-bowl roasters were also built for continuous operation is not covered by the sources consulted for this entry.
