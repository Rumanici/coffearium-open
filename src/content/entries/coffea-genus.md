## What the genus is

*Coffea* is a genus of flowering plants in the coffee family, Rubiaceae.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Every cup of coffee comes from the seeds of one of its species, but most of the genus is wild and never farmed.

The number of species keeps changing as botanists describe new ones and revise old ones. A 2006 conspectus listed 103 species.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In 2011 the closely related genus *Psilanthus* was merged into *Coffea*, raising the count from 104 to 124 and extending the genus from Africa and Madagascar to tropical Asia and Australasia.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The latest revision, in 2025, raised the count from 131 to 133 known species by reinstating two old names.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Kew’s Plants of the World Online also lists 133 accepted species.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Madagascar has by far the most species, 67. In mainland Africa, Cameroon has the most (18), followed by Tanzania (17).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Two species make almost all the coffee

The world’s coffee farmers, and with them the whole coffee supply chain, rely on two species: Arabica (*C. arabica*) and robusta (*C. canephora*).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Arabica alone is the source of about 60% of coffee products worldwide.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

A third species group gives a small but growing share. Cultivation of liberica coffee (*C. liberica*) is increasing as climate challenges affect arabica and robusta, and as demand grows; excelsa (*C. dewevrei*) production is rising in India for similar reasons. Liberica is seeing a renaissance in Malaysia, Indonesia and Fiji. Excelsa draws attention in Uganda, South Sudan and Guinea because it crops under higher temperatures and longer dry spells than robusta, and its production is rising in India, Vietnam and Indonesia.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Two West African species, *C. stenophylla* and *C. affinis*, were grown as small-scale crops more than 100 years ago and are now rare in the wild and no longer farmed.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Arabica is a hybrid

Arabica is not a “pure” species. It is an allotetraploid hybrid of *C. eugenioides* and *C. canephora*. A 2024 genome study dates the founding hybridisation to 350,000–610,000 years ago and finds several bottlenecks before domestication, which left Arabica with little genetic variation.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Earlier estimates of its age differed widely (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> How that narrow base was split into the varieties grown today is the subject of [Arabica varieties and their lines](/entries/arabica-varieties/).

## Wild species at risk

Applying the IUCN Red List criteria to all 124 species then known, a 2019 study found that at least 60% of coffee species are threatened with extinction, 45% are not held in any germplasm collection, and 28% are not known to occur in any protected area.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Wild Arabica itself is listed as Endangered.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Its assessment depends on climate change: with climate projections included it is Endangered; without them it would be Least Concern.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Wild species are critical for developing the coffee crop and so for the sustainability of coffee production.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Forgotten crop species such as *C. stenophylla* may carry useful traits, including taste, disease resistance and climate resilience.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The index below lists every accepted species, where it grows wild and its Red List category. ## How the genus grew: Psilanthus merged

*Coffea* and *Psilanthus* were long kept apart, but DNA sequences from four plastid regions and the nuclear ITS region showed that the sequence divergence between their species is negligible, with strong indications that neither genus is monophyletic (a group made of one ancestor and all its descendants). Only one critical feature separated them: *Coffea* has a long, protruding style, *Psilanthus* a short one hidden inside the flower. On this evidence *Psilanthus* was merged into *Coffea* in 2011.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same study informally named a group of closely related species the “*Coffea liberica* alliance”.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Liberica split into three

Liberica and excelsa coffee were long treated as two varieties of one species, *C. liberica*. Genomic, morphological and distribution data published in 2025 support three separate species instead: *C. liberica* (Liberica), *C. dewevrei* (excelsa) and *C. klainei*, a poorly known species previously treated as a synonym.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## A heat-tolerant relative: stenophylla

*C. stenophylla* is native to Guinea, Sierra Leone and Ivory Coast. Fieldwork published in 2020 rediscovered it in Sierra Leone, where it had not been recorded in the wild since 1954.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Sensory tests confirmed historical reports of a superior flavour and found a profile similar to high-quality Arabica. The species grows and crops at a mean annual temperature 6.2–6.8 °C higher than Arabica, under the same climatic range as robusta and Liberica.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The same research group found that hybridisation is possible across all the major groups of key African *Coffea* species, which matters for breeding.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## The Red List in numbers

Of the 133 accepted species, 131 can be matched cleanly to the IUCN Red List (version 2026-1). Among those, 12 are Critically Endangered, 40 Endangered and 23 Vulnerable: 75 threatened species, or 57% of the matched names. Another 9 are Near Threatened, 25 Least Concern, 14 Data Deficient and 8 Not Evaluated.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> One name was corrected by hand: the GBIF copy of the Red List shows *C. liberica* as not evaluated, but the IUCN Red List itself lists it as Least Concern.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The 2019 assessment, done on 124 species with the full assessment data, gives at least 60%.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In 2006, 72 of 103 species (about 70%) were assessed as threatened, through a decline in the quantity and quality of their habitat.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The counts above are our tally of the Red List data matched to Plants of the World Online names; two names, *C. dewevrei* and *C. klainei*, reinstated in 2025, have no clean match yet.
