    value: Steel blades chip beans; no defined size without a sieve (textbook)

    value: Conical (a cone working against a second burr) or flat (two facing discs); one burr turns, one is held

    value: Roller mills with one or more pairs of rolls; normaliser below the rolls

    value: Gap between the burrs or rolls, adjusted along the axis or across the rolls

## Blades and burrs

A grinder breaks roasted beans into particles small enough to brew. The grinding entry explains why; this entry covers the machines.

**Blade grinders.** A blade grinder works like a kitchen blender.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Steel blades spin and chip the beans apart.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to a coffee science textbook, a defined grind size is only possible if the grinder has a sieve with a set mesh.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Burr grinders.** A burr grinder feeds beans into a gap between two grinding parts with teeth, called burrs.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> One burr is driven and turns; the other is held still.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The spacing between the burrs sets the grind size.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Turning a dial moves one burr closer to the other or further away.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Grinding always makes particles of many sizes and shapes. This spread of sizes is called the particle size distribution (PSD).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Most ground coffee has two peaks: one of fines and one near the average size.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Fines are often defined as particles below 100 µm.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The type and quality of the grinder change this spread.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The particle size and fines entry explains how the spread is measured and what it does in the cup.

## Conical, flat and roller

**Conical burrs.** A cone-shaped burr works against a second burr; one turns and the other is held.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In one patented grinder, an outer burr turns around a stationary inner burr.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In a mill patented in 1891, a crank turned the grinding cone on its spindle.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The grinding shell was fixed to the body, and raising or lowering the spindle set the fineness.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> According to the textbook, conical burrs are made of tool steel or ceramics. Small sets are mainly found in home grinders, larger ones in busy espresso bars.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Flat burrs.** Two toothed discs face each other.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In one patented design, the teeth are angled so that the two burrs cut like scissors.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The grooves are deepest near the centre and shallow towards the edge.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Beans are fed in from above, and the ground coffee leaves all around the rim of the turning burr.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> According to the textbook, flat burrs are used everywhere, from home grinders to packing lines in small and medium roasteries.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Roller mills.** Industrial plants grind between rolls rather than burrs.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Two rolls turn towards each other, with a grinding gap between them.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Several pairs can follow each other in stages.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> According to the textbook, the roller mill was still the state of the art for industrial coffee grinding as of 2017.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## What changes the grind

**The gap.** The distance between burrs or rolls is the main setting.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Settings do not transfer between grinders: to reach the same espresso flow time, different grinders need different settings and give different average sizes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Burr design.** Grinder makers work on the design of the teeth, the casing around the burrs and the spout to shape the PSD, according to the textbook.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> For a given median size, different grinding technologies can give a different share of fines.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> No peer-reviewed study consulted for this entry compared conical and flat burrs at matched settings.

**Speed.** The textbook lists the speed of the burrs as a setting a grinder should let the barista adjust.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Variable-speed drives exist in patented grinders.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured how burr speed changes the PSD.

**Alignment.** Grinders should allow precise, repeatable setting of the parallelism and position of the grinding tools, according to the textbook.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured how misaligned burrs change the PSD.

**Wear.** In industrial roller mills, worn rolls give a wider PSD and more fines at the same average size, according to the textbook.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A university study of grind-on-demand burrs reports that damage and wear made the PSD less precisely controllable.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Temperature.** Warmer ground coffee gives faster flow times, according to the textbook. A warmer bean seems to deform more and break coarser.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a 2016 study, colder beans gave a narrower PSD and a smaller most common particle size (mode).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**The beans.** In the same study, four single-origin coffees gave the same PSD whatever their origin and processing.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> According to the textbook, roast degree is the bean property that matters most. Darker and faster roasts are more porous and brittle, and give a wide PSD with many fines.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Hand and electric

Hand grinders use the same mechanisms as electric ones; the 1891 patent above describes a crank-turned conical mill.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> According to the textbook, small conical burrs are mainly used in home grinders.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry compared the PSD of hand and electric grinders.

## Dosing: doser, on demand and by weight

**Grinding on demand.** Café and home grinders mostly grind at the moment the coffee is needed, which best preserves freshness, according to the textbook.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Ground coffee loses gas fast. In one study, up to 75% of the trapped gas in fresh coffee escaped during grinding and within 90 s.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry describes how doser grinders are built. They are therefore not covered further here.

**Three ways to dose.** According to the textbook, point-of-sale grinders dose in one of three ways.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Pre-dosed:** a weighed amount of beans goes in, and all of it is ground.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **By time:** a timer runs the motor for a set time, which gives roughly the right amount.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> One patent states that the dose is calculated from the grinding time, while the real grinding speed varies.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Another names grind size, coffee type, wear, humidity and temperature as causes of variation.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **By weight:** a load cell weighs the ground coffee and stops the grinder at the set mass.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In one patented grinder, the load cell sits in the base, under the fork that holds the portafilter.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

Weight dosing helps keep the ratio of coffee to beverage fixed when the espresso machine stops at a set beverage weight.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## How burrs break beans

**Fracture and fines.** A bimodal PSD comes not only from the grinder but from the bean’s structure.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Whenever a roasted bean cracks, very fine particles below 100 µm flake off, and this repeats with every further break.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In a 2016 laser-diffraction study, some particles were produced in their final size by the first fracture of the whole bean, and only a few small particles came from that first fracture.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The textbook adds that with other techniques the two peaks can merge, especially when grinding with liquid nitrogen.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Cutting versus crushing.** In industrial roll grinders, cutting tends to produce rounder particles and a narrow PSD; pressure and shear give irregular shapes and a wider PSD.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The textbook attributes this to an older coffee technology handbook and states that the best configuration is found empirically.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the patented flat burr above, angled teeth on both burrs give a scissors-like cutting action.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Conical and flat in one example.** The textbook shows PSDs from several flat burr grinders and a conical grinder, each set to give the same espresso flow time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In its account, the conical grinder gave a wider PSD and needed a clearly coarser median to reach the same flow time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> One of the chapter’s two authors works for a grinder maker, the curves are drawn without numbers, and no peer-reviewed comparison was found; this entry therefore gives no fines share by burr type.

**Why the share of fines matters.** In a 2024 espresso study, more fines reduced the permeability of the coffee bed, slowing flow and lengthening extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Within the conditions tested, fines mainly changed permeability rather than how efficiently coffee was extracted.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Heat in the grinder

According to the textbook, warmer ground coffee gives faster flow times. It was once explained by thermal expansion of the grinding mechanism, but grinders were later found to stay dimensionally constant; the bean itself seems to become more plastic when warm or humid and to break coarser.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Centrifugal and pin grinders keep some particles in the chamber longer than needed, and tend to produce more heat and a wider PSD than roller grinders.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The 2016 study tested bean temperature on a commercial flat-burr grinder fitted with its Turkish burr set, turning at 1,480 rpm at a fixed setting.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Beans came from liquid nitrogen, dry ice, a freezer and room temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Cooling from room temperature to about −200 °C reduced the mode of the size distribution by 31%, and the PSD became narrower, with the biggest change between room temperature and −19 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The authors also marked a fine particulate cutoff, a diameter they defined arbitrarily. Their figure caption gives it as 61 ± 3 µm at −196 °C, 63 ± 3 µm at −79 °C, 73 ± 3 µm at −19 °C and 70 ± 3 µm at 20 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The paper’s text gives the coldest temperature as −200 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> They suggest that particles warm at the interface with a warm burr and that the burrs grow warmer with use; they call the fracture mechanism behind the temperature effect hard to describe.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Static and retention

Coffee charges as it is ground. In a 2024 study, the charge of the grounds depended mainly on the moisture inside the beans, which predicted charge slightly better than roast colour.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Drier coffees charged largely negatively.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The charge grew as coffee was ground finer; finer settings mean more fracturing, longer contact with the burrs and more fines.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Negatively charged coffees reached up to about 120 nC per gram.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The grinder had steel flat burrs of 98 mm.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Adding a little water to the whole beans before grinding reduced charging systematically.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It left near-zero grounds retained in the grinder and broke up electrostatic clumps of fines and boulders.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In espresso brewed from 18.0 g of coffee to 45.0 g of beverage, the shot took nearly 50% longer and reached 8.9% total dissolved solids (TDS) rather than 8.2%.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> According to the textbook, avoiding segregation and fouling of fines, for example by reducing electrostatic charging, is one of the requirements a grinder should meet.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Retention (coffee left inside the grinder between doses) was not measured in grams in that study, and no other study consulted for this entry measured it.

## Industrial grinding for packaged coffee

**Roll stages.** In one patented roller grinder, three pairs of rolls follow each other: crusher rolls, finish rolls spaced to give the target size, and fines rolls, one of them adjustable.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The textbook describes industrial roller grinders with several stages.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It describes axial grooves that are asymmetrical, with a sharp and a dull edge, and facing rolls can be paired sharp to sharp, sharp to dull or dull to dull.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Grooved roll surfaces are usual; one patent instead prefers smooth rolls.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Speed difference.** The rolls of a pair turn at different speeds. One patent specifies a ratio of peripheral speeds from 1:1.5 to 1:4 and, for fine coffee powder, a gap of 0.03–0.05 mm.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> According to the textbook, newer roller grinders give each roll its own drive, intended to tailor the PSD, reduce fines and spread wear.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Bimodal by design.** A roller grinder can also make two populations on purpose. In the textbook’s account, the lowest pair of rolls can be shifted so that part of the coffee passes without further grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The textbook cites a patent application for this; the patent granted on that application describes a bi-modal roller grinder.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Normalising.** Below the rolls, a normaliser mixes, kneads or beats the grounds with paddles or hammers.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It breaks remaining large particles, cuts up released silverskin, reduces fines by binding them to larger particles, and sets the density of the grounds for silos, packs and capsules.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the patented roller grinder, a densifier with blades forms the grinds into a more uniform size and shape.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Where fines and chaff must be removed entirely, as for soluble coffee, air separation is used.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Wear in industry.** At a constant average size, fines rise steadily over the service time of the rolls, according to the textbook; the gaps are re-adjusted and grooved rolls are eventually re-sharpened or replaced.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Monitoring the fines is described as the best way to follow roll wear.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Gas.** Grinding releases trapped gas, including carbon monoxide; the textbook recommends ventilating grinders.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The degassing entry covers how much gas is released.

## Open questions

- **Conical versus flat.** No peer-reviewed study consulted for this entry measured the share of fines for conical and flat burrs at matched median size. Claims that one type gives more fines or more “body” remain untested here.
- **Burr speed.** A patent background states that slower grinding is more precise, but gives no measurement.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry tested it.
- **Alignment and wear in cafés.** How far a burr can be out of parallel before the PSD changes, and how many kilograms a burr set grinds before it does, lack open, peer-reviewed data.
- **Heat in use.** Burr and coffee temperatures during a busy service have not been measured in the studies consulted here.
- **Hand grinders.** No study consulted for this entry compared hand and electric grinders.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Uman et al., 2016 – Bean origin and temperature.</strong> Grinds four single-origin coffees on one flat-burr grinder and measures the PSD by laser diffraction; origin and processing made no difference. Grinding the same coffee from liquid nitrogen, dry ice, freezer and room temperature showed that colder beans give a narrower distribution and a smaller mode.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 6, 24483 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/srep24483">doi:10.1038/srep24483</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Méndez Harper et al., 2024 – Static during grinding.</strong> Measures the charge of ground coffee across roasts, moisture contents and grind settings. Internal moisture predicted charge slightly better than roast colour; a little water on the beans cut charge, clumping and retention, and changed espresso flow and strength.</p>
<p class="meta">Matter 7(1) · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matt.2023.11.005">doi:10.1016/j.matt.2023.11.005</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Smrke et al., 2024 – Fines in espresso.</strong> Grinds one coffee at several burr spacings, adds sieved fines and follows extraction over the shot. Fines lowered bed permeability and lengthened shots but hardly changed extraction efficiency; the paper notes that grinding technologies differ in their share of fines at a given median.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 14, 5612 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-55831-x">doi:10.1038/s41598-024-55831-x</a></p>
