## Which price is charted

There is no single “coffee price”. For long series, the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund (IMF) both publish a monthly arabica price.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The World Bank names its source: the International Coffee Organization (ICO) indicator price for other mild arabicas.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It is averaged over the New York and Bremen/Hamburg markets, ex-dock.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The charts on this page use two datasets, one per chart. From 1970 to 1991 they show the World Bank series; from 1992 they show the IMF series.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Both are monthly averages in nominal US dollars: the money of each month, not adjusted for inflation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> We give them in US cents per pound (lb); 1 lb is 0.4535924 kg.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

A dollar of 1977 bought far more than a dollar of 2026: US consumer prices rose more than fivefold in between.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> So each chart also shows a real, inflation-adjusted price. We divide each month’s price by the United States consumer price index of that month and express it in September 2026 dollars (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The real line answers a different question: what the price was worth in today’s money.

## Frost and quotas, 1970–1991

From the 1960s until 1989 the price moved inside a system of export quotas set under the International Coffee Agreements.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> How the agreements worked is explained in The International Coffee Agreements.

In 1975 a severe frost damaged the Brazilian crop.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> According to a 1985 World Bank profile, it ended a period of high stocks and low real prices.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Arabica rose from 55.8 US cents per lb in June 1975 to 83.5 cents in August, and kept rising to 317.5 cents in April 1977.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In today’s money, that April 1977 price was about 1,780 cents per lb (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Damage to the Brazilian crop in 1985 sent prices up again, and the ICO decided to suspend export quotas.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The quotas were formally suspended in February 1986 and reinstated in 1987.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Monthly arabica rose to 237.7 cents in January 1986 and peaked at 239.5 cents in March.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The quota system broke down in July 1989.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Arabica fell from 125.2 cents in June 1989 to 68.5 cents in October, a drop of about 45% (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The ICO describes a price collapse of some 50% across all groups of coffee, followed by several years of very low prices.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## A free market, 1992–2026

By 2004, supply was no longer managed by international agreements.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Prices stayed low until two frosts hit Brazil in 1994.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> According to a 2004 World Bank study, Brazil lost about 13 million bags of production in 1994, which pushed prices to a very high level.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Arabica rose from 117.2 cents in May 1994 to 218.7 cents in September.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

By the end of 2000 prices had fallen below the levels of 1989–1993.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> This period became known as the coffee crisis.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Arabica reached 54.36 cents in August 2002.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2004 World Bank study describes these prices as 100-year lows in real terms.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Prices rose in 2010 and 2011. The ICO composite indicator averaged 210.39 cents in 2011, against 147.24 in 2010.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Its highest month in 2011 was April, at 231.24 cents; other mild arabica reached 300.48 cents that month.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

In April 2019 the ICO composite fell to 94.42 cents, its lowest monthly average since July 2006.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> From 2024 prices rose to new records. In February 2025 the ICO composite averaged 354.32 cents, the highest monthly average on record in nominal terms.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In September 2026 other mild arabica averaged 330.12 cents and the ICO composite 262.18 cents.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

## Nominal records and real records

The two lines tell different stories. In nominal terms the highs of 2025 are the highest monthly averages on record.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> In real terms they are not. In September 2026 dollars, the April 1977 peak was about 1,783 cents per lb, four times the February 2025 price of ≈432 cents (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Even the 1994, 1997 and 2011 peaks were higher in real terms than 2025 (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

The long trend points down. According to the 2004 World Bank study, arabica prices declined by an average of 3% a year from 1970, and robusta prices by 5%.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In September 2026 dollars, real arabica averaged about 558 cents per lb from January 1970 to June 1989. From 1992 to 2026 it averaged about 236 cents (our calculation).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A coffee science textbook chapter states that real prices in the 1990s and 2000s were lower than in the 1980s, before the agreements collapsed.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## What moved the price

The sources name a few recurring drivers. Weather in Brazil is the most visible: the frosts of 1975 and 1994 and the crop damage of 1985 each sent prices up.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> According to the 2004 World Bank study, a price rise from frost or drought in Brazil starts a cycle: new planting, more supply a few years later, then lower prices.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The rules of the market matter too. A 2008 econometric study found that the agreements created a price cycle that does not exist in periods without them.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> After 1989, Brazil replanted in areas with less frost risk, and Brazil and Vietnam raised both quantity and quality.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Stocks and exchange rates also play a part; how they enter a single price is explained in How a coffee price is made.

These are past prices. Coffearium does not forecast prices and gives no trading advice.

## Method and caveats

**Datasets.** The World Bank describes its arabica price as the ICO indicator for other mild arabicas, averaged over New York and Bremen/Hamburg, ex-dock.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The IMF series says only that it is a benchmark price, a period average in nominal US dollars.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In most months checked, the two series and the ICO agree closely. In January 1992 the World Bank gives 1.62 USD per kg (73.5 cents per lb, our conversion) and the IMF 73.03 cents.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In February 2025 the IMF gives 409.52 cents and the ICO Other Milds group 409.48.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The largest gap found is July 2025: 316.73 cents (IMF) against 325.50 (ICO).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In September 2026 the World Bank gives 7.26 USD per kg (329.3 cents, our conversion), the IMF 330.12.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> We still keep them in separate charts, one dataset each.

**Annual averages.** The World Bank gives annual arabica averages of 4.54 USD per kg in 2023, 5.62 in 2024 and 8.47 in 2025; robusta averaged 2.63, 4.41 and 4.86.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In cents per lb, 2025 arabica is about 384 (our conversion).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The data tables under the charts give annual averages of the monthly values (our calculation).

**Deflator.** We use the US consumer price index for all urban consumers (CPI-U), all items, not seasonally adjusted.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It measures prices paid by US households, not producers’ costs. The World Bank study’s long-run chart is deflated with a different index, labelled “MUV” (manufactures unit value).<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Real values therefore depend on the index chosen, and our numbers are not directly comparable with the World Bank’s. A producer’s income also depends on the exchange rate of the local currency, which neither index captures.

**Coffee year.** The ICO publishes its group weightings for each coffee year (October to September).<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The monthly series here are calendar months; annual averages in the tables are calendar years. Crop year and coffee year explains the difference.

**The quota era in brief.** In a real-price analysis of Santos 4 coffee (not the series charted here), the ICO describes the quota years 1963–1989 as averaging some 185 cents in real terms.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It gives similar averages for 1894–1962 and 1990–2013: 125 and 130 cents per lb in 1990 terms.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In a 2008 study using a price vector autoregression, the agreements decoupled Brazilian farm prices from world prices in the short run, but supported supply response in the long run.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The same study links low prices after the collapse to supply lags and a shift in the composition of coffee demand.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The agreements themselves are covered in The International Coffee Agreements.

## The crisis lows and the 2019 low

According to the ICO, the end of the quotas brought several years of very low prices.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> New plantings then again created an imbalance between supply and demand.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The 2004 World Bank study names lasting changes in supply: Brazilian replanting in areas with low frost risk, mechanisation, currency depreciation, and more and better coffee from Brazil and Vietnam.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> At the market’s low point, total availability was higher than at any previous time.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The 2019 low had a similar shape on a smaller scale. According to the ICO’s April 2019 report, market fundamentals were one of the main drivers. It names a second consecutive season of surplus, 8.35 million bags in total, and a record Brazilian harvest of 62.5 million bags in the crop year March 2018 to April 2019.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Brazilian exports were encouraged by the depreciation of the Brazilian real against the US dollar.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

The series also shows a short spike in May 1997, to 265.61 cents.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry explains it, and none analyses the causes of the 2011 high.

## The 2024–2025 rally

The ICO composite reached 270.72 cents in November 2024, an 8.0% rise from October.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The ICO’s November report names a lower Brazilian crop forecast, a delayed Vietnamese harvest, shipping delays at Santos, disruption in the Red Sea and the Panama Canal, and uncertainty over the EU deforestation regulation (EUDR).<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

In February 2025 the ICO composite averaged 354.32 cents, the highest monthly average on record, with daily values between 337.54 and 375.00 cents.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Colombian Milds and Other Milds averaged 410.64 and 409.48 cents, Brazilian Naturals 401.10 and Robustas 263.08.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> World Bank economists wrote in May 2025 that arabica reached USD 9 per kg in February–March and that coffee had hit record nominal highs.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> They name the lingering effects of the 2021–22 production shortfall and steady demand growth as the reason prices stayed high.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

Prices then eased. In February 2026 the ICO composite averaged 267.57 cents, 9.9% below January.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The ICO names a reassessment of the supply balance, including a forecast record Brazilian crop.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In the ICO’s table for March 2025 to February 2026, no month is higher than March 2025 at 347.85 cents.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Real record of 2025.** Whether the 2025 highs are a real-terms record depends on the deflator and the start year. With the US consumer price index from 1970 they are not (our calculation); no source consulted for this entry gives an official real series to 2026.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Causes of the 2011 high and the 1997 spike.** The sources consulted here give the levels but do not analyse the causes.
- **Robusta history.** This entry charts arabica only; a long monthly robusta series was not charted. The World Bank gives a faster long-run decline for robusta than for arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Producer prices.** The charts show export-market prices in US dollars. How much of each swing reached producers in local currency is not measured here.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Mehta and Chavas, 2008 – Coffee price dynamics and the agreements.</strong> The authors modelled coffee prices with a semi-structural vector autoregression, letting the International Coffee Agreement change how supply responds to price. They found that the agreement created a price cycle absent in periods without it, and link the low prices after its collapse to supply lags and a shift in demand.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Development Economics 85(1–2), 282–311 · <a href="https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/deveco/v85y2008i1-2p282-311.html">ideas.repec.org</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Lewin, Giovannucci and Varangis, 2004 – Coffee markets after the crisis.</strong> A World Bank review of the price collapse after 1989. It describes the frost-driven price cycle, the loss of about 13 million bags in Brazil in 1994, and the structural growth of supply in Brazil and Vietnam that kept prices low.</p>
<p class="meta">World Bank, Agriculture and Rural Development Discussion Paper 3 · <a href="https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/899311468167958765/pdf/283000REVISED0Coffee1Markets01PUBLIC1.pdf">documents1.worldbank.org</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Dubois, 2013 – Fifty years of the ICO.</strong> The ICO’s own history of its agreements, from the quota system to the free market. It describes the frosts, the suspension of quotas in the 1980s and their end in July 1989, and compares average real prices in the quota and free-market periods.</p>
<p class="meta">International Coffee Organization, London · <a href="https://ico.org/documents/history-ico-50-years-e.pdf">ico.org</a></p>
</div>
