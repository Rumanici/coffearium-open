## What green coffee is made of

Most of the dry weight of green coffee is carbohydrate: about 60%, according to a 2014 review.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The rest is a mix of nitrogen compounds, lipids, acids, minerals and water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The main groups, as dry weight unless stated:

- **Polysaccharides** – long sugar chains that build the cell walls. They make up about 50% of the dry weight.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Sucrose** – the main free sugar. A 2000 paper gives 3–8% of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A 2006 review gives 5–12% of dry weight in mature seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Proteins, peptides and free amino acids** – together 9–16%.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The free amino acids are only a small part of this.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Lipids** – the coffee oil and a thin surface wax, 8–18% of dry matter according to a 2014 review<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>; a 2006 review gives 7–17%.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Chlorogenic acids** – a family of phenolic acids, 4–8.4% in arabica and 7–14.4% in robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Other acids** – citric, malic and quinic acid. All acids together, chlorogenic acids included, make up about 11% of the green bean's weight.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Alkaloids** – caffeine, 0.9–1.5% in arabica and 1.2–2.4% in robusta<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>, and trigonelline, about 1%.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Minerals and water.** The Codex code of practice says moisture should not exceed 12.5% anywhere from the point where the coffee leaves the loading area until it is stored or roasted.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

These numbers are ranges across many samples and methods. A single lot can sit anywhere within them, and sources do not always agree (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

## Arabica and robusta compared

The two traded species contain the same classes of compounds in different proportions.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2001 study of 38 wild accessions of each species found more trigonelline and sucrose in *C. arabica*. It found more chlorogenic acids and caffeine in *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Green arabica averages about 15% lipids, robusta around 10%.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

According to a roasting chemistry chapter, arabica is also richer in organic acids such as citric and malic acid. Robusta is richer in free amino acids, ash and transition metals such as iron and copper.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The robusta held less sucrose and more than twice the caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Polymeric sugars and protein were close in both.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Whether robusta reliably holds more chlorogenic acids is disputed. ## What each group does in the roast

Almost every constituent of green coffee is a potential precursor of flavour or colour, or takes part in their formation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to a roasting chemistry chapter, the principal flavour precursors are sugars, proteins, free amino acids, trigonelline and chlorogenic acids.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

<figure>

<figcaption>Which green coffee constituents feed which roast reactions, mainly according to a roasting chemistry chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Schematic, not to scale; main routes only, not amounts.</figcaption>
</figure>

- **Sucrose and free amino acids** react first, because they are immediately available.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Sucrose splits into glucose and fructose, which then take part in the Maillard reaction and caramelisation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Sucrose is also the main source of the formic, acetic, glycolic and lactic acid formed in roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Amino acids** pair with sugars in the Maillard reaction and its Strecker branch.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Proteins release bound amino acids more slowly, later in the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Polysaccharides** lose their arabinose side chains, which feed browning and melanoidins. Cellulose and mannans remain largely unchanged.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Their role in body is covered under carbohydrates and body.
- **Chlorogenic acids** break down later in the roast, into bitter lactones, quinic acid and smoky phenols.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Details are in the chlorogenic acids entry.
- **Trigonelline** is partly degraded to nicotinic acid, pyridines and pyrroles.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Lipids** move to the bean surface and help retain aroma; part of them breaks down to aldehydes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Caffeine** is almost unchanged by roasting, according to the same chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Water** is lost in the early drying stage. The starting moisture affects light roasts more than dark ones.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Species differences carry through to the cup. According to the roasting chemistry chapter, the higher sucrose of arabica explains its higher levels of buttery diketones, furfurals and caramel-like furaneol. Robusta's higher chlorogenic acids give more smoky phenols.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## What changes the composition

**Species and genotype.** Composition varies between accessions within each species, not only between the two species.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Variation was larger in *C. canephora* than in *C. arabica*, except for trigonelline and sucrose.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Climate.** A 2010 study used 16 plots on Réunion Island with a wide range of climates. Chlorogenic acids and fatty acids in the seed depended on the mean air temperature during seed development.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Ripeness.** Chlorogenic acid content and composition change considerably as the fruit matures, according to a 2006 review.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> A 2023 study compared three stages of ripe fruit. Lipids, proteins, alkaloids, total chlorogenic acids and sucrose did not differ; only glucose and fructose varied.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Processing.** In a 2006 study of green arabica, washed coffees held only low amounts of glucose and fructose; unwashed (natural) coffees held significantly more.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Sucrose was not significantly affected by processing.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The washed process and natural process entries cover the methods.

**Storage.** Free fatty acids rose during storage, most in green coffee that had been moistened. In one set of samples they reached almost 4.8 g/kg.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The authors trace this to lipase, an enzyme that frees fatty acids from oil, and report that cold and dry storage inhibits it.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Carbohydrates in detail

Galactomannans are the predominant cell-wall components, accounting for 50% of the polysaccharides.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> They have a backbone of β-1,4-linked mannose with single galactose side units.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The second main group are type II arabinogalactans, highly branched and covalently linked to protein as arabinogalactan proteins.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Cellulose completes the wall.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

During seed development, the wall changes. Arabinogalactan made up about half of the polysaccharides in early growth and 34% in the mature seed, while mannan synthesis increased towards maturity.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Sucrose dominates the free sugars from the earliest stages of maturation and is essentially all of them in mature seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

In roasting, the polymers are depolymerised and lose molecular weight even in a light roast. Up to 40% of the polysaccharides were degraded after a long roast.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The released arabinose is mainly involved in melanoidin formation and may contribute to formic and acetic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Free sugars, by contrast, are lost almost entirely within minutes.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

In the paired samples of 2017, total sugars after hydrolysis were 35.8 g/100 g in the arabica and 36.0 in the robusta. Mannose (16.2 and 15.0), galactose (7.82 and 9.96) and glucose (9.76 and 9.00) dominated, with arabinose at 2.00 and 1.98.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Lipids and diterpenes

Most of the lipids, the coffee oil, sit in the endosperm; the surface wax is about 0.2–0.3% of the bean weight.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Triacylglycerols are the main class.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Linoleic and palmitic acid are the main fatty acids, according to the roasting chemistry chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Diterpenes of the kaurene family make up up to 20% of the total lipids.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Arabica contains cafestol and kahweol. Robusta contains cafestol, small amounts of kahweol and 16-O-methylcafestol (16-OMC).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The diterpenes occur as fatty acid esters.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Their behaviour in the brew is covered under coffee oil.

**16-OMC as a robusta marker.** A 2006 review describes 16-OMC as the ideal characteristic for detecting robusta in arabica blends.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It cites DIN 10779, a method for roasted coffee that detects robusta at less than 2% of a mixture.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2018 study found 16-OMC and 16-O-methylkahweol in arabica roast coffee for the first time.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> By low-field NMR, it could detect robusta in arabica at about 1–2% w/w, but not below about 1% (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Nitrogen compounds and acids

Proteins, peptides and free amino acids account for 9–16% of the dry weight.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the roasting chemistry chapter, total protein is similar in both species.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The main free amino acids are glutamic acid, aspartic acid and asparagine, and almost all are used up in roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The same chapter links robusta's higher isoleucine and leucine to more 2- and 3-methylbutanal after roasting, and its tryptophan to animal-like skatole.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Trigonelline is present at about 1% of dry weight, with slightly higher values in arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Caffeine stays nearly unchanged through the roast (see Caffeine).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2014 review gives 0.9–1.3% for green arabica and 1.5–2.5% for robusta, close to the 0.9–1.5% and 1.2–2.4% of a 2021 review.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

Chlorogenic acids are esters of hydroxycinnamic acids with quinic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In green coffee, 5-caffeoylquinic acid alone makes up about 56–62% of them, according to a 2006 review.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In the paired samples, 5-caffeoylquinic acid was 4.353 g/100 g in the arabica and 5.061 in the robusta, of totals of 6.972 and 9.397.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The roast chemistry of the other acids is covered in acids in coffee.

## Measuring composition

- **Moisture.** ISO 6673 heats a test portion of about 10 g at 105 °C for 16 h and reports the loss in mass.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Results are about 1.0% lower than with the ISO 1446 reference method for water content.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The moisture and water activity entry covers meters and water activity.
- **Sugars.** Free sucrose, glucose and fructose are measured by anion-exchange chromatography. Total sugars, including those in polysaccharides, are measured by gas chromatography after hydrolysis.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Protein.** Commonly calculated from total nitrogen. One study subtracted caffeine nitrogen first and multiplied by 6.25.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Alkaloids, sucrose and chlorogenic acids.** High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was used in the 2001 study of wild accessions.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **16-OMC.** DIN 10779 is a validated method for roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It has since been withdrawn and replaced by DIN EN 18003 (see Arabica and robusta compared). Low-field NMR is an alternative screening method.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Two points matter when comparing numbers. First, the basis: dry matter, dry weight or as-is weight, which several sources do not state.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Second, the sample: a range across many accessions is not the same as one commercial lot.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Chlorogenic acids by species.** Older ranges and a meta-analysis disagree on whether robusta holds more (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **16-OMC in arabica.** If arabica holds small amounts, the limit for detecting robusta in blends is about 1%.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **The insoluble fraction.** According to the roasting chemistry chapter, the role of the water-insoluble carbohydrates in roasting is much less understood than that of the soluble fraction.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Minerals.** No source consulted for this entry measured ash or minerals by species with a stated method.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Ky et al., 2001 – Diversity in wild arabica and robusta.</strong> Analysed caffeine, trigonelline, chlorogenic acids and sucrose by HPLC in 38 wild accessions of each species. Arabica held more trigonelline and sucrose, robusta more chlorogenic acids and caffeine, and robusta varied more except in trigonelline and sucrose.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry 75(2), 223–230 · <a href="https://agritrop.cirad.fr/526749">agritrop.cirad.fr/526749</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Joët et al., 2010 – Climate and processing.</strong> Compared green arabica from 16 plots on Réunion Island with broad climatic variation. Mean air temperature during seed development controlled chlorogenic acids and fatty acids in the seed.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry 118(3), 693–701 · <a href="https://agritrop.cirad.fr/551309">agritrop.cirad.fr/551309</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Knopp et al., 2006 – Processing and sugars.</strong> Measured sugars in green arabica after washed and unwashed processing. Washed coffees held little glucose and fructose, unwashed coffees significantly more, while sucrose was not significantly affected.</p>
<p class="meta">European Food Research and Technology 223, 195–201 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00217-005-0172-1">doi:10.1007/s00217-005-0172-1</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Gunning et al., 2018 – 16-OMC in arabica.</strong> Used low-field NMR and other methods on roast coffees. Found 16-O-methylcafestol and 16-O-methylkahweol in arabica for the first time, which sets a floor of about 1% for detecting robusta in arabica.</p>
<p class="meta">Food Chemistry 248, 52–60 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2017.12.034">doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2017.12.034</a></p>
</div>
