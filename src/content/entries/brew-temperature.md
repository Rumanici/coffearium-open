## What brew temperature means

Brew temperature is how hot the water is while it extracts the coffee. The number depends on where it is measured. A kettle or heater has a set point. The slurry – the wet mix of coffee and water in the brewer – can be cooler.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

Standards therefore say where they measure. The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) home-brewer standard measures the slurry in the basket.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> An Italian espresso specification places its probe about 1 mm from the shower screen, where water leaves the group.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The SCA cupping standard specifies the water as it is poured.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Brew temperature is not drinking temperature; the serving temperature entry covers the cup.

## What heat does to extraction

**Speed.** Hotter water extracts faster. In a 2021 immersion study, a 99 °C brew passed 4% TDS (total dissolved solids) within 20 minutes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Brews started at 94 and 80 °C took more than 30 minutes to reach similar values.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Why.** According to a coffee science textbook, most coffee compounds dissolve better in hotter water.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Hot water is also less viscous, so it enters the bed and the particles more easily.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In the textbook’s account, the effect differs between compounds. High temperatures favour the extraction of less polar compounds, and bitter, astringent and phenolic compounds gain the most.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**End point.** Speed and end point are different things. In the 2021 study, full immersion brews between 80 and 99 °C settled at about the same final strength.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Much colder water did change the end point. In a 2022 study, brews started at 92 °C extracted on average about 13% more soluble material than brews at 4 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The extraction yield entry explains how strength and yield are calculated.

**Flavour.** A 2020 study brewed drip coffee at 87, 90 and 93 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It varied brew time, flow rate, dose and grind size so that every temperature hit the same strength and yield.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Trained panellists then found no appreciable sensory effect of temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Strength and yield shaped the cup; temperature mainly changed how fast the brew got there.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Ranges in standards

Several documents specify a temperature window. Each is written for its own purpose, and each measures at a different point.

- **Home brewers.** SCA 310-2021 requires the slurry to reach 90–96 °C by the time 33% of the water has been sprayed.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It must stay in that range to the end of the brew cycle.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Earlier SCA documents.** A 2020 study reports 92–96 °C from the Coffee Brewing Handbook (2011) and from the SCA home-brewer programme of 2017.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Cupping.** SCA 102-2024 specifies water at 93 ± 3 °C, poured onto the grounds to the rim.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Espresso.** An Italian specification published with the contact details of the Comitato Italiano del Caffè (COMITCAF) sets 90–96 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The Italian Espresso National Institute (INEI) is reported with 90 ± 2 °C for water leaving the machine.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

SCA 310-2021 also limits the holding plate. A heated carafe must keep the coffee at 80–85 °C for the first 30 minutes and never heat it above 85 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Where the heat goes

Heat is lost between the heater and the coffee. In the 2021 immersion study, the beaker had no lid or insulation. The temperature fell after the hot water was added to the room-temperature grounds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Machines show the same gap. In a 2023 espresso study, set points of 80, 89 and 98 °C gave average brew temperatures of 79.1, 88.2 and 96.5 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The machine measured them just above the coffee puck.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A 2009 study of espresso pods found measured temperatures significantly below the controller settings.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

The heating system shapes the curve over a shot. In a 2013 comparison, temperature on the coffee filter peaked at 93 °C in a pump machine and 98 °C in a lever machine.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In the lever machine it then fell about 10 degrees during extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The pump machine extracted more chlorogenic acids; the authors judged this very likely due to its lower temperature and found pressure less influential.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The two machines also differed in their pressure curves<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>, so the comparison does not isolate temperature. The espresso machine entry explains boilers, heat exchangers and temperature control.

For hand brewing, no peer-reviewed measurement of the slurry in a pour-over dripper was found among the sources consulted for this entry. Figures that circulate for kettle-to-slurry losses are therefore left out.

## Boiling point and altitude

Water boils when its vapour pressure equals the pressure of the air above it. The SCA water handbook explains the lower boiling point at high altitude this way.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Air pressure falls with height, so an open kettle reaches a lower maximum temperature in the mountains.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

The figures below are our own calculation. They combine steam tables published by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) with a NASA standard-atmosphere model.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> At about 1,000 m, water boils near 96.7 °C. At about 2,000 m, it boils near 93.5 °C, and at about 3,000 m near 89.9 °C. The US Department of Agriculture gives a similar rule: just under 1 °F less per 500 feet of height.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

Above about 3,000 m, water in an open kettle boils below 90 °C, the lower limit of SCA 310-2021 (our comparison).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> A closed boiler under pressure can exceed 100 °C, because pressure sets the boiling point.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

## Cold brew: the low end

Cold brew uses water at room temperature or in the fridge. It needs hours instead of minutes. In the 2022 study, fridge brews at 4 °C stood for 24–36 h and room-temperature brews for 12–14 h. Brews started at 92 °C needed 1–2 h.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The cold brew entry covers the method and its flavour.

## Rate and equilibrium

Two questions are easily mixed up: how fast a brew extracts, and how much it can extract in the end.

**Rate.** In the 2021 immersion study, hotter water reached a steady TDS sooner.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> At a brew ratio of 5, the 99 °C brew passed 4% TDS within 20 min; the 94 and 80 °C brews took more than 30 min.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> At a brew ratio of 25, all temperatures reached about 0.7% TDS within 20 min.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> To be sure of equilibrium, the study steeped its 1 L brews for 60 min at 80 °C, 45 min at 85 °C, 30 min at 94 °C and 20 min at 99 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

<figcaption>Steeping times one immersion study allowed for its 1 L brews to reach equilibrium, by starting water temperature. They are chosen times, not measured end points; the brews stood on a countertop at room temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup></figcaption>
</figure>

**Equilibrium.** In the same study, the final TDS did not change appreciably with brew temperature. At a brew ratio of 5 it was 4.07 ± 0.15%, and at 25 it was 0.75 ± 0.04%, over 80–99 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The partition constant K, which describes how solids divide between grounds and liquid, did not change appreciably either.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors call this insensitivity to temperature surprising.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Cold water behaves differently. In the 2022 study at a brew ratio of 5, equilibrium extraction averaged 22.28% for brews started at 92 °C, 20.74% at 22 °C and 19.55% at 4 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The average TDS was 4.27%, 3.98% and 3.76%.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Over three coffees and three roast degrees, extraction ranged from about 21.2–23.3% hot to about 17.9–21.2% at 4 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The 92 °C brews were left to cool without further heating.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Kinetics.** According to a coffee science textbook, hotter water molecules have more kinetic energy and mobility, and lower viscosity eases penetration into the particles.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The textbook also states that solubility rises with temperature, but not linearly and not equally for every compound.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In its account, caffeine solubility rises much more steeply than that of citric acid, so the balance between them can shift with temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry fitted an activation energy for coffee extraction; the 2021 study reports no values for its rate constants and no temperature dependence for them.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Sensory evidence

**Drip.** The 2020 study used a commercial drip brewer programmed to 87, 90 or 93 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Brew time, flow rate, dose and grind size were varied to reach nine target points on the brewing control chart at each temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> TDS and extraction yield changed the sensory profile significantly; brew temperature had no appreciable effect.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Coffee from the 87 °C brews was 4–5 °C cooler straight after brewing.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The 2021 immersion authors point out that this drip range was narrower than their own.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Immersion.** The 2021 authors caution that equal TDS and yield do not prove equal flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> They suggest that the drip result may extend to immersion, which remains untested.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In the 2022 immersion study, every brew was diluted to 2% TDS before tasting.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Cold brews were then rated more floral and less bitter, sour and rubbery than hot brews.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Espresso.** The evidence is mixed (see Contested).

- An on-line aroma study brewed espresso at 82, 92 and 96 °C at 9 bar. Higher temperature significantly increased volatile compounds, mostly late in the 25 s shot, and most for the less polar ones.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The study chose 92 °C as its centre point within recommendations of the Specialty Coffee Association of America.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- A 2023 flow-controlled study varied the set point from 80 to 98 °C. Higher temperature generally increased the mass of each compound in the cup. Flow rate had the strongest effect; the effects of flow rate, grind and temperature were small compared with those of brew ratio.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- The same 2023 study summarises earlier work. One study found no significant difference in trigonelline, caffeine and chlorogenic acids at 75, 80 or 85 °C; another reported ambiguous correlations at 88, 92, 96 and 98 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A third described different influences on caffeine and 5-CQA for upward and downward temperature gradients between 88 and 93 °C, without a conclusive correlation.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- A 2009 pod study at set points of 90, 100 and 110 °C linked the solids and caffeine at 110 °C to over-extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- The 2020 drip study cites espresso work in which temperature differences within 80–93 °C were imperceptible in triangle tests.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The espresso recipes entry covers how baristas use temperature alongside dose, ratio and flow.

## Boiling point: the calculation

**Method (our calculation).** The NIST tabulation of the IAPWS-95 formulation lists the saturation temperature of water at each pressure.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The NASA model gives the pressure of a standard atmosphere in the troposphere as p = 101.29 × [(T + 273.1) ÷ 288.08]<sup>5.256</sup> kPa, with T = 15.04 − 0.00649 × h in °C and h in metres.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Solving it for h gives the altitude at which each tabulated pressure occurs.

| Pressure (kPa) | Boiling point (°C) | Altitude in the standard atmosphere (m, our calculation) |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 99.606 | ≈120 |
| 95 | 98.178 | ≈550 |
| 90 | 96.687 | ≈1,000 |
| 85 | 95.125 | ≈1,470 |
| 80 | 93.486 | ≈1,960 |
| 75 | 91.758 | ≈2,480 |
| 70 | 89.932 | ≈3,020 |
| 65 | 87.993 | ≈3,600 |
| 60 | 85.926 | ≈4,220 |
| 55 | 83.709 | ≈4,880 |
| 50 | 81.317 | ≈5,590 |

Saturation temperatures from NIST<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>; altitudes from the NASA model.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The same tables give 101.42 kPa for 100 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

**Cross-check.** Near sea level, the boiling point falls by about 3.3 °C per 1,000 m (our calculation from the table). The USDA rule of just under 1 °F per 500 feet works out at about 3.6 °C per 1,000 m (our conversion).<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The USDA also gives 208 °F at 2,000 feet and about 198 °F at 7,500 feet.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> That is 97.8 °C at 610 m and about 92.2 °C at 2,286 m (our conversion), close to the table.

**Limits.** The standard atmosphere is a model; the pressure on a given day differs. The steam tables describe pure water.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Slurry temperature by hand.** No peer-reviewed measurement of slurry temperature in a hand-poured dripper, or of losses from kettle to bed, was found among the sources consulted for this entry.
- **Sensory effect beyond one drip brewer.** The 2020 study tested 87–93 °C on one brewer.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Whether the result holds at lower temperatures, in immersion or in espresso at fixed strength and yield is not established.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Temperature dependence of rate constants.** No study consulted for this entry reported an activation energy or a temperature-dependent rate constant for coffee extraction as a whole.
- **Energy use.** The 2020 authors suggest that lower brew temperatures could save energy, since work they cite found the brewing step to have the highest carbon footprint in the coffee supply chain.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry quantified the saving per cup.
- **Altitude in practice.** No study consulted for this entry compared brews made at high altitude with brews at sea level.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Batali et al., 2020 – Temperature at fixed strength and yield.</strong> Drip-brewed one coffee at 87, 90 and 93 °C to nine target points on the brewing control chart, varying brew time, flow rate, dose and grind size. Trained panellists found that strength and extraction yield shaped the sensory profile, while brew temperature had no appreciable effect.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 10, 16450 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-73341-4">doi:10.1038/s41598-020-73341-4</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Liang et al., 2021 – Equilibrium in immersion.</strong> Built a desorption model for full immersion and tested it at brew ratios up to 25 and temperatures of 80–99 °C. Hotter water reached a steady TDS sooner, but the final strength and the partition constant did not change appreciably with temperature.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 11, 6904 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-85787-1">doi:10.1038/s41598-021-85787-1</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Batali et al., 2022 – Cold, room and hot immersion.</strong> Brewed three coffees at three roast degrees to equilibrium at 4, 22 and 92 °C, then diluted all to 2% TDS for a trained panel. Hot water extracted about 13% more material than the coldest water, and the cold brews were more floral and less bitter and sour.</p>
<p class="meta">Foods 11(16), 2440 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/foods11162440">doi:10.3390/foods11162440</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Sánchez López et al., 2016 – Aroma during the shot.</strong> Followed volatile compounds in the espresso stream second by second with PTR-ToF-MS at 82, 92 and 96 °C and at 7, 9 and 11 bar. Higher temperature raised aroma extraction mainly in the second half of the shot, and most for the less polar compounds.</p>
<p class="meta">International Journal of Mass Spectrometry 401, 22–30 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijms.2016.02.015">doi:10.1016/j.ijms.2016.02.015</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Schmieder et al., 2023 – Temperature among other settings.</strong> Brewed flow-controlled espresso at set points of 80, 89 and 98 °C, with measured brew temperatures slightly below each, and analysed fractions for caffeine, trigonelline, 5-CQA and TDS. Temperature generally raised the mass in the cup, but its effect was small, and flow rate mattered most.</p>
<p class="meta">Foods 12(15), 2871 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/foods12152871">doi:10.3390/foods12152871</a></p>
</div>
