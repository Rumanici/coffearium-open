## What porosity means in a coffee bean

Porosity is the share of a material's volume taken up by empty space. A green coffee seed has almost none: it is a dense, hard tissue.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> A roasted bean is full of holes.

Roasting changes the dense green structure into a very porous one.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2018 study, citing earlier work, gives a porosity of about 0.5 for dark roasts. That means about half of the bean's volume is pore space.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The pores matter after the roast. They hold the carbon dioxide formed in the roast and let oil and gas move out of the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Together with brittleness, they make the bean easy to grind.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## The green seed: a dense block of cells

The part of the seed that is roasted is the endosperm, a storage tissue. It is built of cells, each enclosed by a wall.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The cells are about 25–50 µm across.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

The cell walls of coffee are unusually thick compared with those of other plants.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> They are made mainly of polysaccharides – long chains of sugars – and carry reinforcement rings.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> There are no spaces between the cells.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> This is why green coffee is so hard.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

Inside each cell is the cytoplasm. According to a 2000 thesis review, it holds mainly lipids, proteins, carbohydrates and caffeine.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The oil sits in tiny oil bodies of about 0.2–2.5 µm.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A roasting textbook chapter places them along the cell walls.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Fine channels called plasmodesmata cross the walls in places and connect neighbouring cells.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## What roasting does to the cells

During roasting, water turns to steam and chemical reactions form carbon dioxide inside the cells. The gas pressure pushes the cells to expand.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The thick walls hold against it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The roast gases and CO₂ entry covers where the gas comes from.

The walls are not always stiff. Depending on moisture and temperature, their polysaccharides are glassy (rigid) or rubbery (soft).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the textbook chapter, the bean swells while the walls are rubbery.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The glass–rubber transition entry explains this change of state.

Inside, the cells are hollowed out. The shrinking cytoplasm is pushed against the walls, and a large gas-filled void opens in the centre of each cell.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A thin layer of cytoplasm with small bubbles stays along the walls.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The walls themselves become porous, with pores far too small to see in images.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## How much the bean swells

As the cells inflate, the whole bean grows. A 2020 paper gives an increase of about 50% to almost 100% in volume.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In a 2018 drum study, beans reached up to 1.8 times their green volume.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

The bean also loses mass, mostly as water and gas. So its density falls. A 2018 study, citing earlier work, gives about 1.3 g/cm³ for green and about 0.7 g/cm³ for dark-roasted beans.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

According to the textbook chapter, the darker the roast, the more volume and porosity are created, but swelling levels off at some point.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The bean size and density entry covers green and roasted density in more detail.

## Why the pores matter after the roast

**Gas.** The pores hold much of the carbon dioxide formed in the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It sits in the large voids and in the porous cell walls, and it leaves slowly during storage.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The degassing entry describes how.

**Oil.** Roasting breaks up the oil bodies.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Gas pressure then pushes the oil through tiny channels in the walls to the surface, faster in dark roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The coffee oil entry covers this.

**Grinding.** According to a grinding chapter, darker and faster roasts are more porous and brittle. They grind easily but give many fines.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In one study, the smallest particles were about the size of a cell: fragments of cells.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Extraction.** According to the textbook chapter, fast-roasted beans generally give more soluble matter on extraction. This may be because more solubles form, because water reaches them more easily in the porous structure, or both.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Pressure against the cell walls

**Driving force.** Water vapour and carbon dioxide form inside the bean faster than they can leave, so pressure builds up; the gas and vapour together drive expansion.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> A doctoral thesis describes green coffee as a collection of small reactor units whose thick walls withstand a large pressure build-up.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Estimates of the internal pressure are discussed in the roast gases and CO₂ entry.

**Resistance.** The thick walls hold against the pressure.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the textbook chapter, their polysaccharides change gradually between glassy and rubbery states with moisture and temperature; in principle the bean passes from glassy to rubbery and back to glassy, and it swells while rubbery.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The extent of swelling is therefore the result of gas formation against wall resistance, and the roast profile decides it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> An SCA handbook places the glass transition around first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Continuous, not explosive.** Unlike popcorn, coffee beans swell steadily rather than in a sudden burst.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In arabica beans roasted in a drum, expansion was isotropic at drum temperatures above 220 °C, and density fell linearly with moisture content, more steeply at higher temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Walls intact, tissue cracked.** A roasting chapter states that the framework of cell walls remains intact while the cells are excavated.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2008 thesis notes that no cell wall disruption was seen in scanning electron microscopy and attributes this to the rubbery state of the walls at high temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Cracks do form in the tissue: in microtomography, mainly in the outer regions of the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> First crack and second crack describe the audible events.

## Macropores and micropores

**Macropores.** The large voids at the centres of the excavated cells are called macropores.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In a cryo-electron micrograph of roasted tissue, a large gas-filled space occupies the centre of each cell, and smaller voids sit in the layer of cytoplasm along the walls.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The layer thins as roasting continues, because more cell mass turns into gas and water vapour and the cells grow.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Micropores.** The cell walls of roasted coffee are porous too. These micropores are too small to be imaged and are measured indirectly by mercury porosimetry.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2000 thesis review cites microscopy that found no pre-existing channels in the walls of green beans, and states that little was known about how micropores form during roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Fine micropores are assumed to carry the oil to the surface.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**What becomes of the plasmodesmata.** The same thesis states in its literature review that their fate during roasting was unknown. In its own electron micrographs, plasmodesmata channels were still present in some spots of roasted cell walls. Whether they stay open for mass transfer was unclear, and the author did not consider them a key route.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Cell size.** In ground coffee, a 2023 microtomography study found the fines peak at the size of a cell for every grind, and read the fines as cell fragments.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Fast and slow roasts

Roast speed changes the structure as well as the flavour.

- **Volume and pores.** In a study of two hot-air processes roasted to the same degree, high-temperature roasting gave greater bean volume, greater pore volume and larger micropores in the cell walls than low-temperature roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Density.** According to the textbook chapter, fast-roasted beans have much greater volume and porosity and lower density than slow-roasted beans of the same colour.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2008 thesis found the same direction in its laboratory roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Homogeneity.** A grinding chapter describes slowly roasted beans as having a more homogeneous matrix and pore sizes and a higher density.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Profile shape.** At identical final colour and roasting time, a slow-start, fast-finish profile usually gave more bean volume and porosity than a traditional profile, according to the textbook chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Oil.** Fast-roasted beans show a considerably stronger tendency for oil sweating.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

The gravimetric degassing study roasted one arabica to three roast degrees at three speeds each.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> For medium and dark roasts, faster roasts degassed faster; for light roasts there was no such trend.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The authors consider differences in porosity the probable cause.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Consequences for degassing, grinding and extraction

**Degassing.** According to the SCA handbook, gas held in macropores leaves by pressure-driven flow, while gas in the micropores of the walls leaves by slower diffusion.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The gravimetric study, citing earlier work, states that total pore volume increases almost linearly with roasting time.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Its authors suggest that more of the gas in dark roasts sits in large pores that grinding destroys, which would explain the larger loss on grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A water-quench study found no correlation between pore size distribution and degassing behaviour.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> The degassing entry gives the release data.

**Grinding.** The grinding chapter names roast degree as the most decisive bean property for grinding.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Porosity and brittleness rise with roast degree, especially in short roasts; such beans grind easily but give a wide particle size distribution with many fines.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Slowly roasted, denser beans need more energy to grind but grind more evenly.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Particle size and fines covers the consequences in the bed.

**Extraction.** In the textbook chapter's account, fast-roasted beans generally yield more soluble matter, possibly through more solubles, easier access for water in the porous structure, or both.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry separated these two causes.

**Staling.** The SCA handbook lists porosity among the factors that influence how roasted coffee interacts with its environment during storage.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> See freshness and storage.

## Measurement methods

| Method | What it shows | Limits | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mercury intrusion porosimetry | Pore volume and pore size distribution, including micropores in the cell walls | Shows the internal pore system, not surface porosity | Schenker et al.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>; Baggenstoss<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> |
| Volumetry (oil displacement) | Bean volume and density | Oil must not soak into the pores during weighing | Schenker et al.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>; Baggenstoss<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> |
| Electron microscopy (SEM, cryo-SEM) | Cell walls, cytoplasm and voids; oil droplets on the surface | Sample preparation can remove cytoplasm | Schenker and Rothgeb<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> |
| Synchrotron X-ray microtomography | Pores and cracks in 3D, pore size distribution by bean region, without cutting | Not stated in the sources consulted | Pittia et al.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> |
| Laboratory X-ray microtomography | Macropores; packed beds of ground coffee | Resolution too low for the pores inside particles in one study | SCA handbook<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>; Mo et al.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> |

**Different methods, different pores.** Microtomography images the macropores; the micropores of the walls are only measured indirectly by porosimetry.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A 2008 thesis notes that porosimetry gives insight into the internal pore system but no information about surface porosity.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Porosity figures from different methods are therefore not directly comparable.

## Risks and defects

- **Over-expanded, oily beans.** Very fast roasting gives high volume and porosity and a stronger tendency for oil sweating.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2008 thesis notes that fast-roasted coffee is generally suspected to be more affected by lipid oxidation, because more oil migrates to the surface.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Fines from brittle beans.** Highly porous, brittle beans give a wide size distribution with many fines.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Faster gas loss.** In medium and dark roasts, faster roasts degassed faster from whole beans.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Moisture after quenching.** In one study, water-quenched coffees with higher moisture content degassed faster.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> See cooling and quenching.

## Open questions

- **Micropore origin.** How micropores form in the cell walls, and how gas and oil move from the bean core to the outside, was not answered in the theses consulted for this entry.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Plasmodesmata.** They were seen in some spots of roasted cell walls, but whether they carry gas or oil is unclear.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Porosity and degassing.** One study points to porosity as the probable cause of faster degassing in fast roasts; another found no correlation with pore size distribution (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Series of values.** No study consulted for this entry reported porosity across a series of roast degrees and speeds for one coffee in a form that could be charted here.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Schenker et al., 2000 – Pore structure and roasting conditions.</strong> Roasted beans in two hot-air processes to the same roast degree and compared them by volumetry, mercury porosimetry and electron microscopy. High-temperature roasting gave greater bean volume, greater pore volume and larger micropores in the cell walls.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 65(3), 452–457 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2000.tb16026.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2000.tb16026.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Pittia et al., 2011 – Synchrotron microtomography of beans.</strong> Imaged green and roasted beans in 3D without cutting them. Roasting created a porous structure through the whole bean, with pore shapes and sizes varying by region and cracks mainly in the outer regions.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 76(2), E222–E231 · <a href="https://research.unite.it/handle/11575/16850">repository record</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bustos-Vanegas et al., 2018 – Physical properties during roasting.</strong> Followed volume, surface area and density of arabica beans, suspended in the centre of a drum, every 20 s at five temperatures. Final volume reached up to 1.8 times the green volume, and expansion was isotropic above 220 °C.</p>
<p class="meta">Industrial Crops and Products 112, 839–845 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.indcrop.2017.12.015">doi:10.1016/j.indcrop.2017.12.015</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Smrke et al., 2018 – Degassing by roast degree and speed.</strong> Weighed whole beans of one arabica, roasted to three degrees at three speeds, as they degassed at 35 °C. Darker and, for medium and dark roasts, faster roasts released gas faster; the authors point to porosity as the probable cause.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 66(21), 5293–5300 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.7b03310">doi:10.1021/acs.jafc.7b03310</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Mo et al., 2023 – Cells in ground coffee.</strong> Measured four commercial grinds of a 100% arabica blend (one of them dark-roasted) by laser diffraction and X-ray microtomography. The fines peak sat at 25–50 µm for every grind, the size of a cell, which the authors read as cell fragments.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 13, 16374 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-42380-y">doi:10.1038/s41598-023-42380-y</a></p>
</div>
