  - Grafting arabica onto resistant rootstocks of C. canephora or C. liberica keeps trees productive on infested land.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## What coffee nematodes are

Nematodes are microscopic roundworms. Some live in the soil and feed on plant roots. On coffee, two groups do most of the damage: root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne*) and root-lesion nematodes (*Pratylenchus*).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

They live in the soil and enter the roots to feed.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Root-knot nematodes form feeding sites inside the root.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Lesion nematodes keep moving through the root and leave dead tissue behind.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Because the damage is underground, it is easy to miss. A 2008 book chapter states that lesion nematodes were long underestimated, because their symptoms are not specific.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Hawaii, early infection is hard to diagnose.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Which species, and where

Different regions face different species. In Brazil the main root-knot species are *M. exigua*, *M. incognita* and *M. paranaensis*.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> According to a 2002 conference paper, *M. arabicida* causes severe dieback in Costa Rica.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In Hawaii the pest is the Kona coffee root-knot nematode, *M. konaensis*.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> In 2023 a Kenyan study reported *M. hapla* on coffee in Kenya for the first time.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Lesion nematodes damage arabica and robusta in Guatemala, El Salvador, Indonesia and Vietnam, among other countries, according to the 2008 chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> *Pratylenchus coffeae* has been characterised from coffee in Vietnam.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> Often several species share one field.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Surveys show how common they can be. In Espírito Santo, Brazil, about 47% of sampled coffee plantations carried root-knot nematodes.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In that survey *M. exigua* was found only on arabica, and *M. incognita* mostly on robusta (conilon).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## What they do to the tree

The roots show the damage first. *M. exigua* forms small, rounded galls on the fine roots.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> *M. incognita* and *M. paranaensis* thicken the roots unevenly, often without typical galls, and the root bark peels.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> *M. paranaensis* also cracks and destroys roots.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> Lesion nematodes cause lesions: patches of dead tissue (necrosis).<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup>

Damaged roots take up less water and fewer nutrients.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Above ground the trees are stunted and yellow, and they look short of nutrients.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> In Hawaii, badly infested trees wilt, drop flowers and fruit, and die back.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> In the end, trees can die.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## How much crop is lost

Losses differ by species and site.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A 2023 study summarises documented losses at 10–35%, mostly from Latin American data.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A 2013 study and a 2020 Brazilian technical circular, both citing a 2004 field study, report losses of up to 45% from *M. exigua*: the study in a plantation in Rio de Janeiro state, the circular where no control is applied.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

Some trials measured the damage directly. In a Guatemalan trial with lesion nematodes, ungrafted plots yielded about one-third of grafted plots in 1995.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Half of the ungrafted, untreated plants had died by then.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In Hawaii, *M. konaensis* cut Kona coffee yields by an estimated 20–25%, according to a 2012 paper citing a 2002 estimate.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

Data from Africa and Asia are thin. A 2023 Kenyan study notes that damage levels in its region are largely undocumented.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## How they spread

Nematodes can move through the soil to new roots.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> People carry them much further. Once a nursery is infested, its seedlings spread root-knot nematodes efficiently.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Soil on machinery and running water also carry them.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

For this reason, Brazil regulates seedlings. Under a 2012 federal rule, finding *Meloidogyne* in one plant condemns the whole seedling lot, which must be destroyed.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In a 2021 survey of nurseries in Espírito Santo, *Meloidogyne* was still found.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## How producers manage them

Management starts before planting.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Brazilian guidance advises testing soil and roots to identify the species first.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Clean seedlings.** Producers plant nematode-free seedlings and avoid planting below infested blocks.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Nurseries can heat their substrate. In a 2023 Brazilian test, solar heating to 60 °C for 45 minutes eliminated *M. incognita* from inoculated soil.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Resistant rootstocks.** An arabica scion is grafted onto the roots of a resistant species. In Brazil the robusta rootstock Apoatã IAC 2258 is used; in Central America, Nemaya.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> In Hawaii, rootstocks of *C. liberica* are used.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Robusta rootstocks are not adapted to high altitudes.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Resistant arabica varieties.** Some arabica varieties resist certain species. Some lines from the Timor Hybrid carry a robusta gene against *M. exigua*.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> Semi-wild arabica from Ethiopia is a further source of resistance.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Nematicides.** In the field trials consulted for this entry, nematicides gave no significant yield gain.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> We describe their use; we do not recommend products. Integrated pest management covers how these tools combine across pests.

## Parasitism in the root

**Root-knot nematodes.** *Meloidogyne* species parasitise the roots and form feeding sites there.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Researchers count their eggs and second-stage juveniles (J2) in roots and soil.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Galls are the clearest response of the host plant, and they hamper the uptake of water and nutrients.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

The species differ in the damage they cause. In a greenhouse study reported in a 2020 technical circular of a Brazilian state research company, *M. exigua* formed rounded galls on new roots.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> *M. paranaensis* thickened new roots with lengthwise cracks and destroyed roots of seedlings.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The authors judge *M. exigua* the less aggressive of the two, because it causes no structural damage.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> In the Espírito Santo survey, roots with *M. incognita* or *M. paranaensis* showed irregular thickening without typical galls.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Some conilon areas showed root damage without any symptoms above ground.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Root-lesion nematodes.** *Pratylenchus* species are migratory and cause non-specific root necrosis, according to a 2008 chapter.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In Vietnam, *P. coffeae* was associated with chlorotic leaves, stunted growth and root lesions.<sup><a href="#src-s19">19</a></sup> In the Espírito Santo survey, *P. brachyurus* occurred together with a root-knot species in 71.4% of infested samples.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Measured damage and yield loss

**Guatemala, lesion nematodes.** A five-year trial on the southern slope of the Atitlán volcano, at 900 m, compared Caturra on its own roots with Caturra grafted onto seedling *C. canephora*.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In 1995 the grafted plots yielded 16,000 kg of coffee berries per hectare against 5,300 kg for ungrafted plots.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> That is about one-third (our calculation). In plots without nematicide, ungrafted roots held 132 nematodes per gram of root that year, grafted roots 23.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The most heavily infested ungrafted plots yielded 12 times less than the least infested.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Hawaii, root-knot nematodes.** In a trial with *M. konaensis*, the arabica cultivar Guatemala on its own roots yielded an estimated 0.05 kg of green coffee per tree in 1995 without nematicide (green coffee estimated as 20% of cherry weight).<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Grafted onto *C. dewevrei* (the name used in the paper), it yielded an estimated 1.93 kg.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Yields of Guatemala on its own roots fell by about 45% in the second year.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In a 13-year trial at the Kona Research Station, 81% of non-grafted Kona Typica trees died; all trees grafted onto the Meisner rootstock survived.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Brazil, M. exigua.** A 2010 field study in Rio de Janeiro state found that a high nematode population drastically reduced growth and yield.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Its authors recommend that resistant cultivars be planted only after the population has been reduced, to avoid large losses.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

## Nurseries and seedling rules

A 2023 Brazilian study notes that infected seeds and seedlings can bring nematodes into new areas.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Until 1980, methyl bromide was the main way to disinfest substrate for coffee seedlings; the study reports that its use was later prohibited because of risks to the environment and health.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

The same study tested a solar heater that warms the soil from above and below. Soil inoculated with *M. incognita* (150 litres, in an exchanger box 8 cm deep) was held at a minimum of 60 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> After 45 minutes, a bioassay with susceptible robusta seedlings over 180 days found the nematode eradicated.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The authors recommend 60 minutes as a safety margin.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Regulation targets the seedlings themselves. Brazil’s Normative Instruction No. 35 of 2012 condemns a whole seedling lot if *Meloidogyne* is found in a single plant, as reported in a 2021 study.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Under a 2016 ordinance, the plant health agency of Rondônia state (IDARON) requires laboratory analysis of nursery seedlings for nematodes.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Raising and handling seedlings is covered in germination and seedlings.

## Rootstocks and grafting

Grafting onto resistant rootstocks is described as the best short-term option in a 2002 conference paper.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A 2019 study from Brazil states that in infested areas, arabica cultivation has only been possible on resistant *C. canephora* rootstocks.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

| Rootstock | Species | Where used | Reported resistance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apoatã IAC 2258 | *C. canephora* | Brazil | *M. exigua*, *M. incognita*, *M. paranaensis*<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> |
| Nemaya | *C. canephora* (hybrid within the species) | Central America | Several root-knot species<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> |
| Fukunaga | *C. liberica* var. *dewevrei* | Hawaii (released 2001) | Tolerant to *M. konaensis*<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> |
| Meisner | *C. liberica* | Hawaii | Tolerant to *M. konaensis*<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> |

According to a 2020 Brazilian technical circular, Apoatã IAC 2258 was the only nematode-resistant rootstock cultivar available in Brazil.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> New *C. canephora* clones resistant to both *M. incognita* and *M. paranaensis* were selected in a 2019 study.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In Hawaii, the extension service considers grafting the only practical long-term solution once the nematode is established.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Grafting on Meisner did not lower cup quality in its trial.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

Rootstocks have limits. Robusta is not adapted to high altitudes, so resistant arabica would avoid grafting above about 800–1,000 m, according to a 2001 study.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Liberica as a rootstock is discussed in liberica and excelsa, where study results differ.

## Resistant arabica

**Mex-1.** A single major gene, *Mex-1*, from *C. canephora* gives resistance to *M. exigua* in arabica.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> It was mapped in a cross between the introgression line T5296 and a susceptible accession.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> T5296 is a Sarchimor, catalogued as Timor Hybrid CIFC 832/2 × Villa Sarchi.<sup><a href="#src-s20">20</a></sup> Its expression appeared incompletely dominant, and 14 linked molecular markers were found.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2013 study of the resistant *C. canephora* cultivar Apoatã concludes that its resistance involves a set of constitutive and induced defence responses, not only a hypersensitive reaction.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Ethiopian accessions.** Tested against a Guatemalan isolate of *M. incognita*, 40% of the semi-wild Ethiopian arabica accessions tested were fully resistant.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The reaction to a Brazilian isolate was similar.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Resistance was dominant, controlled by one gene or two complementary genes depending on the cross.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In Hawaii, all but one of the Ethiopian accessions tested were resistant to *M. konaensis*.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The material is described in Ethiopian landraces.

**Cultivars.** According to a 2020 Brazilian technical circular, Catiguá MG 3, IAPAR 59 and IAC 125 RN resist *M. exigua*, and IPR 100 and IPR 106 resist *M. paranaensis* and *M. incognita*.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The circular advises against cultivars described only as tolerant, because nematodes still multiply on them.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## Biological and chemical control

**Biological control.** In a 2020–2022 field trial in Kiambu County, Kenya, two fungi were drenched around arabica trees: *Trichoderma asperellum* and *Purpureocillium lilacinum*.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> After 12 months, *M. hapla* numbers in the roots had fallen significantly; numbers in the soil had not.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Yield was not measured.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The authors estimate the product cost at USD 23.43 per tree over the two years.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured a yield gain from biological control.

**Chemical control.** In the Guatemalan trial, terbufos reduced nematode numbers only in the first year.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> It lowered the death rate of ungrafted plants but did not raise yield significantly on any rootstock.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In Hawaii, fenamiphos gave no significant yield benefit, and its cost exceeded the return.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> As of 2024, the nematicides available to Hawaiian producers give limited suppression.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A 2002 conference paper describes nematicides as costly and environmentally risky, and their use as declining.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> No regulator’s list of approved or banned coffee nematicides was consulted for this entry; the status differs by country.

## Open questions

- **Losses outside Latin America.** Damage data for Africa are largely lacking, according to a 2023 Kenyan study<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>; no measured loss for Asian robusta was consulted for this entry.
- **Species identity.** In the Guatemalan trial the lesion nematode species was still being identified<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>; a 2002 paper reports unidentified root-knot populations in El Salvador and Guatemala.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Biological control in yield terms.** The Kenyan fungus trial reduced nematodes in roots but did not measure yield.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Durability of resistance.** No study consulted for this entry tested whether *Mex-1* or rootstock resistance breaks down over time.
- **Damage thresholds.** No study consulted for this entry gives a nematode density at which control pays.
