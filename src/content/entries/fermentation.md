  - In washed coffee, pulped seeds rest in a tank – with or without water – until the mucilage has broken down and can be washed off; Codex gives 12–36 hours, reviews 12–48 hours and 6–72 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s15">15</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## What fermentation does

After pulping, the seeds are still covered in sticky mucilage. In the washed process, they are left to ferment until microorganisms have broken the mucilage down and it can be washed off.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In wet fermentation, lactic acid bacteria dominate the microbial community.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> How the mucilage itself is taken apart is covered under mucilage.

Fermentation is not only a cleaning step. The microbes consume sugars and make acids, alcohols and aroma compounds, and some of these end up inside the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Length and conditions of fermentation therefore change the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Where it happens

- **Dry-tank fermentation** – pulped coffee is left in a tank without added water. In a Colombian trial, no water was added to the 30-litre fermentation tanks.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Underwater (submerged) fermentation** – the pulped coffee is covered with water.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In an Ecuadorian trial, about 150 kg of pulped coffee was submerged in clean water in a concrete tank.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Some farms add only a little water: in one Colombian study, about 4 litres were added to 10 kg of cherries after pulping.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **During natural drying** – whole cherries ferment on the drying yard. Acetic acid bacteria and yeasts dominate, and their products, such as gluconic acid and sugar alcohols, build up in the drying outer layers of the fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Cherries left in bags or heaps for days heat up and ferment quickly, which Codex links with quality loss and a higher risk of ochratoxin A.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Honey coffees** – fermentation can be stopped early so that only part of the mucilage is removed before drying. A Cenicafé trial found a 22–77% probability of defective cups in such honey coffees.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Controlled variants** – starter cultures, controlled temperature and sealed, oxygen-poor tanks are used to steer fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Anaerobic fermentation and carbonic maceration are covered in their own entries.

## What changes in the tank

1. **Sugars rise, then fall.** Sucrose, glucose and fructose are released from the mucilage and then consumed.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Microbes use sucrose first, then glucose and fructose, then citric and malic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
2. **Acids and alcohols appear.** Lactic acid, acetic acid, mannitol and ethanol accumulate, mostly after the first day.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
3. **pH falls.** In a Nicaraguan field study, pH started at 5.5–5.7 and fell sharply to about 4.6 as fermentation neared completion.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In a Yunnan trial, it fell from about 6.0–6.5 to about 4.0.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
4. **The mucilage lets go.** The texture changes from slimy to gritty.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The pectin chemistry behind this is covered under mucilage.

## Knowing when to stop

The end point is when the mucilage is degraded and the beans are washable.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Producers use four kinds of test:

- **Touch** – a handful of beans is rubbed between the fingers; when the mucilage is gone they feel rough and gritty instead of slimy.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Stick** – a stick is pushed into the fermenting mass; the hole it leaves is read as a sign of the end point.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **pH** – the sharp pH drop near the end can predict completion and help prevent over-fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> One review reports that fermentation was complete within about 2 hours of the mass reaching pH 5.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Volume** – Cenicafé's Fermaestro measures how far a sample of the mass settles as degraded mucilage drains away.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Effect on the cup

Well-run fermentation can raise quality. In controlled trials in Yunnan and Ecuador, longer fermentation gave a fruitier cup with more acidity.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Run too long, it spoils the coffee: over-fermentation affects taste and flavour,<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> and Cenicafé names vinegary and "fermented" as the cup defects tied to this stage.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

Pooling 22 studies published 2020–2025, a 2025 meta-analysis found that fermentation, spontaneous or with starter cultures, raised SCA scores by 0.6–1.4 points on average over the controls. The fermentation system, oxygen and time made a difference; the processing method (washed, natural or honey) did not on its own.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## Kinetics

The published curves differ between sites, but the pattern repeats: sugars peak and fall, acids rise late, and pH drops.

**pH.** In Nicaragua, seven farm batches started at pH 5.5–5.7 and fell sharply to about 4.6 near completion, while glucose fell throughout most batches and ethanol or lactic acid rose sharply near the end.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> In Yunnan (1,300 m), pH fell from about 6.0–6.5 to about 4.0 in every variant, with the major drop after 36 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In Ecuador, pH was about 4.5 after 16 hours and 4.0 after 36 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In Nariño, Colombia (1,959 m), lactic acid rose to 0.37 g/L over 48 hours and pH fell from 5.2 to 4.2.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In a Colombian dry-tank trial, the mass started at pH 5.53–5.67 depending on the variety (5.61 for Castillo); spontaneous fermentation ended below pH 3.5, while fermentation held at 15 °C levelled off near pH 4.0.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Sugars and °Brix.** In Nariño, the pulp started at 5.3 °Brix, with 0.82 g/L glucose and 1.16 g/L fructose; °Brix and sugars rose during the first 12 hours of fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In Yunnan, sucrose peaked within 8 hours and glucose and fructose after 36 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Temperature.** The Yunnan fermentations started at about 15 °C, followed the day–night cycle and ended at about 11–13 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In the Colombian dry-tank trial, at an ambient average of 26 °C (23–31 °C), the spontaneously fermenting mass warmed to close to 26 °C by the end.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Metabolites

**In the fermentation water.** At the end of fermentation of depulped coffee in Yunnan, the water held 3.0–3.2 mg/mL mannitol, 1.7–1.8 mg/mL lactic acid and 0.7–0.9 mg/mL acetic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> After 64 hours in Ecuador, the most abundant compounds in the water were lactic acid (8.2 mg/mL) and 5-ketogluconic acid (5.7 mg/mL), with acetic acid at 2.4 mg/mL, mannitol at 1.9 mg/mL and ethanol at 1.8 mg/mL.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**In the beans.** After 64 hours, the Ecuadorian beans contained 4.6 g/kg ethanol, 4.5 g/kg acetic acid, 3.6 g/kg mannitol and 2.2 g/kg lactic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Lactic acid, acetic acid and mannitol made in the mucilage were also found in the endosperm in an earlier study.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Sugars leave the seed.** In the same Ecuadorian study, fructose, glucose and sucrose in the endosperm fell significantly during fermentation; the authors link this to the seed itself consuming sugar anaerobically under water, and a further drop during soaking to osmotic loss into the water.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Drying experiments point the same way: glucose and fructose in washed coffee fell within the first day of drying, with no process water present.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Both mechanisms are discussed under washed process.

**Acids that help and hurt.** Low acetic acid gives a clean, sweet taste; above about 2 mg/mL it turns vinegary.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Propionic and butanoic acids can give an onion flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The microbial groups behind these products are described under microorganisms.

## Temperature and altitude

Temperature controls the speed. In a Colombian trial with three varieties, more than 95% mucilage degradation took 16.8–17.7 hours in spontaneous fermentation, 17.5–20.0 hours at 30 °C and 41.3–42.1 hours at 15 °C; for Castillo the times were 17.7 ± 0.7, 20.0 ± 1.2 and 42.1 ± 1.5 hours (means with 95% confidence intervals).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Only the 15 °C treatment differed significantly from spontaneous fermentation; holding the mass at 15 °C prolonged it by more than 24 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Average cup scores for the controlled fermentations were 82.7–83.8 SCA points (spontaneous controls 79.7–83.8), and quality did not differ significantly between controlled and spontaneous fermentation or between the two temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> This fits the wide range of reported times, which a 2023 review attributes to climate.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Altitude also shifts the chemistry. In self-induced anaerobic fermentations of one variety from one Brazilian region, coffee from lower altitudes had more volatile alcohols, while coffee from higher altitudes had more esters, aldehydes and phenolics.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Acetic, malic and citric acid were higher at the two upper sites (1,200 and 1,400 m), and lactic acid was highest at 1,400 m.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> At 48 hours the mass was at 21.6–25 °C according to the paper's table (18–25 °C in its results text).<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

## Water, climate and soaking

**Dry tank or submerged.** None of the sources consulted for this entry compares dry-tank and submerged fermentation of the same coffee, or ties the choice between them to climate. What they do tie to climate is duration. A 2023 review gives 6–72 hours for submerged fermentation depending on the climate.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In Kenya, fermentation takes 1 to 4 days depending on the weather, faster on warm days and slower on cold ones.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> Temperature itself is well documented: in a dry-tank trial, fermentation at 15 °C took more than 24 hours longer than spontaneous fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Whether adding water slows fermentation by cooling or diluting the mass has not been measured head-to-head.

**Soaking after fermentation.** Some mills hold the washed parchment under clean water again, from overnight up to, rarely, 48 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Where fermentation came first, this is sometimes called secondary fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> In Kenya, soaking for about 12 hours after fermentation was found to improve colour and taste.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In Yunnan, a 24-hour soak tempered the effect of longer fermentation and evened out green-coffee quality.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> In Ecuador, ethanol, fructose, glucose, lactic acid and mannitol in the beans dropped during a 24-hour soak.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> No primary source consulted for this entry describes a two-stage ("double") soak.

## Monitoring protocol

A sourced routine for a single batch:

1. **Record the start** – time, mass temperature and pH of the mass or water. Research trials log temperature and pH continuously.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
2. **Track pH** – expect a slow fall followed by a sharp drop near the end.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Once the mass reaches pH 5, check often: completion followed within about 2 hours in one study.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
3. **Track temperature** – cool mass means slow fermentation; in one trial, fermentation at 15 °C took more than 24 hours longer than spontaneous fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
4. **Test the texture** – rub beans between the fingers and look for the change from slimy to gritty.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
5. **Measure settling** – with a Fermaestro, lift the device, drop it three times from about 3 cm and wash when the empty space reaches the 85 mm mark.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In field tests, this put fermentation at about 11.5–20 hours, against farmer estimates of 10–72 hours.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
6. **Wash promptly** – delay after the end point raises the risk of fermentation defects.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## Risks and defects

- **Vinegary** – too much acetic acid, above about 2 mg/mL, gives a vinegary taste.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Cenicafé names "vinagre" among the fermentation defects.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Onion** – propionic and butanoic acids can give an onion flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **"Fermented"** – the "fermento" defect in the cup is tied to the mucilage fermentation stage.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Holding coffee in the tank longer than necessary causes over-fermentation, which affects taste and flavour.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Stinker beans** – stinkers are caused by excessively long fermentation or by unclean water.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Heaped cherries** – cherries kept in bags or heaps heat up and ferment in an uncontrolled way, with quality loss and a higher ochratoxin A risk.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Partial mucilage removal** – honey coffees made by stopping fermentation early had a 22–77% probability of defective cups in one trial.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Migration into the seed.** Ethanol, acetic acid, mannitol and lactic acid have been measured in beans after fermentation.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> In dry processing, the data suggest a slow migration.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> How far these compounds penetrate, and how much survives roasting to shape flavour, is still debated.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Dry tank versus submerged.** The sources report both, but none in this entry compares them directly on the same coffee or links the choice to climate; only fermentation time is linked to weather.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>
- **Tolerance for delay.** Cenicafé's own studies disagree on how quickly defects appear after the washing point (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Optimum length.** Codex advises short fermentation,<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> while trials found that longer fermentation improved fruitiness and acidity.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In a 2025 meta-analysis of 22 studies, runs of 121 hours or more gained most, but the model left most of the variation between studies unexplained.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Soares et al., 2025 – Meta-analysis of 22 studies.</strong> Pools 236 comparisons of fermented coffee with controls, published 2020–2025. Fermentation raised SCA scores by 0.6–1.4 points; the fermentation system, oxygen and time explained part of the differences, the processing method did not, and most of the variation stayed unexplained.</p>
<p class="meta">Trends in Food Science &amp; Technology 163, 105151 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tifs.2025.105151">doi:10.1016/j.tifs.2025.105151</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Jackels & Jackels, 2005 – Chemical indicators of fermentation.</strong> Follows seven farm-scale batches in Nicaragua, measuring temperature, pH, glucose, ethanol and lactic acid. The sharp pH drop near completion is proposed as a practical way to time washing and avoid over-fermentation.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 70(5) · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb09960.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb09960.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Zhang et al., 2019 – From cherry to cup in Ecuador.</strong> Compares 16-hour and 64-hour submerged fermentation of Typica, measuring microbes and metabolites in water and beans. Extended fermentation put more ethanol, acetic acid, mannitol and lactic acid into the beans and gave a significantly fruitier cup; acidity also scored higher, though less clearly.</p>
<p class="meta">Applied and Environmental Microbiology 85(6), e02635-18 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.02635-18">doi:10.1128/AEM.02635-18</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Zhang et al., 2019 – Processing parameters in Yunnan.</strong> Tests fermentation length, mechanical demucilaging and soaking on Catimor at 1,300 m. pH fell from about 6.0–6.5 to about 4.0 with the main drop after 36 hours, and longer fermentation gave a fruitier, more acidic cup.</p>
<p class="meta">Frontiers in Microbiology 10, 2621 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2019.02621">doi:10.3389/fmicb.2019.02621</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Peñuela-Martínez et al., 2023 – Controlled temperature.</strong> Ferments three Arabica varieties in dry tanks at 15 °C, 30 °C and ambient temperature. Cold fermentation took 41.3–42.1 hours against 16.8–17.7 hours for spontaneous fermentation and ended at a higher pH, yet cup quality did not differ significantly between treatments, with treatment averages of 82.7–83.8 SCA points.</p>
<p class="meta">Fermentation 9(11), 976 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/fermentation9110976">doi:10.3390/fermentation9110976</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Sanz-Uribe & Velásquez-Henao, 2022 – Incomplete and prolonged fermentation.</strong> Uses the Fermaestro to stop fermentation early for honey coffees or extend it by 2–8 hours past the washing point. Honey coffees carried a high risk of defects, while prolonged fermentations rarely produced defective cups.</p>
<p class="meta">Revista Cenicafé 73(1) · <a href="https://doi.org/10.38141/10778/73105">doi:10.38141/10778/73105</a></p>
</div>
