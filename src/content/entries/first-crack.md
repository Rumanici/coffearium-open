## What first crack is

First crack is the first audible cracking of the beans during a roast. In sensory studies it is timed by ear, as the moment a popping sound is first heard.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The sound is often compared to popcorn popping.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

In a 2024 study, first crack ended the phase that began once the beans had turned from greenish yellow to yellowish brown.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> It is step 4 of 6 in Coffearium's roast steps. Previous step: the browning phase. Next step: development time.

First crack is not a single pop but a series of cracks, which a news release on a 2014 acoustics study calls a “chorus”.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> According to a roasting textbook chapter, roasters notice at this point the first aromas of true coffee character and a smoother bean surface.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Why the bean cracks

A coffee bean is made of cells with thick walls. During roasting, water turns to steam inside the cells, and chemical reactions form carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The gas cannot leave quickly, so pressure builds up inside the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

According to a roasting textbook chapter, the cell walls stretch and the bean swells.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> At some point the structure gives way in places: small cracks open, release a little gas in a sudden burst, and this is what roasters hear.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In images of roasted beans, cracks lie mainly in the outer regions of the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## What roasters hear and see

Recordings show that first crack and second crack sound different. In one study, as reported in a news release, first crack was louder than second crack, and second crack had more cracks per unit of time.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Second crack comes later, when the beans are much darker.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

According to the textbook chapter, roasters rely on sight and smell during the roast. They watch colour and surface texture and smell the beans from the trier.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A roasting textbook chapter recommends logging the time and temperature of first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## At what temperature

A study of roast colour reports bean cracking at 180–200 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It does not say whether this is the temperature of the beans or of the air.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

In a laboratory roaster with a probe inside a single bean, carbon dioxide and water from chemical reactions began to form in relevant amounts at a bean core temperature of 180–200 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> This is the same range as the cracking temperature above.

According to a roasting textbook chapter, production roasters usually cannot measure the bean core. Their probe sits in the bean mass and touches both beans and hot air, so it reads a mix of the two.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The reading at first crack therefore depends on the machine and cannot simply be compared between roasters.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The roast-profile entry explains the probes in detail.

## Why roasters use it as a landmark

Because it can be heard, first crack serves as a marker on the roast log.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Roasters divide the roast into the time to first crack and the time after it, the development time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

The time after first crack matters for flavour. In eight sensory studies, roast colour predicted flavour best; at the same colour, development time mattered more than the time to first crack.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Roast degree and colour and development time each have their own entry.

After first crack, the bean is described as particularly sensitive to heat, because the escaping steam dries it out and browning moves faster.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> According to one textbook chapter, the senses become more crucial here, because the roaster must now decide when to drop the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Pressure, cell walls and fracture

**Pressure.** Roasting forms a large amount of gas, mostly carbon dioxide, and much of it stays trapped in the bean; a roasting textbook chapter concludes that the trapped gas must cause high pressure inside the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A doctoral thesis on hot-air roasting traces bean expansion to a rapid pressure build-up from water vapour and gas formed inside the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> The pressure values in these sources come from gas measurements and model calculations<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>; no direct measurement was found for this entry, so we give no figure here (see the roasting overview).

**Cell walls.** The polysaccharides of the cell walls are glassy or rubbery depending on moisture and temperature. In principle the bean passes from glassy to rubbery and back to glassy during roasting, and it swells while rubbery.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> An SCA handbook places this glass transition around first crack, with the cells inflated by carbon dioxide.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Swelling is continuous, not a sudden popcorn-like burst; the walls stretch rather than break.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Fracture.** According to the same chapter, minor structural breakdowns and cracks occur during the final roasting stages and release a tiny quantity of gas in a sudden microburst, heard as cracking and popping.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Synchrotron microtomography of roasted beans found cracks mainly in the outer regions of the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> A heat-transfer study states plainly that a detailed physical explanation for first crack has not yet been found.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Older descriptions.** A literature review in the thesis notes that earlier authors described bean expansion as progressive but including a “popping phase”, and that it is unclear whether that term referred only to the sounds.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>

## Water, carbon dioxide and mass loss

In a laboratory fluid bed, the exhaust air was analysed continuously for carbon dioxide and water vapour, and a fine thermocouple recorded the bean core temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Below a core temperature of 180–200 °C there was no significant increase in carbon dioxide or in water formed by chemical reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Reaction water made up 41% of all water evaporated in the fast roast and 36% in the slow roast.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

The two ranges coincide: bean cracking is reported at the same temperatures at which reaction water and carbon dioxide begin to form in relevant amounts.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> According to an SCA handbook, the Maillard reaction contributes most of the carbon dioxide up to and around first crack, and pyrolysis adds more only in darker roasts.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Degassing after the roast is covered in its own entry.

Two sensory papers call first crack a release of steam pressure that expels steam with other volatile compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> No source consulted for this entry measured how much mass a bean loses at the moment of cracking. Roast loss over the whole roast is covered in its own entry.

**Chemistry peaks around first crack.** In a 5 kg drum, titratable acidity peaked during first crack in every profile and coffee tested and fell back by second crack.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The acids in coffee entry and the Maillard reaction entry describe what forms and breaks down around this point.

## Heat release around first crack

Early in the roast, energy goes into heating the beans and evaporating water: the roast is endothermic.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Later it becomes progressively more exothermic and needs much less heat input.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Where the switch lies, and whether it coincides with first crack, is not agreed.

- **At first crack.** A roasting textbook chapter says the first crack corresponds to the beans' switch from endothermic to exothermic, so a roaster may adjust the flame at that point.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **A separate phase after drying.** A second chapter of the same book describes roasting as an endothermic drying phase followed by an exothermic phase in which most flavour components form.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Nearly 200 °C.** A heat-transfer model states that exothermic reactions such as Maillard reactions can begin once the bean reaches nearly 200 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Above 150 °C.** A 2025 single-bean model of a drum roaster assumed exothermic heat above 150 °C, taken from earlier literature.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **Different for each coffee.** Differential scanning calorimetry of six green coffees showed an endothermic phase followed by an exothermic one, but the change happened at a different temperature for each sample; the authors relate it to the beans' physical properties.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

None of the sources consulted measured heat release in a bean at the moment of first crack. In the sources, the cracks are described as structural breakdowns<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>, and the heat as coming from chemical reactions such as Maillard reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> On the roast log, the rate of rise often changes around first crack; the roast-profile entry discusses the practitioner terms “crash” and “flick” and how little is known about them. In one 2024 study, a sudden increase in the rate of rise after first crack was described as typically associated with the onset of exothermic reactions.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## The sound of first crack

**Two choruses.** A 2014 acoustics study recorded the cracks in a small electric drum roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> As reported in the news release on the study, first crack had higher acoustic amplitudes than second crack, and second crack had a higher rate of cracks.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The related patent application describes the same kind of recordings: first crack carried more low-frequency energy, and second crack a flatter spectrum, lower in amplitude, with its spectral peak at a much higher frequency.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

**Not the same on every roaster.** On a much larger gas-heated roaster, a 2018 conference paper found first crack dominated by higher-frequency components and second crack by lower ones – the opposite pattern.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Its authors found second crack hard to identify and called for further measurements.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> The two set-ups differed in size and heating: a small consumer electric drum roaster and a much larger gas-heated one.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Automatic detection.** The patent application describes a roasting system with a microphone that records crack sounds, processes them and uses them to control the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> The 2018 study, by an Italian university group working with two companies, recorded crack sounds on the larger roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> No peer-reviewed study of automatic first-crack detection with a stated accuracy was found for this entry.

## Variation between coffees and roasts

**Time to first crack.** In eight sensory studies, airflow was constant and the charge temperature was the same within each study, except in Study 1.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The times listed in the paper's Table 2 show how much the timing can vary.

In the defect study (Study 1, a Kenyan coffee), the charge air ranged from 135 to 275 °C. The “Scorched” roast, charged at 275 °C, reached first crack after about 6 min; the “Underdeveloped” roast, charged at 135 °C, after about 18 min.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The times we derived from the defect paper (total roast time minus development time; our calculation) differ from the multi-study table by up to 80 s, so we give rounded values.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>

**Density, moisture, origin and processing.** According to a roasting textbook chapter, green bean density, moisture content, screen size and shape all factor into the roast plan.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In a calorimetry study, six green coffees differing in density and moisture (basis not stated) switched from endothermic to exothermic at different temperatures, and the authors conclude that denser, moister samples needed more heat.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured how density, moisture, origin or processing method shift the timing or temperature of first crack itself. The sensory evidence on first crack and development comes from washed arabica only.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Fast and slow roasts.** Fast roasting raises gas formation and makes the beans expand faster; at the same colour, fast-roasted beans have much greater volume and porosity.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> How this changes the cracking itself has not been measured in the sources found.

## Fluid bed and drum

According to a roasting textbook chapter, convection dominates heat transfer in a fluidised-bed roaster; in a drum roaster, a substantial share of heat may be transferred by conduction from the hot walls.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The detailed measurements of carbon dioxide and reaction water around 180–200 °C come from a laboratory fluid bed, roasting fast (170 s) and slow (720 s).<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The sensory studies that time first crack by ear used drum roasters and one convection roaster.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

According to a second chapter of the same textbook, a similar temperature profile applied in a laboratory fluid bed and a drum roaster gave coffees with similar physical properties and aroma formation.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry compared the sound or timing of first crack between fluid beds and drums. The fluid-bed roaster and drum roaster entries describe the machines.

## Risks and defects

- **Stopping too soon after first crack.** In a defect study, a “Light” roast followed the normal curve but was stopped 10 s after first crack. It was much lighter in colour and the sample consumers liked least.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> See roast defects.
- **Losing heat during first crack.** One profile in a 2024 study stopped the roast energy during first crack, to mimic an undesired loss of gas flow; it was assessed for titratable acidity only.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Mistaking a probe value for first crack.** Probe readings at first crack are machine-specific<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>; a temperature copied from another roaster does not mark first crack on one's own machine.
- **Running on after first crack.** According to a roasting textbook chapter, the roast speeds up in the later, exothermic stages, and a few seconds can separate the desired roast degree from an over-roasted product.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Mechanism.** A detailed physical explanation of first crack has not been found.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> No direct measurement of pressure inside a bean was found for this entry; the values in the sources consulted come from gas measurements and calculations.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **Heat at first crack.** Whether first crack coincides with the switch to exothermic reactions is disputed (see Contested).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Acoustics.** The two recordings found disagree on which crack carries more high-frequency energy; one is described in a patent application, the other in a conference paper without stated peer review.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>
- **Green coffee properties.** How density, moisture, origin and processing shift first crack has not been measured in the sources found.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- **Bean versus probe.** Studies report the bean core temperature in laboratory roasters<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>, an unstated basis<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>, or a production probe that reads a mix of bean and air.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Geiger et al., 2005 – Carbon dioxide and water during roasting.</strong> Roasted 100 g batches fast and slow in a laboratory fluid bed and analysed the exhaust air continuously, with a thermocouple in the bean core. Carbon dioxide and reaction water rose significantly only from a core temperature of 180–200 °C, the range in which beans are reported to crack.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 70(2), E124–E130 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb07084.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2005.tb07084.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Münchow et al., 2020 – Time to first crack versus development.</strong> Pools eight sensory studies of washed arabica, timing first crack by ear. Colour predicted flavour best; at constant colour, development time after first crack mattered more than the time to first crack.</p>
<p class="meta">Beverages 6(2), 29 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/beverages6020029">doi:10.3390/beverages6020029</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Anokye-Bempah et al., 2024 – Acidity peaks at first crack.</strong> Sampled beans every minute of 16-minute roasts on a 5 kg drum, with up to seven profiles and three coffees. Titratable acidity always peaked during first crack and fell back by second crack.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 14, 8237 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-57256-y">doi:10.1038/s41598-024-57256-y</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Pittia et al., 2011 – Inside the roasted bean.</strong> Imaged green and roasted beans by synchrotron X-ray microtomography. Roasting created a porous structure throughout the bean, with cracks mainly in the outer regions.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 76(2), E222–E231 · <a href="https://research.unite.it/handle/11575/16850">repository record</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Fadai et al., 2017 – A model of the roasting bean.</strong> Models heat and moisture transport, carbon dioxide formation and changing porosity in a single bean. Its background section notes that no detailed physical explanation of first crack has been found and places the start of exothermic reactions at nearly 200 °C.</p>
<p class="meta">International Journal of Heat and Mass Transfer 104, 787–799 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2016.08.083">doi:10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2016.08.083</a></p>
