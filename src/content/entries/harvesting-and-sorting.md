## How ripeness is judged

A coffee cherry is ready when its skin has turned from green to its ripe colour: bright red to deep red, or yellow.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The colour change comes from the replacement of chlorophyll in the skin by red flavonoid pigments.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> As the fruit ripens it also becomes softer and sweeter: in a Cenicafé study, firmness fell steadily and soluble solids (°Brix) rose through ripening, with the highest mean value in overripe fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

Ripeness is classified by colour, in research with a colorimeter.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Softness and °Brix are also measured in research;<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> the sources consulted for this entry give no agreed °Brix target for picking (see Deep dive).

## Harvest methods

The Codex code of practice names four systems:<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

1. **Single-pass stripping** – all fruit-bearing branches are stripped at once.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
2. **Multi-pass stripping** – only branches bearing mainly ripe cherries are stripped on each pass.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
3. **Multi-pass selective picking** (finger picking) – only ripe cherries are picked.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It needs several picking rounds and a lot of labour, but gives uniform cherries.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Kenya, for example, harvests by selective picking of ripe berries.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
4. **Mechanical harvesting** – machines take the fruit all at once.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Stripping, by hand or by machine, mixes unripe and overripe cherries into the harvest, and the lot may miss the desired quality.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In a Brazilian trial on microterraces in sloping land, one unilateral harvester did the work of about 24 manual pickers.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Fruit on the ground.** The Codex code advises against collecting fallen cherries, especially in humid conditions, because fungal growth on them can lead to ochratoxin A. In wet climates only same-day collection from the ground is acceptable.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## From tree to mill

Coffee cherries should be processed as soon as possible after harvesting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Guidance agrees on the direction but differs in detail:

- Codex: as soon as possible; holding fruit in bags or heaps, sometimes for up to a week, causes high temperatures and quick fermentation, quality loss and a higher risk of ochratoxin A.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- FAO manual for Lao PDR: process the same day, and do not mix with the previous day's harvest.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>
- Kenya: cherry is taken to the factory immediately after harvesting,<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> and pulping must be done within 10 hours of harvesting.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- Cenicafé (Colombia): do not leave harvested cherry more than 6 hours before pulping.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Leaving cherry more than 12 hours or overnight is listed as a quality risk.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Sorting before processing

Low-quality cherries – unripe, overripe or diseased – should be removed before processing.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Two steps are common:

- **Hand sorting.** In Kenya the cherry is sorted before pulping to remove immature, diseased, insect-damaged and dry berries, and leaves, twigs and other foreign matter.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Flotation.** Cherries are put in water and the floaters are separated: overripe and undeveloped cherries, sticks and leaves.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Codex names visual sorting or water separation as the two ways to remove low-quality cherries.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Not separating floaters, borer-damaged fruit, immature fruit and debris is listed as a quality risk.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

For natural coffee the same rule applies before drying: immature and over-mature cherries should be sorted out.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Ripening physiology

**Timing.** In the Cenicafé study on var. Colombia, fruit was sampled weekly from 182 to 231 days after flowering. Green stages ran from 182 to 203 days, semi-ripe (*pintón*) came at 210 days, ripe at 217 days, overripe at 224 days and dry at 231 days.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> A later Cenicafé paper cites maximum fruit development between 220 and 240 days after anthesis.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**Mass and firmness.** Fresh weight rose from 1.33 g in the early green stages to 1.99 g at ripeness, fell to 1.88 g when overripe and to 0.66 g in dry fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Between the semi-ripe and overripe stages (210–224 days), firmness ranged from 23.63 to 8.78 N along the polar axis and from 23.32 to 6.93 N across the equator, falling as the fruit ripened.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Pigments.** Red colour comes from flavonoid pigments that replace chlorophyll in the skin.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Anthocyanins kept rising after ripeness: from 1.52 µg per g fresh weight in semi-ripe fruit and 8.26 µg/g in ripe fruit to 47.15 µg/g in overripe fruit.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Sugars.** Sugar content rises in both pericarp and seeds during ripening, and sucrose can make up 73% of the total sugars in the seeds.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> But the fruit and the seed behave differently late in ripening. In a 2024 study of six red-colour classes, sucrose nearly doubled from the lightest to the darkest class in the skin-and-pulp and in the juice, while it did not change significantly in the beans.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Total soluble sugars were highest in the outer fruit and lowest in the bean.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Across three mature classes (red-orange, crimson, red-wine), a 2023 study found no differences in organic acids, lipids, chlorogenic acids, proteins, alkaloids or sucrose in the green coffee; only fructose and glucose rose.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The practical point: more sugar in the pulp does not mean more sugar in the seed.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Ripeness and cup quality: measured effects

**Across the whole ripening range.** Green fruit (182–203 days after flowering) and dry fruit (231 days) gave low cup scores; semi-ripe to overripe fruit (210–224 days) gave the best.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Within mature fruit.** Red-orange, crimson and red-wine fruit of var. Castillo scored 82.73, 82.79 and 82.43 SCA points, with no significant difference in any sensory attribute.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Darker fruit had less mucilage (10.07% against 15.44%).<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Risks and defects arising at harvest

Cenicafé links each class of faulty fruit to cup defects:<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

- **Green (unripe) fruit** – fermented, dirty, acrid and green cup defects.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Overripe fruit** – vinegary and fermented defects.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Black fruit** – acrid and carbonous defects.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Delays and fermentation in sacks.** Cherries held in bags or heaps heat up and ferment quickly.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Leaving cherry more than 12 hours or overnight before pulping is one of the practices Cenicafé lists behind stained parchment, vinegary beans, moulds and ochratoxin A in wet parchment coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Mycotoxins.** In a 2026 Colombian study (var. Castillo), ochratoxin A in the green coffee averaged 2.89, 2.98 and 3.03 µg/kg for red-orange, crimson and red-wine fruit processed without delay (the paper's Table 2 gives 3.17 µg/kg for red-wine).<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Holding cherry for 24 or 48 hours at 15 or 20 °C gave a highest treatment mean of 3.35 µg/kg (3.31 µg/kg in the abstract), and neither holding time nor temperature had a significant effect.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The authors conclude that the initial quality of the fruit matters most for bean safety.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Fruit collected from the ground is a separate ochratoxin A risk.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Measurement

- **Colour.** Research classifies ripeness by colour, for example by the red coordinate (a*) of a colorimeter.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **°Brix.** A refractometer on fresh pulp. Values rise through ripening,<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> but pulp sugar does not track bean sugar late in ripening.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Firmness and detachment force.** Measured with a penetrometer or dynamometer in research.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **A °Brix target.** The studies consulted here measured °Brix but published no picking threshold that ties it to cup quality. Numbers circulating in the trade could not be traced to a primary source and are left out.
- **Share of each harvest method by country.** No primary source for these shares was found, so they are left out.
- **Optical sorting of cherry.** No study measuring the effect of colour sorting of cherry on cup quality was consulted for this entry.
- **Unripe fruit in flotation.** Floaters include undeveloped fruit,<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> but how well water separates sound unripe cherries from ripe ones was not measured in the sources here.

Colombia weekly from 182 to 231 days after flowering and followed weight, firmness, soluble solids, pigments and cup quality. The best cups came from semi-ripe to overripe fruit; green and dry fruit gave poor ones.</p>
<p class="meta">Cenicafé 54(3), 208–225 · <a href="https://biblioteca.cenicafe.org/bitstream/10778/265/1/arc054(03)208-225.pdf">Cenicafé repository</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Osorio Pérez et al., 2023 – Chemistry and cup of three mature stages.</strong> Compared red-orange, crimson and red-wine fruit of var. Castillo. Cup scores and most compounds did not differ; only fructose and glucose rose. The authors argue that dark fruit that is still turgid should not be called overripe.</p>
<p class="meta">Agronomy 13(2), 341 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy13020341">doi:10.3390/agronomy13020341</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Baptistella et al., 2024 – Sugars in fruit and bean.</strong> Split cherries into six red-colour classes and measured sugars in the skin-and-pulp, juice and bean separately. Sucrose nearly doubled in the outer fruit, but not in the bean, which suggests the two are regulated separately.</p>
<p class="meta">Plants 13(13), 1853 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/plants13131853">doi:10.3390/plants13131853</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Martínez et al., 2017 – Harvest composition and the cup.</strong> Cupped defined mixes of ripe, overripe, semi-ripe and unripe fruit in Caturra and Colombia. Scores stayed within a narrow band, and a mix with 2% unripe fruit matched pure ripe fruit for var. Colombia.</p>
<p class="meta">Vitae 24(1), 47–58 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.17533/udea.vitae.v24n1a06">doi:10.17533/udea.vitae.v24n1a06</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Osorio et al., 2026 – Ripeness, delays and mycotoxins.</strong> Held cherries of three mature stages for 24 or 48 hours at 15 or 20 °C, and extended fermentation. Holding time and temperature had no significant effect on ochratoxin A, and the authors conclude that fruit quality at the start matters most.</p>
<p class="meta">Frontiers in Plant Science 17, 1734522 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2026.1734522">doi:10.3389/fpls.2026.1734522</a></p>
</div>
