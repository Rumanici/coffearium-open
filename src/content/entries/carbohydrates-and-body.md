## What carbohydrates green coffee contains

Carbohydrates are sugars and the chains built from them. In green coffee they fall into two groups: free sugars, and polysaccharides, long chains of sugar units.

**Polysaccharides.** They make up about half of the dry weight of green coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Most of them form the walls of the seed’s cells. A 2006 review names three main types:<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

- **Galactomannans.** Chains of mannose with single galactose units attached. They account for about half of the polysaccharides.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to a roasting chemistry chapter, they also serve as an energy store for the seed, as starch does in cereals.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Arabinogalactan-proteins.** Galactose chains with branches that end in arabinose, bound to protein.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Cellulose.** The rigid framework of the cell wall.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Free sugars.** In mature seeds, sucrose makes up 5–12% of dry weight and almost all of the free sugar.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Glucose and fructose are present only in traces.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> According to the roasting chemistry chapter, arabica holds more sucrose than robusta.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> How green coffee is composed overall is covered in [Green coffee composition](/entries/green-coffee-composition/).

## What roasting does to them

**Sugar is consumed.** Sucrose is almost entirely broken down in roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It feeds the Maillard reaction and caramelisation, and gives rise to colour, aroma, carbon dioxide and water.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> How fast this happens during a roast is shown in [Browning phase](/entries/browning-phase/).

**Polysaccharides partly break down.** In a 2002 study of three arabica varieties, roasting degraded 12–40% of the polysaccharides, depending on the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Arabinogalactans lost up to 60% after a dark roast, mannans up to 36%. Cellulose was barely affected.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**The arabinose branches go first.** The side chains of the arabinogalactans break off faster than their galactose backbone.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The freed arabinose is a reactive sugar. According to the roasting chemistry chapter, most of it ends up in melanoidins, the brown polymers of roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

**More dissolves.** Roasting cuts the long chains into shorter ones and loosens the cell wall.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Galactomannans, mostly insoluble in green coffee, become largely extractable with hot water as roasting increases.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Cellulose stays insoluble whatever the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## From the bean to the cup

The polysaccharides that dissolve during brewing are mainly galactomannans and arabinogalactans.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Part of them reach the cup bound into melanoidins.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> How much reaches the cup depends on the brew.

**Espresso extracts more per millilitre.** In a 2017 study of four brew methods with one coffee, espresso held about four to six times more oligosaccharides per volume than the other brews.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Oligosaccharides are short sugar chains, here of 3–15 units. Per serving, the small espresso and the larger cups gave comparable amounts.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Temperature matters most.** In a 2019 study that varied time, temperature, ratio and grind, temperature was the main factor separating the extracts.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> How well the coffee extracted depended more on galactomannans than on arabinogalactans.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**The machine matters too.** In a 2022 espresso study, changing the height of the perforated disc under the basket changed how much polysaccharide reached the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

## Carbohydrates, body and crema

**Viscosity and mouthfeel.** According to the roasting chemistry chapter, polysaccharides add little to aroma but give the brew viscosity and mouthfeel.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured the effect of polysaccharides on perceived body directly. Dissolved solids, fines and oil also shape body; they are covered in [Body and mouthfeel](/entries/body-and-mouthfeel/) and [Coffee oil](/entries/coffee-oil/).

**Crema.** In a 1997 espresso study, foam stability was related to the amount of galactomannan and arabinogalactan in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> How much foam formed depended on protein, and rose with roast degree.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> More on crema is in the [espresso](/entries/espresso/) entry.

**Roast and body.** Sources disagree on whether roasting changes body. A roasting textbook chapter describes body as rising with roast degree to a point and then falling.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> In a 2020 analysis of eight roasting studies, the timing of the roast phases did not affect body at all; in the one study that rated body across roast colours, darker roasts scored only slightly higher.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## The chemistry of roasting carbohydrates

**Which go first.** According to the roasting chemistry chapter, sucrose and free amino acids react first, because they are immediately available.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Arabinose branches react later, because they must first be released by depolymerisation or hydrolysis.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Galactans, mannans and cellulose resist breakdown and join the Maillard reaction only late in the roast.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The sequence for sucrose is described in [Maillard reaction](/entries/maillard-reaction/) and [Caramelisation](/entries/caramelisation/).

**Arabinogalactan-proteins.** Arabinose sits at the end of most arabinogalactan side chains. Its furanose ring form makes it labile to hydrolysis.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Side chains are removed faster than the galactan backbone is cut.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The arabinogalactans are depolymerised already after a light roast.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Their covalent link to protein survives roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> According to the review, arabinogalactan populations with very different galactose-to-arabinose ratios can be isolated.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Galactomannans.** Roasting reduced both the chain length and the branching of the galactomannans; the review describes this as a linearisation.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The mannans that remain in the bean keep their molecular weight even after a dark roast.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Roasting also made the cell-wall polymers more accessible to extraction and allowed galactomannans of higher molecular weight to dissolve.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Sequential extraction of arabica at several roast degrees showed galactomannans moving from the insoluble to the extractable fraction.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

**Small fragments.** In the same sequential study, most roasted-coffee carbohydrate was still polymeric. Oligomers and especially monomers appeared to be converted very rapidly into Maillard and pyrolysis products.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Oligosaccharides found in brewed coffee are thought, by the authors who measured them, to derive from galactomannans and arabinogalactans; their structures were inferred, not confirmed.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Acids.** Sucrose is the principal precursor of formic, acetic, glycolic and lactic acid in roasting.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> A model roast of arabinogalactan from larch wood, similar in structure to the coffee polymer, gave the same four acids, but with relatively more acetic acid.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The acids themselves are covered in [Acids in coffee](/entries/acids-in-coffee/).

**Melanoidins.** In a 2008 study of brews from light, medium and dark roasts, arabinogalactans appeared relatively more involved in melanoidin formation than galactomannans.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Galactomannans were continuously incorporated into arabinogalactan-protein melanoidins as roasting went on.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Longer roasting led mainly to melanoidins of high molecular weight.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Their structure is discussed in [Melanoidins](/entries/melanoidins/).

**The cell wall as structure.** Polysaccharides also give the bean its mechanical behaviour in the roaster. According to a roasting chapter, cell walls pass between a glassy and a rubbery state, and the bean swells while they are rubbery.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> This is covered in [Glass–rubber transition](/entries/glass-rubber-transition/).

## Extraction: grind, pressure and temperature

**Brew method.** In a 2005 Brazilian comparison of three brew methods with one arabica coffee, espresso gave the highest yield of soluble solids.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Its polysaccharides were the smallest: 6 kDa, against 11 kDa for paper drip and 21 kDa for the electric machine.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The sugar make-up of the polymers was nearly the same in all three, with mannose about half.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

**Temperature and ratio.** In the 2019 extraction study, temperature was the major factor that set the extracts apart.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Extraction efficiency followed galactomannan extraction more closely than arabinogalactan extraction.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>

**Pressure and viscosity.** According to a brewing chapter, an earlier study found that higher espresso pressure gave a more viscous brew with more intense body.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The chapter does not say which compounds carried the change.

**Bed swelling.** The same chapter notes that particles in an espresso bed swell on wetting, because roasted coffee contains water-insoluble polysaccharides.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Swelling and particle rearrangement consolidate the bed, lower its porosity and slow the flow.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

**Basket set-up.** A 2022 study varied the perforated-disc height and filter basket for arabica and robusta espresso at 12 and 14 g. A higher disc reduced galactomannans and arabinogalactans in the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

**Soluble coffee.** In industrial extraction for soluble coffee, how far the galactomannans dissolve is a critical factor in the yield of powder.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Polysaccharides and foam

**Foam stability.** The 1997 study roasted a Brazilian natural arabica and a Ugandan natural robusta to several degrees and made espresso from each.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Foamability rose with roast degree and depended on the protein in the brew.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Foam stability was related to galactomannan and arabinogalactan, and seemed independent of origin.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> The authors concluded that maximum foamability and maximum foam stability cannot be had in the same espresso.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Foam fractions.** A 2011 review describes two foaming fractions isolated from espresso. One, of about 80% mannan and 20% arabinogalactan with a molecular weight of 34,000–47,000, was involved in foam stability.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> A second fraction, of lower molecular weight and higher surface activity, was linked to foamability.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The review cites work that ascribes the loss of foam persistence at high roast to depolymerisation of polysaccharide-derived Maillard products, which lowers the viscosity of the liquid.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Other actors.** A crema chapter adds that a strong correlation was found between foam stability and high-molecular-weight compounds, suggested to be complexes of polysaccharide, protein and phenolic compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> It names 4-vinylcatechol oligomers, not polysaccharides, as key foam-active compounds in the crema of roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> According to the brewing chapter, water hardness lowers crema stability, possibly through cations interacting with protein–polysaccharide complexes.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

## How strong the link to body is

**What is stated.** A textbook chapter states that polysaccharides give the brew viscosity and mouthfeel, citing the 2002 roasting study.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> A second chapter reports that pressure raised viscosity and body in espresso.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Both are Tier B and come from the same book.

**What was measured.** The studies consulted for this entry measured polysaccharides in beans and brews and their link to foam.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> None of them measured viscosity or rated body against polysaccharide content. A 2020 analysis of eight roasting studies found no effect of roast phase timing on body, against the popular idea that larger molecules from the Maillard reaction thicken the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

**What else counts.** Dissolved solids, fines, oil and melanoidins all bear on body. The evidence for each is set out in [Body and mouthfeel](/entries/body-and-mouthfeel/).

## Measuring coffee carbohydrates

- **Sequential extraction.** Hot water at 90 °C, then at 170 °C, then cold dilute alkali, compared with the green beans of the same batch.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>
- **Ethanol precipitation.** Polymeric carbohydrates in espresso were precipitated in 55% and 75% ethanol fractions.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Monosaccharide analysis.** Polymers are hydrolysed and the sugar units measured by gas chromatography.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>
- **Molecular weight.** Gel filtration on a Sepharose column separates polymers by size.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **Antibodies.** Labelling of arabinogalactan side chains fell in both arabica and robusta even after the lightest roast.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Polysaccharides and perceived body.** No study consulted for this entry related polysaccharide content in the cup to viscosity or rated body. The link rests on textbook statements.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- **Cellulose.** The review notes no published work on the characteristics of coffee cellulose.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Interactions in the roast.** The review lists interactions of polysaccharides with other molecules, such as proteins and melanoidins, during roasting among the points still to be understood.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Oligosaccharide structures.** The origin of brew oligosaccharides from galactomannans and arabinogalactans is inferred, not confirmed.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Roast profile.** The studies consulted here compared roast degrees, not profiles at the same colour. How time and temperature change polysaccharide breakdown at equal colour is not established.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Redgwell et al., 2002 – Polysaccharides through the roast.</strong> Three arabica varieties were roasted to different degrees and their cell-wall polysaccharides analysed. Between 12% and 40% of the polysaccharides were degraded; arabinogalactans lost up to 60% and mannans up to 36% after a dark roast, while cellulose barely changed.</p>
<p class="meta">Carbohydrate Research 337(5), 421–431 · <a href="https://agris.fao.org/search/es/records/65de2eb44c5aef494fdab739">AGRIS record</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Oosterveld et al., 2003 – What roasting makes extractable.</strong> Arabica beans at several roast degrees were extracted step by step with hot water and alkali and compared with the green beans. Galactomannans became largely extractable with roasting, arabinose side chains broke down faster than the galactan backbone, and cellulose stayed insoluble.</p>
<p class="meta">Carbohydrate Polymers 54(2), 183–192 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/S0144-8617(03)00164-4">doi:10.1016/S0144-8617(03)00164-4</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Nunes et al., 1997 – Espresso foam and roast degree.</strong> A natural arabica and a natural robusta were roasted to several degrees and brewed as espresso. Foamability rose with roast and followed protein; foam stability was related to galactomannans and arabinogalactans.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 45(8), 3238–3243 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf970009t">doi:10.1021/jf970009t</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Bekedam et al., 2008 – Polysaccharides in melanoidins.</strong> Brews from green, light, medium and dark roasts were fractionated to follow how melanoidins form. Arabinogalactans were relatively more involved than galactomannans, and galactomannans kept being incorporated as roasting went on.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 56(16), 7138–7145 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/jf800999a">doi:10.1021/jf800999a</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Tian et al., 2017 – Oligosaccharides by brew method.</strong> One coffee at espresso roast (25 Agtron) was brewed by French press, batch brewer, pod machine and espresso, and the short sugar chains in each brew were measured. Espresso held about four to six times more per volume, but servings delivered comparable amounts.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 65, 2784–2792 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.6b04716">doi:10.1021/acs.jafc.6b04716</a></p>
</div>
