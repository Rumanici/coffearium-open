## What packaging has to do

According to the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) freshness handbook, oxygen, temperature and moisture are the three main enemies of roasted coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Light exposure also affects how fast it stales.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The handbook describes primary shelf life, the life in the closed pack, as strongly influenced by the barrier properties of the packaging material.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Roasted coffee also releases gas, mostly carbon dioxide. Sealed in an airtight bag without a valve, it inflates the bag, which can eventually burst.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Bags for freshly roasted coffee are therefore commonly fitted with a one-way valve that lets gas out.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> According to the handbook, the released gas also pushes oxygen out of the bag, which slows oxidation.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Roasters can also remove oxygen by nitrogen flushing, which replaces the air in the bag with nitrogen before sealing.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

There is no single best package. A 2017 coffee science textbook argues that packaging should match the expected time between roasting and drinking.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Coffee drunk within one or two weeks may not need a high barrier, which often has a higher environmental impact.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Coffee that travels through long supply chains to supermarkets needs much better barriers, which help keep it from being wasted.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> What happens inside the pack is covered in the freshness and degassing entries.

## Materials and barriers

Most coffee bags are laminates: several thin layers bonded together, each with its own job.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In a review of multilayer packaging, the inner layer seals the pack. The outer layer resists abrasion, takes the print or adds a barrier.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> An oxygen barrier may come from a metal foil, a metallised layer or a barrier polymer such as ethylene vinyl alcohol (EVOH).<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

A 2014 study compared four packages for freshly roasted whole beans, stored at room temperature for about a year.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Three were laminates with a one-way valve: polyethylene terephthalate (PET), aluminium and polyethylene (PE); paper, PE, thin aluminium and PE; and paper with PE but no aluminium.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The fourth was a paper bag without a valve.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Packaging with an aluminium layer kept the coffee fresher, measured by ratios of aroma compounds.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> According to the handbook, the differences between the paper and plastic packages grew clearer over the first weeks of storage.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Cupping trials comparing paper and foil bags gave mixed results; the freshness entry describes them.

**Recycling.** The same layering that protects coffee makes the pack hard to recycle. According to the review, most multilayer packaging is incinerated or landfilled because of its poor recyclability.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> The review describes all-polyolefin and full-PE laminates designed as recyclable alternatives.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> It cautions that such a switch should only be made if the product is equally well protected.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Compostable films.** The European Commission states that compostable plastics typically decompose in industrial composting facilities and first need to be collected.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It recommends them where reducing, reusing or recycling is not possible.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> Industrial composting needs high temperatures and high humidity, so breakdown in a home compost heap risks being slower.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

## Packaging and wasted coffee

A pack can protect too little or too much; the textbook calls these under- and overpackaging.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Under-packaging can lead to premature product loss; overpackaging to unnecessary, overengineered packaging.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> It also states that, in most cases, roasted and ground coffee has a higher overall environmental impact than its packaging.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

The carbon footprint entry explains how life-cycle assessments (LCAs) work, including functional units and system boundaries, and gives the share of packaging across studies. The studies below compare packaging with the coffee it holds:

- **A 2009 study of a 1 dl (100 mL) cup made at home.** It compared soluble coffee, capsule espresso and drip filter coffee over their whole life cycle.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> For drip filter coffee it assumed that one third of the coffee made is wasted: 13.5 g of ground coffee per cup, including 33% losses.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> Packaging made up 10% of the impacts of soluble coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> For capsule espresso, the packaging was energy-intensive and comparable in size to the use phase.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>
- **A literature review for the Oregon Department of Environmental Quality.** It frames single-serve packaging as a trade-off against coffee made but not drunk, as is common with drip brewing.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> It reports one study in which a capsule system had essentially the same carbon footprint as a drip maker without waste or inefficiencies.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> It suggests that single-serve systems may be preferable where too much coffee is often made.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup>
- **A 2021 study of a 40 mL cup.** It left out the coffee itself and counted packaging, transport, machine use and disposal.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Of 10 g CO₂e (carbon dioxide equivalent) per capsule cup, 5.5 g came from the packaging. Of 18.5 g per pod cup, 9.5 g did.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> A moka pot came to 8 g.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup>
- **A 2025 study of eight formats.** It assessed the use and disposal phases for 720 kg of roasted and ground coffee with the EU Product Environmental Footprint method.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Coffee from multilayer bags scored significantly lower per cup than coffee balls, pads or polypropylene (PP) capsules.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

Taken together, single-serve formats carry several times more packaging per kilogram of coffee than bags.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> Whether that gives them a larger total footprint depends on how much coffee and hot water other methods waste, and on what each study counts.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a>,<a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> No study consulted for this entry measured how much roasted coffee is thrown away because its packaging failed.

## Capsules and pods

EU law treats coffee bags and single-serve units as packaging, because consumers throw them away with the used coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> It separates two kinds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> **Pads** (also called pods) are permeable, such as a sealed paper filter holding ground coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> **Capsules** are non-permeable units made for a machine.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Materials.** In a 2014 study, four capsule systems differed widely.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Two had bodies and lids of PP and EVOH, with the barrier built in. One had a PP body without barrier and a paper lid with an aluminium coating, each capsule wrapped in aluminium. One had an aluminium body and an aluminium lid.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Other studies examined capsules of PE-Al-PET laminate, polypropylene, compostable polymers and paper.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a>,<a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup>

**Freshness.** In storage for up to a year, the all-aluminium capsule best preserved freshness.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The two capsules without an aluminium layer preserved less.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The ground coffee must also be degassed to the right point before sealing. Too little leaves pressure that can deform or burst a capsule; too much costs aroma.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

**Compostable pods.** A 2020 study at a university in the United States compared compostable pods, mainly of polylactic acid (PLA), with pods of polypropylene.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> In an industrial-scale composting facility, the compostable pods degraded completely within 46 days.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Composting them gave the lowest impacts in 7 of 10 categories. Their embodied energy, however, was 6.1% higher than that of the plastic pods.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> The study left out the coffee itself.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup>

**Collection.** The Swiss Federal Office for the Environment (FOEN, German BAFU) states that shops and many municipal collection points take back coffee capsules.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> According to the FOEN, making packaging from recycled aluminium needs 95% less energy than making it from new material.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup> No collection or recycling rates for capsules from a public authority were consulted for this entry. The planned entry on capsules and the environment will cover them in more detail.

## EU packaging rules (PPWR)

This entry describes what the legal texts and the Commission’s guidance say, as of October 2026. It is not legal advice.

The EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR), Regulation (EU) 2025/40, dates from 19 December 2024.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It entered into force on 11 February 2025 and applies from 12 August 2026.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> It replaces the 1994 Packaging Directive, which was generally repealed on 12 August 2026.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> The Commission’s guidance notice C(2026) 3702 is not binding; the binding interpretation of EU law rests with the Court of Justice of the EU.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The rules that touch coffee most directly:

- **Everything recyclable.** All packaging placed on the market must be recyclable.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Detailed design-for-recycling criteria follow from 1 January 2030 at the earliest, and “recycled at scale” from 1 January 2035.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Packaging will be graded A, B or C; below grade C it counts as technically non-recyclable.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> From 1 January 2038, it must reach at least grade B.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>
- **Pads.** Permeable coffee bags and soft single-serve units disposed of with the coffee must be designed for composting by 12 February 2028.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The Commission reads this as covering any material, including paper.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The Commission’s policy page describes it as industrial compostability.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Capsules.** Capsules fall under the general recyclability rules.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Member States may instead require compostable capsules not made of metal, where suitable collection and treatment exist.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Food contact.** From 12 August 2026, food-contact packaging must not exceed limits for per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS).<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- **Less and labelled.** Manufacturers and importers must keep packaging to a minimum by 1 January 2030.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> A harmonised label showing the material, to help consumers sort waste, follows from 12 August 2028 at the earliest.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

The Commission’s policy page, consulted in October 2026, lists no postponement of these dates. A Commission proposal of December 2025 would suspend one obligation for producers selling into other Member States; as of October 2026 it was before the European Parliament and the Council.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Words such as “recyclable” or “compostable” printed on a pack are also environmental claims; the sustainability claims entry covers the rules for them.

## Switzerland

The PPWR covers all packaging placed on the EU market, whatever its material.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> For packaging from outside the EU, the importer is the person established in the EU who places it on the market.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Coffee packed in Switzerland and sold in the EU is therefore covered by the EU rules there.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

Switzerland has its own rules. On 24 June 2026 the Federal Council adopted a new ordinance for all packaging, replacing the beverage-packaging ordinance of 2000.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Its aims include better recyclability, more recycled content and avoiding unnecessary packaging.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> At least 55% of plastic packaging and 70% of beverage cartons must be recycled as material.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> Producers and retailers of single-use plastic packaging must offer customers separate collection.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The ordinance applies from the start of 2027.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The Federal Council’s announcement does not mention coffee capsules.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

## Barrier needs in detail

Packaging can be specified from the coffee itself. The textbook describes how the rate at which roasted and ground coffee takes up oxygen lets engineers calculate the barrier needed for a target shelf life.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Two studies it reports found oxygen consumption rates of the same order.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The textbook expects specialty coffee, judged largely on freshness, to tolerate less oxygen uptake than mainstream coffee, so its barrier needs are even more demanding.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Headspace and secondary packs matter too. In the capsule comparison, the capsule wrapped in a secondary aluminium pack had a larger headspace and so more residual oxygen after packing.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> It took an intermediate position for freshness.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

Paper bags with a plastic liner are not oxygen barriers. The SCA handbook describes the Kraft bags used in its cupping trials as having a polypropylene liner that keeps coffee oils from wicking onto the paper but is not an effective barrier to oxygen.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The foil bags in the same trials were a three-ply polyester and aluminium lamination, giving an oxygen and moisture barrier.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## The PPWR provisions in detail

**What counts as packaging.** Article 3(1), point (1)(f) covers a permeable tea, coffee or other beverage bag, or a soft single-serve unit, used and disposed of together with the product.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Point (1)(g) covers a non-permeable single-serve unit for use in a machine, also disposed of with the product.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The recitals explain this by how consumers dispose of tea and coffee bags and single-serve units.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Recyclability (Article 6).** Article 6(1) requires all packaging to be recyclable and, in the Commission’s reading, applies from 12 August 2026.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Until the design-for-recycling criteria apply, manufacturers follow the requirements and standards of the old directive, such as EN 13430:2004.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Article 6(2)(a), design for recycling, applies from 1 January 2030 or 24 months after the delegated act enters into force, whichever is later.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Article 6(2)(b), on collection, sorting and recycling at scale, applies from 1 January 2035; for recycling at scale, it applies five years after the implementing act if that is later.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The recitals set out the grades: A, B or C from 2030, assessed on both criteria from 2035, and at least grade B from 1 January 2038.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

**Compostability (Article 9).** The Commission reads Article 9(1) as material-neutral: permeable coffee bags and soft single-serve units of any material must be designed for composting by 12 February 2028.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Under Article 9(2), by way of derogation from Article 6(1), Member States may require that capsules of material other than metal be compostable, if collection and treatment exist.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Home composting is to be considered only under specific local conditions.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> The recitals state that all other packaging should go into material recycling without harming the recyclability of other waste streams.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

**Other articles.** Article 5(5) sets PFAS limits for food-contact packaging from 12 August 2026: 25 ppb for any targeted PFAS, 250 ppb for their sum and 50 ppm total fluorine.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Packaging placed on the market before that date may stay on the market.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Article 10(1) requires minimisation by 1 January 2030.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Article 12(1) requires a pictogram label on material composition from 12 August 2028, or 24 months after the implementing acts.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Recycled-content targets for plastic packaging (Article 7) exempt, among others, plastic food-contact packaging where recycled content would threaten health.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>

## Open questions

- How “recycled at scale” will be measured for capsules depends on implementing acts that, as of October 2026, have not been adopted.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- As of the Commission’s 2026 guidance, a harmonised EU standard for home composting was yet to be developed; the Commission was to request one from the European standardisation bodies.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup>
- Whether recyclable mono-material films protect coffee as well as aluminium laminates over long supply chains: the review cautions that a switch must not reduce protection<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>, and the storage trials consulted here tested laminates and paper, not mono-material films.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>
- How much roasted coffee is lost to staling in the supply chain and at home, which decides how much a better barrier is worth, is not established by the sources consulted here.
