## How hot people drink coffee

Coffee is brewed much hotter than people like to drink it.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) specifies water at 93 ± 3 °C for cupping.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> Consumer studies place the preferred drinking temperature of coffee well below that.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Researchers have measured preference in several ways. In one approach, consumers mixed a hot and a cooler coffee until it reached the temperature they wanted. About 300 people did this in a first experiment; across the experiments, the mean was around 60 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> Black-coffee drinkers chose slightly higher temperatures than people who added creamer.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a></sup> In a 2018 pilot study, 87 participants drank instant coffee and warmed it step by step until it felt too hot. Their preferred temperature averaged 63 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

A 2022 study asked 118 consumers whether each coffee was too cold, too hot or “just about right”. Each person tasted 27 black coffees between 56 and 71 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Acceptance was highest at 58–66 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Only above 70 °C did most consumers find the coffee too hot.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Even at 68 °C, a few percent still found it somewhat too cold.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

Studies of black coffee report preferred or optimal temperatures from 57.8 to 71.4 °C, according to a 2019 review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Part of the spread comes from method: ranking set temperatures, mixing, rating on scales or calculating an optimum.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> People also differ. In the 2018 pilot study, 72% of participants preferred their coffee below 65 °C and 22% above it.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## How temperature changes flavour

Temperature changes what a taster perceives, not only how the cup feels.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> In a study summarised in a 2020 review, a trained panel tasted four coffees at 50, 60 and 70 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The review sums up its result: consumption temperature was critical to the flavour profile of every sample.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Coffee identity and fidelity grew stronger as the temperature rose.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup>

Studies of single attributes point in different directions.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

- **Aroma.** More aroma rises above a hotter cup, because aroma compounds leave the liquid more readily when it is hot.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> In a 2018 thesis study, the hottest samples had the strongest aroma.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Roasted notes.** In a 2018 consumer study, 63% chose “roasted flavour” for coffee at 65 °C. Only 33–37% chose it at 25 or 5 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>
- **Bitterness.** In the same study, 67% chose “bitter” at 65 °C and 84% at 25 or 5 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> The thesis study found a different pattern: bitterness was lowest in its lukewarm sample.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Acidity.** A textbook chapter states that drinkers often report more fruity and acid flavours as coffee cools.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The thesis study found that sourness rose as samples cooled.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> In the 2022 consumer study, by contrast, temperature had little effect on how adequate the acidity seemed.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Sweetness.** Physiology research reports that warming enhances perceived sweetness and bitterness.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> In the 2018 consumer study, sweetness was not among the attributes that changed with temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

According to the textbook chapter, tasting a cooling coffee is a changing experience: tasters also get used to some flavours, so others seem to appear.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> The 2018 thesis concluded that subtle attributes may be easier to identify in coffee served cooler.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

## Temperature in cupping

Cupping uses the cooling of the cup on purpose. The SCA standard specifies that the brew cools to about 70 °C before tasting, or “liquoring”, begins.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> At least three rounds of tasting follow as the coffee cools below body temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s12">12</a></sup> If a cupper’s impression changes as the coffee cools, the affective form allows a second rating. An arrow marks the direction of change.<sup><a href="#src-s14">14</a></sup>

The SCA’s descriptive standard defines mouthfeel as the tactile feel of the brew, excluding the temperature perception.<sup><a href="#src-s13">13</a></sup> Heat is therefore not part of mouthfeel in the CVA. The earlier SCAA protocol also ordered its attributes by temperature, according to a textbook chapter. The sequence followed how flavour perception changes as coffee cools.<sup><a href="#src-s11">11</a></sup> How cupping works step by step is covered in *Cupping*.

## How preference is measured

The methods give different answers because they ask different questions.

- **Fixed temperatures.** Judges rate or rank coffees served at set temperatures. One study rated seven temperatures between 57.2 and 90.6 °C on a just-about-right scale; another ranked six between 39.2 and 82.1 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The result can only be one of the temperatures offered.
- **Mixing.** Consumers mix hot and cold coffee until they reach the temperature they want.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The 2018 pilot study used a variant: participants started at 60 °C and added hot coffee from a 0.04 L beaker, each addition raising the temperature by 2–3 °C, until it felt too hot.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>
- **Just-about-right scales.** The 2022 study did not set temperatures. They varied naturally with brewing and cooling, and each tasting was recorded with a just-about-right rating and a 9-point liking score.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Judging the temperature too hot or too cold cost only about 0.4 points of liking; flavour intensity and acidity that missed the mark cost 1.2–1.9 points.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>
- **Calculation.** The 2008 optimum of 57.8 °C combines preference data with a model of burn risk.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Who is asked and what they drink also matters. The 2018 pilot study used instant coffee in insulated foam cups, with 87 participants.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The 2022 study served about 30 mL of one washed Honduran coffee in 120 mL paper cups; it was funded by the SCA, with underwriting by an appliance maker.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Two studies from one research group found that preferred temperatures lay above the thermal pain and thermal damage thresholds.<sup><a href="#src-s2">2</a>,<a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup> Their judges ranked similar temperatures as most likely to be served in a coffee shop, and customers observed in coffee shops began sipping at similarly high temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s3">3</a></sup>

## Heat on the tongue

Coffee in the cup is hotter than the surface it touches. The 2018 pilot study estimates by formula that coffee at 70 °C gives a contact temperature of about 55–57 °C; the paper gives both values.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> It cites measurements showing that coffee at 60 °C may raise the tongue surface to 53 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> The heat-sensitive TRPV1 receptors open above 43 °C, and a painful heat sensation begins above about 44–45 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> On average, its participants found coffee too hot at 66–67 °C, about 3 °C above the temperature they preferred.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

Temperature also acts on taste cells directly. Receptors for sweet, bitter and umami signal through the ion channel TRPM5, which is itself activated by heat.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> This is offered as a molecular explanation for why warming enhances perceived sweetness and bitterness.<sup><a href="#src-s10">10</a></sup> Only the abstract of that study was read for this entry, so its temperature range is not given here.

## Flavour across temperatures: the studies

**Descriptive panels.** In the study summarised in a 2020 review, seven trained panelists rated 36 attributes in two arabicas, one robusta and a blend at 50, 60 and 70 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> Temperature interacted with the coffee: attributes such as fullness, fidelity and balance changed differently from sample to sample.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> The review therefore advises considering the coffee and the temperature together when developing hot coffee flavour profiles.<sup><a href="#src-s7">7</a></sup> A 2018 thesis with a trained panel found that serving temperature had a larger effect on perception than the choice of coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> Aroma, roasted and cocoa notes were strongest in the hottest samples; stale and tobacco notes, sourness and astringency were stronger in the coldest.<sup><a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>

**Consumers.** In a 2018 study, 79 black-coffee drinkers described brewed coffee served at 65, 25 and 5 °C with check-all-that-apply lists. Six attributes differed with temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Roasted aroma and roasted flavour were chosen more often for hot coffee; bitter taste, metallic and skunky flavours more often for cold coffee.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup> Pungent aroma was the sixth attribute. The total number of sensory terms chosen did not change with temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a></sup>

**Why aroma changes.** Volatile compounds dissolve better in cooler liquid, so a hot brew releases more of them into the air above the cup.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup> According to the textbook chapter, the coffee may also keep changing chemically after serving.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

The panel and consumer studies served each sample at a set temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup> A cupper tastes one coffee as it cools, and according to the textbook chapter, habituation then adds to the effect of temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s9">9</a></sup>

## How fast a cup cools

In tabulated cooling data, a cup loses heat quickly at first, then more slowly.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> A 2019 review states that hot beverages typically lose 10–15 °C in 5 min in a room, and that a lid roughly doubles the required cooling time.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Pouring coffee or tea into a cup takes off 2–5 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> In the 2022 consumer study, the coffee lost about 8 °C between brewing and the insulated carafe, and about 12 °C more on pouring small samples into room-temperature paper cups.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

A 2017 review on scald injuries tabulates the cooling of uncovered beverages in a room at about 20 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> A 237 mL drink served at 95 °C reached 65.5 °C after 10 min; a 473 mL drink was still at 73.9 °C after 10 min.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The review does not separate measured from calculated values in these tables.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> In a 2010 study, black coffee and black tea made in 230 mL ceramic mugs stayed above 65 °C after 10 min of cooling.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Milky drinks started cooler, at 75–77 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

**Holding temperatures.** No source consulted for this entry measured the temperatures at which cafés hold or serve coffee. A 2019 review estimates that coffee brewed at 93 °C (source: 200 °F) is about 90 °C in the carafe and 85–88 °C in the cup at service.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> A 2017 review notes that beverage service temperatures are often above 82 °C (source: 180 °F).<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The 2022 study advises retail coffee operations to check their actual post-pour temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup>

## Scald risk

Hot liquids are among the most common causes of burns.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The 2019 review states that brewing temperatures are high enough to pose an immediate and serious risk of scald injuries.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> It describes the rate of injury as rising very rapidly with each degree of liquid temperature.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> Spills at 82 °C (source: 180 °F) are very likely to cause at least mid-dermal burns, according to the same review.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup>

Children are at particular risk, according to both the 2017 review and a 2010 study.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a>,<a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup> Their skin is about 70% as thick as an adult’s, according to the 2017 review. It calls for lower temperatures or longer cooling for people with limited mobility, such as children or the elderly.<sup><a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup> The 2010 study showed the potential for a full-thickness burn despite 10 min of cooling or the addition of cold milk.<sup><a href="#src-s18">18</a></sup>

Both reviews argue for serving below brewing temperature. The 2019 review proposes a service range of 130–160 °F (≈54–71 °C), on the grounds that preferred drinking temperatures lie significantly below brewing temperatures.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a></sup> No authority consulted for this entry sets a maximum serving temperature for coffee.

## Very hot beverages and the IARC

In 2016 a working group of the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), the cancer agency of the World Health Organization, evaluated drinking coffee, maté and very hot beverages; the IARC announced the results on 15 June 2016.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> Its conclusions, as published:

- **Very hot beverages:** “drinking very hot beverages (> 65 °C) is probably carcinogenic to humans (Group 2A)”.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup> “Very hot” refers to any beverage consumed at a temperature above 65 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>
- **Basis:** limited evidence from epidemiological studies of a causal association with cancer of the oesophagus in humans; the working group judged a causal relationship biologically plausible.<sup><a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>
- **Coffee:** drinking coffee was “not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans (Group 3)”.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a>,<a href="#src-s16">16</a></sup>

The classification concerns temperature, not a particular drink. In the press release, the IARC director said that “it is the temperature, rather than the drinks themselves, that appears to be responsible”.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> The studies named in the press release come from places such as China, Iran, Turkey and South America, where tea or maté is traditionally drunk very hot, at about 70 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup>

The 65 °C line falls inside the range of preferred drinking temperatures. In the 2018 pilot study, 22% of participants preferred their coffee above 65 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup> In the 2022 study, acceptance was highest at 58–66 °C.<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> The authors of the 2018 study note a lack of research on which temperatures consumers perceive as “very hot” or “too hot”, and propose their method as a basis for cancer risk assessment.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a></sup>

## Open questions

- **Café temperatures.** No source consulted for this entry reports measured serving or holding temperatures in cafés; the reviews give estimates and ranges.<sup><a href="#src-s1">1</a>,<a href="#src-s17">17</a></sup>
- **Cup and sip.** The 65 °C threshold refers to the beverage as consumed.<sup><a href="#src-s15">15</a></sup> How sip size, cup material and lids change the temperature that reaches the mouth and oesophagus is not established in the sources consulted here.
- **Bitterness and acidity.** Studies disagree on how bitterness and acidity change as coffee cools (see the contested points below).<sup><a href="#src-s5">5</a>,<a href="#src-s6">6</a>,<a href="#src-s8">8</a></sup>
- **Specialty coffees.** The two most recent preference studies each used a single coffee: instant coffee in one, a medium-roast washed coffee in the other.<sup><a href="#src-s4">4</a>,<a href="#src-s5">5</a></sup> Whether lighter roasts or different origins shift the preferred temperature has not been measured in the sources consulted here.

## Key studies

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Lee & O’Mahony, 2002 – Mixing to a preferred temperature.</strong> About 300 consumers mixed hot and cooler coffee, with creamer and sweetener to taste, until it was as hot as they liked; a second experiment varied the strength of black coffee. The mean preferred temperature was around 60 °C in all experiments, with black-coffee drinkers choosing slightly higher temperatures.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 67(7), 2774–2777 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2002.tb08814.x">doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2002.tb08814.x</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Dirler et al., 2018 – Preferred temperature and the pain threshold.</strong> Eighty-seven participants warmed instant coffee step by step until it felt too hot. The mean preferred temperature was 63 °C and the mean “too hot” temperature 66–67 °C (the abstract and the results differ); 22% preferred their coffee above the IARC line of 65 °C.</p>
<p class="meta">Foods 7(6), 83 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/foods7060083">doi:10.3390/foods7060083</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Pramudya & Seo, 2018 – Hot, ambient and cold coffee.</strong> Seventy-nine black-coffee drinkers described brewed coffee at 65, 25 and 5 °C with check-all-that-apply lists. Six attributes changed with temperature: roasted aroma and flavour were chosen more often for hot coffee, bitterness and metallic notes for cold coffee.</p>
<p class="meta">Frontiers in Psychology 8, 2264 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02264">doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02264</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Ristenpart et al., 2022 – Consumer acceptance by tasting temperature.</strong> In 3,186 tastings, 118 consumers rated black coffees between 56 and 71 °C on just-about-right and liking scales. Acceptance peaked at 58–66 °C; only above 70 °C did most find the coffee too hot, and some still found it somewhat too cold at 68 °C.</p>
<p class="meta">Scientific Reports 12, 20621 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-23904-4">doi:10.1038/s41598-022-23904-4</a></p>
</div>

<div class="study">
<p><strong>Abraham & Diller, 2019 – Hot beverage temperatures.</strong> A review of consumer preference studies and burn research. It sets the preferred temperatures of six studies against brewing temperatures and argues that serving temperatures should be clearly separated from brewing temperatures.</p>
<p class="meta">Journal of Food Science 84(8), 2011–2014 · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/1750-3841.14699">doi:10.1111/1750-3841.14699</a></p>
</div>
